Input latency: how late is too late?

Why the delay between pressing a button and seeing the result decides whether controls feel crisp or mushy, and where that delay hides in your game.

You press jump and the character jumps. Nothing is broken, yet the game feels like steering a boat. Players call it "floaty" or "unresponsive" and cannot say why. Very often the cause is a few dozen milliseconds between the press and the first pixel that moves.

Latency is the gap between intent and feedback

Input latency is the time from the moment a player presses a button to the moment the screen first shows a result. Players never see this number. With low latency the character feels like part of their hand; with high latency it feels like it is following orders.

Latency is not one delay. It is a chain of small waits, and every link adds to the total.

Frames are the unit of delay

Most games read input once per frame, at the start of the frame. A press that arrives just after that moment waits almost a whole frame to be noticed. At 60 frames per second a frame is 16.7 milliseconds, so every extra frame anywhere in the chain costs 16.7 ms. At 30 frames per second it costs 33.3 ms. This is one reason low frame rates feel sluggish even when the motion looks acceptable.

The display adds its own share. Many TVs process the picture before showing it, which is why many offer a "game mode" that skips some of that work. You cannot fix the player's TV, but you can avoid adding to the total.

How much do players notice?

There is no single threshold. It depends on the person, the action and what the eye is watching. Two research results give a sense of scale:

  • Mark and Kajal Claypool (2006) sorted game actions by how precise they are and how quickly they must happen. Fast, precise actions such as aiming in a first-person shooter suffered at around 100 ms of added delay, while commanding units in a strategy game tolerated around a second.
  • Kjetil Raaen and Ragnhild Eg (2015) asked people to judge when a click changed the spin of a disc. When participants tuned the delay down themselves, the median result was about 40 ms, far lower than the delay they could reliably tell apart in a yes-or-no test. In other words, people can sense small delays when they are paying attention to them.

These come from specific experiments. Treat them as a scale, not a target.

Starting points to tune

For a platformer or action game, aim for the first visible response on the same frame the input is read, or the next one: under about 2 frames (33 ms) of delay added by your own code. For menus and turn-based choices, 100 ms of extra delay tends to go unnoticed. Measure your own game before you trust any of these numbers.

The delay you add yourself

The most fixable delay is often a design decision. A four-frame crouch before the feet leave the ground adds 67 ms at 60 frames per second. Waiting for the current animation to end before starting the next can add far more. The fix is not always to delete the wind-up. Often it is enough to show something on the first frame, such as a squash, a sound or a dust puff, so the player knows the press was heard.

The code

This is the demo's jump in LÖVE. Each press is stamped with the time it happened and queued, and the jump only fires once its delay has passed. In a real game addedDelay is zero; the queue is here to let you feel the difference and to measure what your code adds. Height y is measured upward from the ground.

local GRAVITY = 3000       -- pixels per second squared
local JUMP_SPEED = 750     -- pixels per second at take-off
local GROUND = 300         -- screen y of the ground line
local addedDelay = 0.100   -- seconds; the demo's slider

local clock = 0
local pending = {}         -- presses waiting for their delay
local player = { y = 0, vy = 0, grounded = true }
local lastLatency = nil

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "space" or key == "w" or key == "up" then
    table.insert(pending, { pressed = clock, fireAt = clock + addedDelay })
  end
end

function love.update(dt)
  clock = clock + dt
  local i = 1
  while i <= #pending do
    local p = pending[i]
    if clock >= p.fireAt then
      table.remove(pending, i)
      if player.grounded then          -- in the air: the press is lost
        player.vy = JUMP_SPEED
        player.grounded = false
        lastLatency = clock - p.pressed
      end
    else
      i = i + 1
    end
  end

  if not player.grounded then
    player.vy = player.vy - GRAVITY * dt
    player.y = player.y + player.vy * dt
    if player.y <= 0 then
      player.y = 0
      player.vy = 0
      player.grounded = true
    end
  end
end

function love.draw()
  love.graphics.rectangle("fill", 100, GROUND - player.y - 24, 24, 24)
  if lastLatency then
    local ms = math.floor(lastLatency * 1000 + 0.5)
    love.graphics.print("last jump fired " .. ms .. " ms after the press", 10, 10)
  end
end

With addedDelay at 0 the jump fires on the next update after the press. That one-frame wait is the floor when input is read once per frame.

Pitfalls

  • Testing only on your own machine. A fast monitor hides delay that a player on a TV at 30 frames per second will feel.
  • Wind-up before any feedback. Anticipation frames look good in an animation tool and feel bad in the hand. If the action must wait, respond visibly or audibly on the first frame anyway.
  • Reading input after the update. If your loop moves the character and only then reads the controller, every press waits an extra frame. Read input first, then update, then draw.
  • Extra frames in the rendering setup. Vertical sync and extra frame buffering smooth the picture but can each add a frame or more. Let the player choose.
  • Removing delay that was a design choice. Latency is not always a bug. A heavy knight or a fighting-game special move can wait on purpose, because commitment is part of the challenge. Keep such delay deliberate and consistent, and still acknowledge the press instantly.
  • Inconsistent delay. A steady delay can be learned. One that wanders between 20 ms and 80 ms because of hitches cannot, and tends to feel worse than a larger steady one.

In real games

Fighting games commonly publish "frame data" for every move, listing its start-up frames: the delay before the attack can hit. Here the delay is designed and documented, and players plan around it.

Rhythm games usually include a calibration screen where the player taps along to a beat, so the game can measure and subtract the delay of that particular screen and audio setup.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Pick the most common action in your game, such as jump, attack or shoot. Film the screen and your hand at 60 frames per second with a phone. Step through the video frame by frame and count the frames from the press to the first frame where anything on screen changes.

If the count is well above what an empty test scene gives you, find where your code waits: a wind-up animation, an input read after the update, or a state machine that finishes one state first. You are done when the first visible response arrives within one or two frames of the press.

Takeaways

  • Input latency is the time from a press to the first visible result, and players feel it as control, not as a number.
  • It is a chain of waits. Each frame in the chain costs 16.7 ms at 60 frames per second.
  • The delay your own code adds, such as wind-ups and waiting for animations, is usually the easiest to cut.
  • When an action must wait by design, give instant feedback anyway and keep the delay consistent.
  • Thresholds from research are a scale, not a rule. Measure your own game.
