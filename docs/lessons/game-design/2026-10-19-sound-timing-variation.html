Sound timing, variation and layers

How the timing, pitch variation and layering of a sound effect change how the same hit feels, with starting numbers to tune.

The hammer lands, the sparks fly, and the clang arrives a moment later, as if the hit happened somewhere else. Or the timing is right, but every hit plays the exact same recording, and by the twentieth it sounds like a typewriter. The picture is identical in both games. The sound decides whether the hit feels solid.

Three things decide how a hit sounds

Three choices around a sound effect change the feel of the action: when it starts, how much it changes between plays, and what it is made of.

1. Timing: sound and picture must fuse

When a flash of sparks and a clang arrive close enough together, the brain merges them into one event. This is often called audio-visual sync. Pull them apart and the player hears two events, and the hit feels soft or laggy.

The tolerance is not the same in both directions. In the real world, light arrives before sound: sound travels about 343 metres per second, so it falls behind by roughly 3 milliseconds for every metre of distance. Slightly late sound is familiar. Sound before the picture never happens in nature, and people tend to notice it sooner.

Broadcast engineers measured this for television. The ITU recommendation BT.1359 gives detection thresholds of roughly 45 milliseconds for sound that leads the picture and 125 milliseconds for sound that lags it. Those numbers came from tests with people speaking on screen, not from games. A sharp impact that the player caused with their own button press is a much harsher test, so treat them as an outer limit, not a target.

Delay usually sneaks in from a few places: the sound is triggered at the start or the end of an animation instead of the contact frame; a long file is streamed from disk instead of loaded into memory; the audio buffer is large; or the player wears wireless headphones, which can add far more delay than your code ever does. You control the first three.

2. Variation: no two hits identical

Hearing the exact same recording over and over is so common a problem that game audio people have a name for it: the machine-gun effect. The ear spots the repetition, and the sound stops being a hit and becomes a loop.

The cheapest fix is to change the pitch, how high or low the sound is, by a small random amount each time. Pitch is usually measured in semitones, the steps between neighbouring piano keys. A playback rate of 2 ^ (s / 12) shifts the sound by s semitones, and one semitone is about 6 percent. A random shift within one semitone either way is a common place to start. Push past about three and the hits stop sounding like the same object.

Other kinds of variation stack well with pitch: a small random volume change, two to five recorded versions of the sound, and a rule that never plays the same version twice in a row.

3. Layering: build one hit from parts

Layering means playing several short sounds at once so that each one does one job. A common split for an impact:

  • Click: a very short, bright burst. It marks the exact moment of contact.
  • Thump: a low tone that drops in pitch. It carries weight.
  • Rattle: a longer noisy tail. It says what the material is and how big the space is.

Shift every layer by the same random pitch: the hit stays one sound but never repeats exactly. Layers shifted separately drift apart and stop fusing.

Starting points to tune

Start the sound on the impact frame. Random pitch within one semitone either way. Two or three layers, all starting together. These are numbers to begin from, not rules: a cartoon game may want wider pitch swings, a realistic one narrower.

Try it

Turn your volume down a little first; nothing plays until you press. Hit the block a few times with the default settings. Then drag the sound offset to +150 milliseconds and hit again: the hammer seems to land on something soft. Try −80 to hear the sound arrive first. Back at 0, switch pitch variation off and press the rapid button to hear the machine-gun effect, then switch it back on. Last, compare layers on and off.

Many people find the hit stops feeling instant somewhere around +60 to +100 milliseconds, well inside the TV figure. The sounds are generated in the browser as stand-ins for recorded files.

The code

This is the demo's logic in LÖVE, with the same names and default numbers. A press starts the swing; the impact and the sound each get their own countdown, so the sound can start before, on or after the impact. The three layers are recorded files here.

local SWING = 0.08         -- seconds from press to impact
local SOUND_OFFSET = 0     -- seconds; positive = sound after the impact
local PITCH_RANGE = 1      -- semitones either way; 0 = no variation
local LAYERED = true

local layers = {
  click  = love.audio.newSource("click.wav", "static"),
  thump  = love.audio.newSource("thump.wav", "static"),
  rattle = love.audio.newSource("rattle.wav", "static"),
}
local pending = {}         -- countdowns for impacts and sounds

local function playHit()
  local semitones = (love.math.random() * 2 - 1) * PITCH_RANGE
  local pitch = 2 ^ (semitones / 12)    -- one pitch for every layer
  local names = LAYERED and { "click", "thump", "rattle" } or { "thump" }
  for i = 1, #names do
    local s = layers[names[i]]:clone()  -- lets hits overlap
    s:setPitch(pitch)
    s:play()
  end
end

local function press()
  table.insert(pending, {
    impact = SWING,
    sound = math.max(0, SWING + SOUND_OFFSET),
  })
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "space" or key == "j" then press() end
end

function love.update(dt)
  for i = #pending, 1, -1 do
    local p = pending[i]
    if p.impact then
      p.impact = p.impact - dt
      if p.impact <= 0 then
        p.impact = nil
        spawnSparks()      -- your own impact effect
      end
    end
    if p.sound then
      p.sound = p.sound - dt
      if p.sound <= 0 then
        p.sound = nil
        playHit()
      end
    end
    if not p.impact and not p.sound then table.remove(pending, i) end
  end
end

Load hit sounds as "static" sources, which are decoded into memory once. A "stream" source decodes as it plays and is meant for music.

Pitfalls

  • Triggering on the wrong frame. A sound fired when the attack button is pressed plays before the contact; one fired when the animation ends plays after it. Fire it from the same line of code that applies the hit.
  • Too much pitch range. Past a few semitones, hits sound like different objects, or like a tune. If players start hearing melodies in your footsteps, narrow the range.
  • Unlimited overlapping copies. Cloning a source for every hit is fine for a few, but twenty explosions in one frame stack into a loud blast and a lot of garbage. Keep a small pool of sources per sound and cap how many play at once.
  • Random pitch on sounds that carry meaning. A low-health warning or a voice line must stay recognisable. Vary the noisy, frequent sounds; keep the informative ones fixed.

When not to use these

Rhythm and music games, where every sound must land on the beat and in key: random pitch is wrong there, and so is any delay. And sometimes a delay is right: a distant explosion that rumbles in a moment after the flash sells the distance, exactly as it would in real life. The rule is about the player's own actions up close.

In real games

Random pitch and volume are common enough that engines and audio tools build them in. Godot 4 has an AudioStreamRandomizer resource with a random pitch setting, a random volume offset, and a playback mode that tries not to play the same sound twice in a row. Audio middleware such as FMOD and Wwise offers similar random containers and pitch randomisation, so sound designers can set up variation without code.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Pick the most frequent sound in your game: a footstep, a shot or a hit. First check its timing: find where it is played and move the call to the line that applies the effect. Then add random pitch within one semitone either way.

Then add a second layer, a short click or low thump you are licensed to use, played at the same moment with the same pitch. You are done when twenty fast repeats of the action no longer sound like a loop, and the sound feels attached to the moment of contact.

Takeaways

  • Start an impact sound on the frame of the impact. Late sound makes hits feel soft; early sound feels wrong even sooner.
  • Repeating the same recording causes the machine-gun effect. A random pitch within about one semitone is a cheap first fix.
  • Layer a hit from parts that do different jobs: a click for timing, a thump for weight, a tail for material.
  • Shift all layers by the same pitch so they stay one sound.
  • Keep rhythm-game sounds and meaningful cues fixed, and keep delays for things that are far away.
