Positive feedback loops: when winning makes winning easier

How rewards that make the winner stronger cause snowballing, how to measure when a match is really decided, and how to brake the loop.

In a strategy match, one player wins the first skirmish by a little luck. The spoils buy more units, which win the next fight, which pays for more units. Twenty minutes later the match is still going, but both players have known the winner since minute five.

A loop that feeds the leader

A feedback loop is a cycle where the result of something changes the next attempt at it.

A positive feedback loop, also called a reinforcing loop, is one where success makes the next success more likely. "Positive" does not mean good. It means the loop pushes in the same direction it is already going, so small differences grow. Players usually call this snowballing. The opposite, a negative feedback loop, pushes back toward even, and is the next lesson.

A model small enough to reason about

Take two teams, A and B, each with 10 power. They fight 31 rounds. In each round, team A's chance to win is its share of the total power: a / (a + b). The winner of a round gains extra power, which is the knob this lesson is about.

With a gain of 0, every round is a coin flip forever. With a gain of 5, one win makes it 15 against 10, so the winner's next chance is 60 percent. Two wins make it 67 percent, three make it 71 percent. Nobody played better. The dice simply remember.

Here is what happens over 2000 simulated matches at each gain. A comeback here means the team behind after round 15 wins the match. "Settled by" is the round in which the eventual winner took the lead for the last time.

What the player feels

Without feedback, comebacks are common and endings are often tense, but it can feel flat: a great early play buys you nothing later.

With a mild loop, an early win feels earned and carries forward as momentum. With a strong loop, the leader feels powerful and the trailing player feels helpless, often well before the game admits it. In a multiplayer game that loser is a real person deciding whether to queue again.

Why designers use it anyway

Positive feedback ends games. Designer Marc LeBlanc, in a 1999 Game Developers Conference talk on feedback systems, described it as driving a game to a conclusion and magnifying early successes. A game with only negative feedback can stall. It also rewards play: if good early decisions are worth nothing later, players stop caring about them. So the question is rarely "positive feedback or not", but "how strong, and for how long".

Starting points to tune

Measure how much one win changes the next win chance. A shift of a few percentage points tends to read as momentum. A shift of 10 points or more per win, as with a gain of 5 in the model, usually means the match is decided in its first quarter. Then add one brake and measure again. These are starting points, not rules: a short match can stand a stronger loop than a long one, because the losing player is not kept waiting.

Three brakes

  • Cap the advantage. Limit how far the win chance, damage or income can go, however big the lead.
  • Make bonuses wear off. Power from a win fades over time, so the leader has to keep winning to stay ahead. The demo has this one: each round, 20 percent of every bonus disappears.
  • Make the lead cost something. Upkeep on a large army, or a bounty on the leader, turns part of the advantage into a target.

A fourth option is to let a decided game end sooner, with a surrender vote or a mercy rule.

The code

This is the demo's model in LÖVE, with the same numbers. Press Space to fight a round. The marked lines are the whole positive feedback loop; the WEAR_OFF block is the brake.

local BASE = 10          -- starting power of each team
local GAIN = 5           -- power the winner gains per round
local WEAR_OFF = false   -- the brake: bonuses fade each round
local FADE = 0.8         -- share of each bonus kept per round
local ROUNDS = 31

local a, b = BASE, BASE  -- power of team A and team B
local lead, round = 0, 0 -- lead is A's wins minus B's wins

local function fight()
  local chanceA = a / (a + b)
  if love.math.random() < chanceA then
    a = a + GAIN         -- winning buys power...
    lead = lead + 1
  else
    b = b + GAIN         -- ...which raises the next chance
    lead = lead - 1
  end
  if WEAR_OFF then
    a = BASE + (a - BASE) * FADE
    b = BASE + (b - BASE) * FADE
  end
  round = round + 1
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "space" and round < ROUNDS then fight() end
end

function love.draw()
  local chanceA = a / (a + b)
  love.graphics.print("Round " .. round .. " of " .. ROUNDS, 20, 20)
  love.graphics.print("Lead: " .. lead, 20, 40)
  love.graphics.print("A win chance: " .. math.floor(chanceA * 100 + 0.5) .. "%", 20, 60)
end

In a real game the "power" is rarely one number: it is gold, units, map control or experience. If you can follow arrows from "win" back to "easier to win" without passing a brake, you have this loop.

Pitfalls

  • Loops hidden across resources. Kills give gold, gold buys items, items get kills. No single number snowballs, so the loop is easy to miss until playtesters say matches feel decided early.
  • Testing only with your team. Players of equal skill keep matches close by skill alone. Test with uneven players too: the loop magnifies whoever gets ahead first.
  • No exit for a decided game. A strong loop plus a fixed long match length is the worst mix. Either weaken the loop or let the game end when it is over.
  • A brake so strong that winning means nothing. Brakes should slow the snowball, not delete it.
  • Pay to snowball. Selling power in a competitive game hands a reinforcing loop to whoever pays: they win more, earn more and pull further ahead. That is a business choice made against the other players, and it is fair to call it what it is.

When not to brake at all. In a single-player game, snowballing against the computer is often the fun: growing from weak to unstoppable is a power fantasy, and the computer does not mind losing. Short rounds can also let a lead build freely, because the loser does not wait long.

In real games

Monopoly is the classic example: property earns rent, rent buys more property and houses, and the player who falls behind usually keeps rolling the dice long after the result is clear.

Real-time strategy games commonly have an economic loop: workers gather resources, resources buy more workers. An early lead in workers grows into a lead in everything else.

Multiplayer online battle arenas run the kills, gold and items loop described above. League of Legends adds a brake to it: a player on a kill streak carries a larger bounty, so ending their streak pays the trailing team extra gold.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Pick one success in your game: a kill, a captured point, a level-up. Write the chain of arrows from it to the next attempt. If the chain makes the next success easier, estimate by how much. Then add one brake, a cap or a wear-off timer, and play three matches. You are done when you can say how much one success changes the next chance, before and after the brake.

Takeaways

  • A positive feedback loop makes success produce more success, so small early leads grow.
  • It ends games and rewards good play, but a strong loop decides matches long before they finish.
  • Measure it: how much does one win change the next win chance, and when is the result settled?
  • Brake it with caps, bonuses that wear off, or costs on the lead, or let a decided game end.
  • Against the computer, snowballing is often the point. Between people, the loser's experience is the cost.
