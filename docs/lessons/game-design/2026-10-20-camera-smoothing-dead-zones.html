Camera smoothing and dead zones

How a dead zone and frame-rate independent smoothing stop a 2D camera from copying every small movement, and how to tune both without losing the player.

You tap left to line up a jump, and the whole level twitches. You hop in place, and the ground bounces with you. Nothing is broken: the camera is doing what it was told, which is to keep the player in the exact centre of the screen at every moment.

A camera that copies the player

A locked camera sets its position to the player's every frame. The player can never leave the screen, but every movement of the character becomes a movement of the entire picture.

A four pixel correction moves every tile and enemy by four pixels. A jump moves the floor. Players rarely name this, but they tend to describe the game as twitchy or tiring, and some report motion discomfort from cameras that bob with every jump.

Fix one: a dead zone

A dead zone (also called a camera window) is a box around the centre of the screen. While the player is inside it, the camera does not move at all. When the player pushes against an edge, the camera moves by exactly the distance the player is past that edge, which this lesson calls the excess. Small corrections and jumps stay inside the box, so the world stays still. A long run drags the box along.

Fix two: smoothing

The dead zone decides where the camera should be: its target. Without smoothing, the camera jumps straight to it, so it starts and stops as hard as the player does. Smoothing (or easing) makes the camera cover only part of the gap each frame, so it moves fast when far away and slows as it arrives.

The clearest way to set its strength is a half-life: the time it takes to close half the gap. With a half-life of 120 ms, the camera is half way there after 120 ms, three quarters after 240 ms, and so on. The fraction to move this frame is:

local k = 1 - 2 ^ (-dt / HALF_LIFE)
cam.x = cam.x + (target.x - cam.x) * k

Because dt (the length of the frame in seconds) is inside the formula, the camera behaves the same at 30, 60 or 144 frames per second.

The cost: lag

A smoothed camera always trails a moving target. When the player runs at a steady speed, the gap settles at about speed × half-life ÷ 0.69. At the demo's run speed of 220 pixels per second, a 120 ms half-life trails by about 38 pixels. A 300 ms half-life trails by about 95. That lag adds to half the dead zone width, and all of it comes out of the view ahead of the player.

Starting points, not rules

Dead zone: about a sixth to a quarter of the view wide, and tall enough to hold an ordinary jump (in the demo, 80 by 140 pixels for a 122 pixel jump). Smoothing: a half-life of 80 to 150 ms tends to feel smooth but still attached. Past about 250 ms it tends to feel floaty. Tune on your fastest section.

When not to use it

Never smooth a camera the player aims with, such as a first person view driven by the mouse. There, the camera is the input, and smoothing adds latency to every movement. Single-screen games have no camera to move. And be sparing in fast games: a big dead zone plus heavy smoothing leaves a running player near the edge of the screen, meeting obstacles too late to react.

The code

One function, with the demo's names and numbers. A dead zone size of zero or a half-life of zero switches that part off.

local DEAD_W, DEAD_H = 80, 140   -- dead zone size in pixels
local HALF_LIFE = 0.12           -- seconds to close half the gap
local FOCUS_Y = 0.6              -- camera centre sits 60% down the screen
local cam = { x = 0, y = 0 }

-- How far p is past the edges of centre +/- half (0 when inside).
local function excess(p, centre, half)
  if p > centre + half then return p - (centre + half) end
  if p < centre - half then return p - (centre - half) end
  return 0
end

local function updateCamera(dt, px, py)   -- px, py: player centre
  local tx = cam.x + excess(px, cam.x, DEAD_W / 2)
  local ty = cam.y + excess(py, cam.y, DEAD_H / 2)
  local k = 1
  if HALF_LIFE > 0 then
    k = 1 - 2 ^ (-dt / HALF_LIFE)
  end
  cam.x = cam.x + (tx - cam.x) * k
  cam.y = cam.y + (ty - cam.y) * k
end

function love.update(dt)
  updatePlayer(dt)                         -- your movement code
  updateCamera(dt, player.x, player.y)    -- the player's centre
end

function love.draw()
  local w, h = love.graphics.getWidth(), love.graphics.getHeight()
  love.graphics.push()
  -- round only the drawing offset; keep cam.x unrounded
  love.graphics.translate(math.floor(w / 2 - cam.x + 0.5),
                          math.floor(h * FOCUS_Y - cam.y + 0.5))
  drawWorld()                              -- your level and player
  love.graphics.pop()
end

Pitfalls

  • Smoothing with a fixed fraction. The common line cam.x = cam.x + (target - cam.x) * 0.1 runs once per frame, so its strength depends on the frame rate. Its half-life is about 110 ms at 60 frames per second, 46 ms at 144 and 219 ms at 30. Put dt in the formula as above.
  • A dead zone that is too wide. A player at the edge of the box sees little of where they are going. Keep it narrow horizontally.
  • Higher platforms. After landing on a higher platform the player sits near the top of the box, so the next jump pushes the camera up anyway. Moving the camera vertically only on landing fixes this; that is in the next lesson.
  • Losing the player. Lag plus half the dead zone must stay well inside half the screen at top speed, and falls are often faster than runs. Clamping the gap to a maximum distance is a common safety net.
  • Jitter from rounding. Smoothing from a rounded camera position makes it stall and step. Round only the drawing offset.
  • Smoothing the shake. Add screen shake to the drawing offset, not to cam.x, or the smoothing eats it.

In real games

Camera windows and eased cameras are common in 2D side-scrollers. Itay Keren's essay Scroll Back, based on his talk at the GDC 2015 Independent Games Summit, catalogues these techniques and many variations across decades of side-scrolling games.

Both engines covered in these lessons build them in. Godot's Camera2D has drag margins, which are a dead zone, and a position smoothing option. Unity's Cinemachine package offers dead zone and damping settings on its 2D follow camera. Property names change between releases, so check the documentation for your version.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Replace the camera in your project with updateCamera. With both sizes and the half-life at zero, check it behaves like a locked camera. Make the dead zone just taller than a normal jump and confirm that hopping in place no longer moves the screen. Then run at top speed and raise the half-life until the player ends up a third of the way from centre to edge, and halve it. You are done when you can hop, correct and run without the world twitching.

Takeaways

  • A locked camera turns every small player movement into movement of the whole screen.
  • A dead zone lets the player move inside a box without moving the camera. Outside it, the camera moves only by the excess.
  • Smoothing with a half-life softens starts and stops. Put dt in the formula so it feels the same at any frame rate.
  • Smoothing always costs lag, roughly speed times half-life divided by 0.69. Check it at top speed.
  • Do not smooth a camera the player aims with, and do not add either technique where there is no scrolling.
