Impact particles: dust, sparks and debris

How short bursts of dust, sparks and debris tell the player what happened, what was touched and how hard, and how to scale them with the impact.

Your character drops from a high ledge, lands, and simply stops. The game knows it was a hard landing because it measured the speed. The player is told nothing. A puff of dust and a few pebbles would say it in a quarter of a second.

Particles are messages

A particle is a tiny, short-lived visual object: a dot, a line, a small square. It usually has no effect on the game rules. Particles are spawned in groups called bursts when something happens, or streamed steadily by an emitter for things like smoke or rain. This lesson is about bursts on impact.

A good impact burst answers four questions for the player at a glance:

  • What happened? Something landed, hit or broke.
  • Where? The burst starts at the point of contact.
  • What was touched? Soft dust for dirt, bright sparks for metal.
  • How hard? More particles, flying faster, for a harder impact.

A burst that answers none of these is decoration, competing for the player's eyes.

One particle, start to finish

Every particle needs only a few numbers: a position, a velocity, an age and a lifetime (how many seconds it lives). Each frame it is pulled by gravity, slowed by drag (a steady loss of speed, like air resistance), moved and aged, and removed when its age reaches its lifetime. The ratio t = age / life runs from 0 to 1 and drives the look: fade as t rises, grow a dust puff.

Three kinds, three messages

Dust, sparks and debris use the same code with different numbers.

Scale the burst with the impact

The most useful trick is to turn the impact into a strength between 0 and 1 and multiply count and launch speed by it. In the demo, strength is the landing speed minus 200, divided by 700, clamped to that range. A small hop lands at about 500 pixels per second, a strength near 0.4. A big jump lands at about 850, near 0.9. Below 200 nothing spawns, so tiny drops stay quiet.

A threshold adds a second layer of meaning. Debris only appears when strength is 0.6 or more, so the player learns, without being told, that pebbles mean "that was a big one".

Add a little randomness to every particle's angle, speed and lifetime. Otherwise every burst is the same stamp, and the eye soon stops reading it.

Starting points to tune

At full strength: dust about 12 puffs living 0.5 seconds, sparks about 16 streaks living 0.3 seconds, debris about 6 chunks living 1 second. Most of a burst should be gone within half a second. These are starting points, not rules: a slow, chunky game wants fewer, larger particles; a fast arcade game more, shorter ones.

Try it

Hop and big-jump on the dirt with every toggle off, then turn them on one at a time. Do the same on the metal. Then switch off Scale with landing speed: every landing gets the full burst and the hop and big jump look alike. Finally push count and lifetime to 300 percent and notice when the effect starts to hide the character.

With everything off, a landing is only a stop. With dust on, the hop and the big jump already look different, and sparks or pebbles tell you the surface without looking at the floor.

The code

The demo's particle system in LÖVE, same names and numbers. Call land on the frame the player touches the ground, with their downward speed, before zeroing it. updateParticles(dt) goes in love.update and the drawing loop in love.draw.

local FLOOR = 340
local KINDS = {
  dust   = { count = 12, life = 0.5, speed = 120, gravity = -40,  drag = 6, size = 4 },
  sparks = { count = 16, life = 0.3, speed = 420, gravity = 900,  drag = 1, size = 2 },
  debris = { count = 6,  life = 1.0, speed = 320, gravity = 1200, drag = 0, size = 4, bounce = 0.4 },
}
local DEBRIS_MIN = 0.6               -- only hard landings throw debris
local countScale, lifeScale = 1, 1   -- the demo's two sliders
local particles = {}

local function burst(kind, x, y, strength)
  local k = KINDS[kind]
  local n = math.floor(k.count * countScale * strength + 0.5)
  for i = 1, n do
    local angle
    if kind == "dust" then              -- low, half left and half right
      angle = -math.random() * 0.35
      if i % 2 == 0 then angle = math.pi - angle end
    elseif kind == "sparks" then        -- wide upward fan
      angle = -math.pi * (0.1 + 0.8 * math.random())
    else                                -- debris: mostly straight up
      angle = -math.pi * (0.33 + 0.34 * math.random())
    end
    local speed = k.speed * (0.4 + 0.6 * strength) * (0.5 + 0.5 * math.random())
    table.insert(particles, { kind = kind, x = x, y = y, age = 0,
      vx = math.cos(angle) * speed, vy = math.sin(angle) * speed,
      life = k.life * lifeScale * (0.7 + 0.3 * math.random()),
      spin = (math.random() - 0.5) * 20 })
  end
end

local function land(x, impactSpeed, surface)
  local strength = math.max(0, math.min(1, (impactSpeed - 200) / 700))
  -- strength = 1 here makes every landing look the same
  if surface == "dirt" then
    burst("dust", x, FLOOR - 2, strength)
    if strength >= DEBRIS_MIN then burst("debris", x, FLOOR - 2, strength) end
  else
    burst("dust", x, FLOOR - 2, strength * 0.4)
    burst("sparks", x, FLOOR - 2, strength)
  end
end

local function updateParticles(dt)
  for i = #particles, 1, -1 do         -- backwards, so removing is safe
    local p = particles[i]
    local k = KINDS[p.kind]
    p.age = p.age + dt
    p.vx = p.vx / (1 + k.drag * dt)
    p.vy = p.vy / (1 + k.drag * dt) + k.gravity * dt
    p.x = p.x + p.vx * dt
    p.y = p.y + p.vy * dt
    if p.y > FLOOR then
      if k.bounce then
        p.y = FLOOR
        p.vy = -p.vy * k.bounce
        p.vx = p.vx * 0.7
      else
        p.age = p.life                 -- sparks and dust die on the floor
      end
    end
    if p.age >= p.life then table.remove(particles, i) end
  end
end

function love.draw()
  for _, p in ipairs(particles) do
    local k = KINDS[p.kind]
    local t = p.age / p.life             -- 0 at birth, 1 at death
    if p.kind == "dust" then
      love.graphics.setColor(0.6, 0.6, 0.6, 1 - t)
      love.graphics.circle("fill", p.x, p.y, k.size + 8 * t)
    elseif p.kind == "sparks" then
      love.graphics.setColor(1, 0.75, 0.25, 1 - t)
      love.graphics.line(p.x, p.y, p.x - p.vx * 0.03, p.y - p.vy * 0.03)
    else
      love.graphics.setColor(0.45, 0.35, 0.25, math.min(1, (1 - t) / 0.3))
      love.graphics.push()
      love.graphics.translate(p.x, p.y)
      love.graphics.rotate(p.spin * p.age)
      love.graphics.rectangle("fill", -k.size, -k.size, k.size * 2, k.size * 2)
      love.graphics.pop()
    end
  end
  love.graphics.setColor(1, 1, 1, 1)
end

Sparks are lines pointing back along their velocity, so faster sparks are longer streaks. Debris fades only in its last 30 percent, so it reads as solid.

Pitfalls

  • Particles that outstay the event. Dust still drifting a second later blends into the next burst. Keep most of a burst under half a second.
  • Hiding the action. A big burst can cover the character or the next bullet. Keep particles small near the player and draw them behind characters.
  • Particles that look like gameplay. If enemy bullets are small orange dots, orange sparks will be mistaken for them. Never borrow the look of threats or pickups.
  • The same burst every time. Without scaling, the effect carries no information. Even two levels, soft and hard, beat one.
  • Spawning per frame. For emitters that stream particles, spawn by time (for example 30 per second, using dt), not a fixed number per frame, or the effect doubles on a 120 hertz screen.
  • Creating garbage. A new table per particle is fine for a few hundred. For thousands, reuse dead particles from a pool or use the engine's particle system.
  • Celebrating everything. Showers of coins and sparkles on every reward can make tiny wins feel large. Slot machines are known for celebrating payouts that are smaller than the bet. If your bursts make a loss feel like a win, you are misleading the player.

When to hold back

Bullet-dense games, where clutter hides threats. Puzzle and strategy games, where the player needs to read the board. Minimal art styles, where detail clashes. Competitive games, where effects tend to be kept readable. And bright, flickering sparks can bother players sensitive to flashing, so keep them small and offer a setting to reduce effects.

In real games

Dust on landing, dust when a character turns sharply, and sparks when metal strikes metal are common in action games and platformers. Many shooters show a different bullet impact for each surface, such as stone chips or sparks off metal, which tells the player what they hit even at a distance.

Engines build this in. LÖVE has love.graphics.newParticleSystem. Godot has GPUParticles2D and CPUParticles2D and their 3D versions. Unity has a Particle System component, and Unreal Engine has the Niagara effects system and physical materials, whose surface types are commonly used to choose which impact effect to play.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Pick one impact in your game, such as landing, a hit or a wall bump. Add the burst and updateParticles functions, and spawn only dust, with strength from the impact speed. Tune the 200 and 700 so your gentlest impact is near 0 and your hardest near 1.

Then add one second burst: a threshold kind for hard impacts, or a different kind for a second surface. You are done when someone watching only the character, not the floor or the numbers, can tell a small impact from a big one, and the screen is clear again within half a second.

Takeaways

  • An impact burst should say what happened, where, what was touched and how hard.
  • Turn the impact into a strength from 0 to 1 and scale count and speed with it.
  • Use the kind of particle to show the surface, and thresholds to mark big events.
  • Keep bursts short, small near the player, and visually unlike anything dangerous.
  • Hold back in busy, competitive or puzzle games, and never use bursts to dress up a loss.
