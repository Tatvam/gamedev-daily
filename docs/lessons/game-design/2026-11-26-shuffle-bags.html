Shuffle bags: random order, fair shares

How drawing outcomes from a shuffled bag without putting them back keeps variety while capping both droughts and floods, and what it costs.

Your falling-block game deals one of seven pieces at random. The board is set up for a long straight piece, and forty pieces later it still has not come. Then three square pieces arrive in a row. Nothing is broken: every piece really was a fair 1 in 7. It just does not feel fair.

Dealing instead of rolling

Rolling a seven-sided die for every piece makes each draw independent: the die does not remember what it rolled before. Independence gives you two kinds of streak. A drought is a run of draws without one particular outcome. A flood is the same outcome arriving again and again.

With seven pieces, the chance that one given piece is missing from the next 20 draws is (6/7)20, about 4.6 percent. Over 1000 pieces, the longest drought of any piece is typically around 46. And in a game of only 100 pieces, the chance of seeing the same piece three times in a row somewhere is above 80 percent.

A shuffle bag replaces the die with a deck of cards. Put one of each piece in a bag, shuffle it, and deal from it without putting anything back. When the bag is empty, refill it and shuffle again. Inside each bag the order is random, but the contents are fixed, so every piece appears exactly once per seven.

What the bag guarantees

With one copy of each piece, the worst case is easy to work out. A piece can be dealt first in one bag and last in the next, so at most 12 pieces pass without it. It can also be dealt last in one bag and first in the next, so at most two arrive in a row. Under pure random, about 13 percent of droughts are longer than 12.

You can put more than one copy of each piece in the bag. That is the strength control: a bigger bag is more random and gives weaker guarantees. With k copies of each piece, a drought is at most 12k and a flood at most 2k in a row.

Bags are not only for equal odds

The bag holds whatever mix you want. A bag of three normal hits and one critical hit gives a 25 percent critical rate that can never go more than six attacks without one. A bag of one coin room, two enemy rooms and one empty room spreads a dungeon's rewards out evenly. Compared with the last two lessons: pseudo-random distribution bends the chance of one outcome, and a pity timer caps the wait for one rare outcome. A bag fixes the proportions of every outcome at once and limits both kinds of streak.

The cost: the bag can be counted

A bag remembers, so a player can remember too. Halfway through a seven-piece bag the remaining pieces are known, and the last piece is certain. In a puzzle game that tends to be welcome: counting the bag becomes a skill. In other places it is a leak. If a player can tell that the critical hit in a bag of four has not come yet, they can save their best attack for the fourth swing.

When not to use it

  • Rare outcomes. A 1 in 100 item needs a bag of 100. After it drops, the player is certain not to see another for the rest of the bag, and the dry spell starts to feel scheduled. A pity timer suits rare rewards better.
  • Dice the player can see. If the game shows dice, players expect dice. Swapping in a bag silently breaks that promise. A visible deck is fine; a hidden one pretending to be dice is not.
  • Games where streaks are the fun. A party game or a gambling-themed game may want the wild swings.

Bags can be used against the player

A bag is only fair if it is really shuffled. A designer can order a "random" bag by hand so that near misses come just before a purchase prompt, which is deception. And when players pay per draw from a finite box of prizes, the cost of emptying the box is the real price of the best prize. If you sell draws, say what is in the box and how many are left.

Try it

Start with the shuffle bag off and press "Deal 1000". Look at the chart: some piece has gone 40 or more pieces without showing up, and the numbers under the bars show floods of three or four. Now switch the shuffle bag on. The same seed replays, every bar stops at the dashed limit of 12, and nothing appears more than twice in a row. Deal a few pieces one at a time and watch the bag panel: near the end of each bag it tells you which pieces cannot come next. Then raise the copies per bag and see the limits loosen.

The code

The whole system is refillBag and nextPiece. The since and longest tables are the same bookkeeping as the chart, so you can print them and check the limit of 12.

local PIECES = { "I", "O", "T", "S", "Z", "J", "L" }
local USE_BAG = true
local COPIES = 1        -- copies of each piece per bag: 1 is strictest

local bag = {}
local since = {}        -- pieces dealt since each piece last appeared
local longest = {}      -- longest drought seen for each piece
for _, name in ipairs(PIECES) do
  since[name] = 0
  longest[name] = 0
end

local function refillBag()
  for _, name in ipairs(PIECES) do
    for _ = 1, COPIES do bag[#bag + 1] = name end
  end
  -- Fisher-Yates shuffle: every order is equally likely
  for i = #bag, 2, -1 do
    local j = love.math.random(i)
    bag[i], bag[j] = bag[j], bag[i]
  end
end

local function nextPiece()
  local piece
  if USE_BAG then
    if #bag == 0 then refillBag() end
    piece = table.remove(bag)       -- deal the last one, never put it back
  else
    piece = PIECES[love.math.random(#PIECES)]
  end
  for _, name in ipairs(PIECES) do
    if name == piece then
      since[name] = 0
    else
      since[name] = since[name] + 1
      if since[name] > longest[name] then longest[name] = since[name] end
    end
  end
  return piece
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "space" then
    print(nextPiece(), "longest drought for I: " .. longest.I)
  end
end

love.math.random(i) returns a whole number from 1 to i, which is exactly what the shuffle needs. Set USE_BAG to false to compare.

Pitfalls

  • A biased shuffle. Swapping each item with a random position anywhere in the bag, or sorting by a random comparison, makes some orders more likely than others. Use the Fisher-Yates loop above: each step picks only from the items not yet placed.
  • Forgetting to save the bag. If the bag is rebuilt on every load, quitting and reloading resets the guarantees, and players can reroll by restarting. Save the remaining contents with the game state.
  • One bag for everything. If loot and critical hits share a bag, one system eats the other's draws. Give each decision its own bag.
  • One bag for several players. In multiplayer, a shared bag lets one player take the pieces another needed. Separate bags per player are fairer, while a shared bag with the same seed for everyone suits races where all players should get the same sequence.
  • Floods at the seam. Two in a row can only happen where bags meet. Some games reshuffle a new bag if its first item matches the last one dealt. That removes the repeat but changes the limits a little, so check them again.

In real games

The best-known shuffle bag deals Tetris pieces. Official Tetris games made under The Tetris Company's design guideline commonly use a seven-piece bag, which fan wikis call the Random Generator. Fan sources usually credit Tetris Worlds (2001) with bringing it to a wide audience, but they disagree about which game used it first, and I could not open those pages to check the details.

Card games and board games are shuffle bags you can hold: a deck of cards is a bag dealt without replacement, and some board games offer a deck of cards as an alternative to rolling dice, for the same reason this lesson does. Another common approach in falling-block games keeps a short history of recent pieces and rerolls any piece that appears in it, which cuts floods without a fixed bag.

Find a random choice in your game with a few outcomes of similar weight: which enemy spawns, which pickup appears, which voice line plays. Log 200 results with the plain random version and note the longest drought and the longest repeat. Replace it with refillBag and nextPiece, with one copy of each outcome, and log again. You are done when the new longest drought is at most 2 × (bag size − 1) and you have decided, by playing, whether the pattern is noticeable enough to need a second copy.

Takeaways

  • A shuffle bag deals outcomes without putting them back, so the order is random but the proportions are exact.
  • With one copy of each of seven outcomes, a drought lasts at most 12 and a repeat at most 2 in a row. With k copies, 12k and 2k.
  • Bag size is the strength control: bigger bags feel more random and guarantee less.
  • A bag can be counted. That is a skill in a puzzle game and a leak in a combat system.
  • Use a bag for common outcomes, a pity timer for rare ones, and never fake the shuffle.
