Dynamic difficulty adjustment

How a game can measure the player and nudge a hidden difficulty value to match them, how to tune it, and what goes wrong when players notice.

One playtester dies on your third wave twelve times and quits. Another clears all ten waves without a scratch and calls the game "fine". Yesterday's lesson drew a difficulty curve for one imagined player. Neither of these two is that player.

Some games stop guessing. They watch how the player is doing and quietly move the difficulty while they play.

A game that tunes itself

Dynamic difficulty adjustment, usually shortened to DDA, means the game measures the player's performance during play and changes its own difficulty in response. It is a negative feedback loop, like the catch-up mechanics from an earlier lesson, balancing the player against the game.

Every DDA system has three parts:

  1. A measurement. How the player is doing: hits taken, deaths, time to clear a room.
  2. A hidden value. One number, here called difficulty, from 0 (easiest) to 1 (hardest). The measurement nudges it up or down.
  3. Rules that read it. Spawn rate, enemy speed, damage: the knobs the player feels.

The simplest rule that works

Raise difficulty a little every second the player is doing fine. Lower it by a fixed step every time they are hit. That is the whole system in the demo below:

difficulty = difficulty + CLIMB * dt   -- every frame
difficulty = difficulty - DROP          -- on each hit

Over a minute the climb adds CLIMB * 60 and the hits remove DROP per hit. The two cancel when the player is hit CLIMB * 60 / DROP times a minute. So if you set CLIMB = DROP * target / 60, the value drifts until the player is being hit at your target rate, whatever their skill.

So you no longer pick how fast the rocks are. You pick what the game should feel like, "about four hits a minute", and the system finds the rock speed that produces it for each player.

Fall fast, climb slow

The rule is lopsided on purpose. A hit drops the value at once, so help arrives while the player is struggling; the climb back is gradual, so pressure returns gently. Two more guards are needed:

  • A floor and a ceiling, limits the value may not pass. They stop the game becoming trivial or unfair.
  • A short cooldown after a hit, so a cluster of rocks hitting at once counts as one mistake, not three.

Starting points, not rules

Difficulty from 0 to 1, starting at 0.4. Drop 0.15 per hit, cooldown 0.6 seconds, floor 0.05, ceiling 1. Target four hits a minute for a game with no lives, fewer where hits really cost. Tune by watching players.

When players notice

Three problems tend to appear once players see the system:

  • Getting better stops showing. If the game always matches your skill, it always feels equally hard, and the moment of "this used to be difficult" never comes.
  • Sandbagging. A player who knows that failing makes things easier can fail on purpose before a hard section. The rule now rewards playing badly.
  • Lost trust. Some players feel patronised or cheated when they learn a win was helped. Others do not mind at all.

Hidden DDA is, plainly, a decision to deceive the player about how the game works. When it only keeps someone out of frustration, many designers consider that a fair trade. The same machinery becomes manipulation when it is tuned for spending instead of enjoyment, for example making a game harder right when a paid boost is on offer. Ask who the adjustment serves.

Try it

Press Start and dodge for a minute with "Reveal hidden difficulty" switched off. Did the game change? Then switch Reveal on and watch the line. Try being hit on purpose three times and see what the game does for you. Then pick each bot: the same rule settles at very different difficulties, while both end up near the target hit rate.

The code

The same names and numbers as the demo. spawnRock and updateRocks stand for your game's own spawner and collision code; updateRocks should call onPlayerHit when a rock touches the player.

-- Hidden difficulty from 0 (easiest) to 1 (hardest)
local difficulty = 0.4
local FLOOR, CEILING = 0.05, 1.0
local TARGET_HITS_PER_MINUTE = 4
local ADAPT_SPEED = 1
local DROP = 0.15 * ADAPT_SPEED                   -- fall per hit
local CLIMB = DROP * TARGET_HITS_PER_MINUTE / 60  -- rise per second
local HIT_COOLDOWN = 0.6

local cooldown = 0
local spawnDebt = 0

local function clamp(x, lo, hi)
  return math.max(lo, math.min(hi, x))
end

-- What difficulty means in this game
local function rocksPerSecond() return 1 + 7 * difficulty end
local function crossTime() return 3 - 2 * difficulty end

function onPlayerHit()
  if cooldown > 0 then return end   -- a cluster of rocks is one mistake
  cooldown = HIT_COOLDOWN
  difficulty = clamp(difficulty - DROP, FLOOR, CEILING)
end

function love.update(dt)
  cooldown = math.max(0, cooldown - dt)
  difficulty = clamp(difficulty + CLIMB * dt, FLOOR, CEILING)

  spawnDebt = spawnDebt + rocksPerSecond() * dt
  while spawnDebt >= 1 do
    spawnDebt = spawnDebt - 1
    spawnRock(crossTime())   -- your spawner: seconds to cross the screen
  end
  updateRocks(dt)            -- your movement and collision code
end

Log difficulty and hits per minute for a few testers. If the hit rate settles near the target while the value settles at different levels for different people, it is working.

Pitfalls

  • Adjusting too fast. A large step makes the game swing between easy and hard within seconds, which feels random. Raise the adapt speed in the demo to see it.
  • Measuring the wrong thing. Deaths alone react too late. Pick a measurement that moves often and that good play really reduces.
  • Changing what the player can see. Players notice a health bar that suddenly lasts longer. Prefer knobs that are hard to perceive, such as spawn timing or enemy accuracy.
  • Adjusting mid-moment. Changing a boss while the player learns its pattern breaks what they learned. Adjust between attempts or rooms instead.

When not to use it

Competitive play, score attack and speedrunning need everyone on the same rules. Games whose appeal is mastery of a fixed challenge lose their point if the challenge moves. For many games a visible choice, such as difficulty options or an offer of help after several failures, does the same job without the secrecy.

In real games

Resident Evil 4 (2005) is widely reported to keep a hidden difficulty rank that rises and falls with how the player is doing, affecting things such as enemy aggression and damage. Capcom did not advertise it, so treat specific numbers found online with care.

Andy Gavin, co-founder of Naughty Dog, has written that the early Crash Bandicoot games used what the team called DDA to quietly help players who kept failing in the same place. The original post was not checked for this lesson, so details are left out.

The AI Director in Left 4 Dead (2008), described by Valve's Michael Booth, estimates how intense the game is for each player and builds up or holds back enemies to create peaks and rests: adaptive pacing, a close relative of this lesson's loop.

Robin Hunicke's research system Hamlet, built on the Half-Life engine, adjusted item supplies and enemy strength from a running estimate of player performance.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Pick one difficulty knob in your game and drive it from a single difficulty value between 0 and 1. Add the climb and drop rule from the code, with a floor and a ceiling. Draw the value on screen in a debug corner.

Play badly on purpose for one minute, then as well as you can for one minute. You are done when the value visibly falls and then rises, and when you have written down one way a player could exploit it.

Takeaways

  • DDA is a feedback loop: measure the player, nudge one hidden value, let that value set the rules.
  • Climb slowly while the player is safe and drop on each failure. The value settles where the player fails at your target rate, so you design a feel, not a number.
  • Fall fast, climb slow, clamp to a floor and ceiling, and ignore clusters of hits with a cooldown.
  • Once players notice, DDA can hide their progress and reward failing on purpose. Prefer invisible knobs and adjustments between attempts.
  • Hidden adjustment is a form of deception. It is defensible when it serves the player's enjoyment and manipulative when it serves spending.
