Screen shake that sells the hit

How a short, decaying camera shake sells impact, and how strength, decay, smooth noise and direction keep it from becoming tiring.

A rocket hits a wall, a sprite swaps to an explosion, and nothing else happens. It looks correct and feels like nothing. Add a few pixels of camera jolt and the same explosion suddenly has weight. Add too much and, ten minutes later, the player has a headache and cannot see what hit them.

A shake is a small offset that dies away

Screen shake moves the whole drawn image by a small offset for a fraction of a second after something big happens. The world does not move. Only the camera's drawing position does, and only for a moment.

The player reads it as a real jolt: something hit hard enough to move the viewer. That is why it suits explosions and heavy landings, and feels wrong for small, frequent events.

Trauma: one number that drives everything

A clean way to organise shake is a single value called trauma, between 0 and 1. The name and the approach come from Squirrel Eiserloh's GDC 2016 talk on camera maths. It works in three rules:

  1. Impacts add trauma. A small hit might add 0.3, a big blast 0.7. Trauma is capped at 1, so ten explosions at once cannot shake the screen off the monitor.
  2. Trauma drains at a steady rate. If full trauma takes 0.4 seconds to drain, the shake from any hit is over within 0.4 seconds.
  3. Shake strength comes from trauma squared. The offset each frame is MAX_OFFSET * trauma * trauma. Squaring (cubing is another common choice) keeps small hits subtle and makes big ones fall away quickly, instead of every hit ending in a long, low buzz.

Which way to move the camera

Knowing how much to shake each frame, you still have to pick a direction. Three common choices:

  • Random every frame. A new random offset each frame. Simple, but harsh and jittery, and it changes speed with the frame rate: at 144 frames per second it buzzes more than twice as fast as at 60.
  • Smooth noise. Read the offset from a smooth noise function, a function that returns random-looking values that change gradually, sampled at the current time. The camera wobbles instead of teleporting, and the wobble speed is set in hertz, not frames.
  • Directional. Push the camera back and forth along the direction of the hit, like a recoil. It tells the player where the force came from, which a random shake cannot.

Many games also add a small rotation, a degree or two at full trauma. In 3D games rotation-only shake is often preferred, because moving a 3D camera can push it through a nearby wall.

Starting points to tune

Maximum offset: about 1 to 2 percent of the screen width, so 3 to 6 pixels on a 320 pixel wide pixel-art screen, or 15 to 30 pixels at 1920. Decay: 0.2 to 0.5 seconds for hits, up to about a second for a rare huge event. Rotation, if any: 1 to 3 degrees. These are numbers to begin tuning from, not rules.

Try it

Fire a few shots with shake off, then switch it on and fire again. Try a blast. Then hold fire and watch the trauma line pin against the cap instead of climbing forever. Finally, switch between the three shake types and watch the small circle at the bottom left, which traces the camera's recent offsets.

With squaring on, a single shot barely moves the camera while the blast lands hard. Turn squaring off and every shot buzzes. Directional mode draws a straight line in the trace circle: the camera recoils along the line of fire.

The code

This is the demo's shake in LÖVE, with the same names and default numbers. Call addTrauma wherever an impact happens. The shake is applied only while drawing, inside push and pop, so the heads-up display is drawn after it and stays still.

local MAX_OFFSET = 8     -- pixels at full trauma
local DECAY_TIME = 0.4   -- seconds for full trauma to drain to zero
local NOISE_FREQ = 15    -- wobble speed of smooth shake, per second
local DIR_FREQ = 12      -- swings per second of directional shake
local mode = "noise"     -- "noise", "random" or "directional"

local trauma, time, hitTime = 0, 0, 0
local dirX, dirY = 1, 0
local shakeX, shakeY = 0, 0

-- Call on impact: 0.3 for a shot, 0.7 for a blast.
function addTrauma(amount, dx, dy)
  trauma = math.min(trauma + amount, 1)
  hitTime, dirX, dirY = time, dx or 1, dy or 0
end

function love.update(dt)
  time = time + dt
  trauma = math.max(trauma - dt / DECAY_TIME, 0)
  local amount = MAX_OFFSET * trauma * trauma
  if mode == "noise" then
    shakeX = amount * (love.math.noise(time * NOISE_FREQ, 0.5) * 2 - 1)
    shakeY = amount * (love.math.noise(time * NOISE_FREQ, 9.5) * 2 - 1)
  elseif mode == "random" then
    shakeX = amount * (love.math.random() * 2 - 1)
    shakeY = amount * (love.math.random() * 2 - 1)
  else
    local swing = math.sin((time - hitTime) * DIR_FREQ * 2 * math.pi)
    shakeX, shakeY = dirX * amount * swing, dirY * amount * swing
  end
end

function love.draw()
  love.graphics.push()
  love.graphics.translate(math.floor(shakeX + 0.5), math.floor(shakeY + 0.5))
  drawWorld()   -- your game world: shaken
  love.graphics.pop()
  drawHud()     -- score, health, menus: never shaken
end

love.math.noise returns smooth noise between 0 and 1, so * 2 - 1 centres it on zero. Sampling it at two different second coordinates gives two unrelated wobbles for x and y. The demo uses its own small noise function with the same behaviour. Rounding the offset to whole pixels keeps pixel art crisp.

Pitfalls

  • Moving the real camera. If the shake changes the camera position that gameplay reads, aiming, culling and mouse-to-world conversion all wobble with it. Keep the shake as a separate offset added only at draw time.
  • Shaking the interface. A shaking health bar or menu is hard to read at exactly the moment the player needs it. Draw the heads-up display after the shake is removed.
  • No cap. If every hit adds to an unlimited value, a big fight shakes harder and longer than anything you designed. Cap trauma.
  • Shaking for everything. If the player's own pistol shakes the screen as hard as a boss explosion, neither means anything and the player tires fast. Save the big numbers for rare, important events.
  • Random offsets tied to the frame rate. A new random value each frame shakes faster on faster screens. Smooth noise sampled by time behaves the same at any frame rate.
  • No option to turn it off. Shake can cause discomfort or motion sickness. Offer a strength setting that goes to zero; it costs one multiplier.

When not to use screen shake

Puzzle games, strategy games and anything where the player reads text or fine detail during the action tend to suffer from it. Long-range precision aiming fights it directly. In virtual reality, moving the view without the player's own head moving is commonly advised against because it causes sickness. And in any game, if players keep missing what happened during a shake, it is too strong or too long.

In real games

Jan Willem Nijman of Vlambeer, the studio behind Nuclear Throne and Luftrausers, gave a well-known talk called "The art of screenshake", in which he applies a long series of small feel tricks to a simple shooter, screen shake among them, to show how much each one changes how it plays.

Celeste has a screen shake setting. According to the game's own changelog, a later update added a 50 percent shake option and made it the default: a public example of a team turning its shake down after release.

The trauma approach has spread well beyond one talk. The Bevy engine's examples, for instance, include a 2D screen shake built on it.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Add trauma, addTrauma and the draw-time offset from the code above to your own game. Hook it to exactly two events: one small (0.2 to 0.3) and one big (0.6 to 0.8). Add a key that toggles shake so you can compare.

Play for five minutes with it on. You are done when the big event clearly feels heavier with shake than without, the small event is barely noticeable on its own, and you never lose track of the player character during a shake.

Takeaways

  • Screen shake is a short, decaying draw offset that tells the player something hit hard.
  • Drive it with one capped trauma value: impacts add, time drains, and shake is trauma squared.
  • Smooth noise feels better than random jumps and behaves the same at any frame rate. Directional shake also says where the force came from.
  • Never shake the interface or the gameplay camera, and always let players turn shake down to zero.
  • Reserve strong shake for rare, important events, and skip it where the player must read fine detail.
