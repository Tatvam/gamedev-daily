Pity timers: putting a ceiling on bad luck

How a counter that guarantees a rare reward after a set number of misses caps the worst run of bad luck, what it does to the real drop rate, and when to skip it.

Your rare sword drops 2 percent of the time. Most players get it soon. But one player in a hundred opens 228 chests or more without it, and that player writes the angry review. The average is fine. The worst case is the problem.

A guarantee at the end of the drought

A pity timer is a counter of attempts since the last rare reward. Every attempt adds one. The attempt that brings the counter to a set number, the pity threshold, gives the rare for certain, and any rare resets the counter to zero. Many online games call the same idea bad luck protection.

Pseudo-random distribution, from the last lesson, bent the odds on every roll. A pity timer leaves them alone: a 2 percent chest with a threshold of 90 still pays 2 percent on openings 1 to 89. It does nothing for lucky or average players. It only cuts off the long tail of very unlucky ones.

Hard pity and soft pity

The plain version is called hard pity: a flat chance, then a sudden 100 percent. It has a visible edge: players learn the rare arrives "on 90", and the openings before it feel like paying off a debt.

Soft pity smooths that edge. Shortly before the threshold, the chance starts climbing with every miss, so most unlucky players get the rare a few openings early. Here soft pity starts three quarters of the way, at opening 68 of 90, and rises in a straight line to 100 percent. That is one simple choice, not a standard; a fixed step per miss also works.

What it does to the numbers

A pity timer always raises the real average rate, because it turns some long waits into shorter ones. With a 2 percent base chance:

With hard pity at 90, about 17 percent of all rares come from the guarantee, and the other 83 percent arrive by plain luck exactly as before. That is the shape you want: a safety net that catches a minority.

So the number on the box is no longer the real rate. Show players the real one, which the code below calculates. And if your economy was balanced around 2 percent, rares are now about a fifth more common: accept that, or lower the base chance.

Choosing a threshold

A reasonable starting point is two to three times the average wait: 100 to 150 openings at 2 percent. Then tune by how many rares come from the guarantee.

  • Too high and it is decoration: at 2 percent with a threshold of 300, about 1 wait in 400 reaches it.
  • Too low and the timer becomes the schedule. At a threshold of 50, 37 percent of rares come from the guarantee, and players start counting to 50 instead of hoping. The rare starts to feel like a wage.

Think in time too. Ninety openings might be one evening or two months.

When not to use it

  • Common effects. Above about 15 percent, droughts are short; pseudo-random distribution or a shuffle bag suits them better.
  • Short runs. In a 40-room roguelike run, a threshold of 90 never triggers, and scarcity is part of the run.
  • Items players must have. If progress needs the item, do not make it random at all.
  • Games where variance is the fun. A party game may want the wild streaks.

Pity can be turned against the player

When attempts cost real money, the threshold becomes the real price of the item: 90 pulls times the price of a pull. A visible counter at 70 of 90 pressures players to finish, because stopping feels like losing what they already paid. Pity is also used to justify very low base rates. If you sell attempts, show the base rate, the real rate and the worst-case cost in money before purchase, and do not let counters quietly expire. Some app stores and countries already require paid random items to show their odds.

Try it

With the pity timer off, press "Open 1000" a few times: the waits stretch far to the right. Switch pity on. The same rolls replay, but every long wait now stops at the threshold and piles into one tall bar at the dashed line. Turn on soft pity and watch that bar spread into a slope. Then drag the threshold down to 30 and see how much of the reward comes from the guarantee.

The code

The system is rareChance and openBox. realRate works out the true average once. Set SOFT_PITY to true for the ramp.

local BASE_CHANCE = 0.02    -- 2 percent on a normal opening
local PITY = 90             -- opening 90 since the last rare always pays
local SOFT_PITY = false      -- true: chance ramps up over the last quarter
local SOFT_START = math.floor(PITY * 0.75 + 0.5)   -- 68

local sinceRare = 0         -- save this with the player's progress

-- The rare chance on the nth opening since the last rare.
local function rareChance(n)
  if n >= PITY then return 1 end
  if SOFT_PITY and n > SOFT_START then
    local t = (n - SOFT_START) / (PITY - SOFT_START)
    return BASE_CHANCE + (1 - BASE_CHANCE) * t
  end
  return BASE_CHANCE
end

local function openBox()
  sinceRare = sinceRare + 1
  if love.math.random() < rareChance(sinceRare) then
    sinceRare = 0
    return "rare"
  end
  return "common"
end

-- The real average rate once pity is included.
local function realRate()
  local expected, survive = 0, 1
  for n = 1, PITY do
    local chance = rareChance(n)
    expected = expected + n * survive * chance
    survive = survive * (1 - chance)
  end
  return 1 / expected
end

function love.load()
  print(string.format("Real rate: %.2f%%", realRate() * 100))   -- 2.39%
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "space" then
    print(openBox(), "since last rare: " .. sinceRare)
  end
end

realRate adds up every possible wait, weighted by its chance, and stops at the threshold, where the chance is 1.

Pitfalls

  • Forgetting to save the counter. If sinceRare resets on restart, a player with short sessions never reaches the threshold. Save it, or keep it on the server.
  • Showing the base rate as the rate. With pity, "2 percent" understates the real chance. Show realRate(), or lower the base so the real rate matches the number you want.
  • One counter for many things. Decide what each counter protects: one item, one tier or one box type. A counter reset by any rare does not help a player chasing one specific rare.
  • Resetting only on the pity payout. Reset on every drop of the protected reward. Otherwise a lucky drop leaves the counter high and the next guarantee comes too soon.

In real games

Genshin Impact is the example players discuss most. Its wish banners guarantee a 5-star item within 90 wishes, as community wikis and guides consistently report. Players analysing large sets of recorded pulls report that the chance also rises from roughly wish 74, which they call soft pity. That comes from community analysis, not an official statement, and I could not open the wiki pages to check details.

Hearthstone guides describe a legendary card guaranteed within the first ten packs of each new set, a change press coverage dates to 2017, and a timer of about 40 packs between legendaries. Sources disagree on how officially the 40-pack figure was confirmed, so treat it as reported.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Pick the rarest random reward in your game. Note its base chance and how many attempts a player makes per hour. Set the demo to that chance, read the 1-in-100 wait, and turn it into hours. If that feels unfair, add rareChance and openBox with a threshold of two to three times the average wait, and print realRate(). You are done when the worst case is a time you would accept as a player, and you have decided whether to lower the base chance.

Takeaways

  • A pity timer counts misses and guarantees the reward at a threshold, so the worst case has a hard limit.
  • It leaves most attempts alone and cuts off only the unlucky tail. Soft pity ramps up just before the threshold.
  • Pity always raises the real rate. Calculate it, show it, and rebalance the base chance if needed.
  • A threshold of two to three times the average wait is a reasonable start. Too high does nothing; too low turns the rare into a wage.
  • With real money, the threshold is the real price. Be open about it.
