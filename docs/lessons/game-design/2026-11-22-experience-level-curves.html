Experience and level curves: the rhythm of level-ups

How the XP needed per level and the XP earned per minute decide when every level-up arrives across a whole game, and how to shape that timeline.

In the first twenty minutes you gain four levels and each one feels like a gift. By hour six you have not levelled in an hour. Or you hit the level cap halfway through, and the rest of the game hands you experience that does nothing. All three come from a table of numbers nobody looked at as a timeline.

A level curve is a schedule of rewards

Experience points (XP) are a score the player earns by playing. When the score reaches a threshold, the character gains a level: a sound, a flash of the interface, and usually something new, like more health or a skill. The list of thresholds is the level curve, sometimes called the XP table.

Every level-up is a reward, so the level curve decides when rewards arrive across the whole game. The player feels the time between level-ups, and that time comes from two numbers:

minutes to the next level = XP to the next level ÷ XP earned per minute

This is the cost over power ratio from the upgrade curves lesson at a bigger scale: minutes and hours instead of taps, judged by play session ("did I level tonight?").

Two ways to write the table

A table can list the XP to the next level or the total XP needed to be at a level. Totals hide the shape: they always climb steeply, even when the time per level is steady. Design with a third column, minutes to the next level, which needs an estimate of XP earned per minute at each level.

A formula to start from

The table comes from two rules, the same ones the demo and the code use:

xpToNext(level)    = BASE_XP * level ^ CURVE
xpPerMinute(level) = XP_PER_MINUTE * (1 + REWARD_GROWTH * (level - 1))

The second rule says higher-level players earn more, because later enemies and quests pay more. When rewards grow by a fixed amount each level, the minutes per level grow roughly like level ^ (CURVE - 1):

  • CURVE = 1: waits level off. After a while every level takes about the same time.
  • CURVE = 1.5: waits keep stretching, but more slowly as the game goes on.
  • CURVE = 2 or more: waits grow in proportion to the level or faster, and the late game becomes a long wait.

Many games use an exponential table, where each level needs a fixed percentage more than the last. That always outruns rewards growing by a fixed amount, so the top levels tend to become very long waits, which is sometimes the point.

The shape across a whole game

Designers commonly think about the timeline in three parts:

  • A fast start. Several quick levels teach what a level-up gives while the player is still deciding whether to stay.
  • A stretching middle. Waits grow slowly so each level feels earned, and levels line up with content: if area 2 expects level 8, read off when the typical player gets there. Too early and the area is trivial; too late and the player must grind, repeating easy content only for XP.
  • The cap. The level cap is the last level. Reaching it long before the end leaves hours of XP that means nothing.

Starting points to tune

First level-up within one to three minutes. Three to five levels in the first half hour. In the middle game, at least one level per typical play session: if people play for 45 minutes at a time, a wait longer than 45 minutes means some sessions end with no level at all. Cap near the end of the main content. These are starting points from common practice, not rules.

Slow curves can be sold

When a game sells XP boosters or level skips, there is pressure to stretch the free curve so the purchase feels necessary. Season passes with paid tiers work the same way. That is artificial scarcity, and it works against the player. If you sell boosts, tune the curve for someone who never buys one.

When not to use a level curve

A short game, or one where the player rather than the character gets better, may not need levels. In competitive multiplayer, levels that add power let veterans flatten newcomers. And if every enemy scales to the player's level, level-ups bring no real gain and feel empty.

The code

The demo's model in LÖVE, with the same names and numbers. love.load prints the timeline before you play; then a simulated player earns XP at 50 game minutes per second and logs each level-up.

local BASE_XP = 90          -- XP to go from level 1 to level 2
local CURVE = 1.5           -- 1 = waits level off, 2 = waits keep growing
local XP_PER_MINUTE = 50    -- what a level 1 player earns
local REWARD_GROWTH = 0.10  -- each level earns 10% of that rate more
local LEVEL_CAP = 20
local SPEED = 50            -- game minutes per real second

local function xpToNext(level)
  return math.floor(BASE_XP * level ^ CURVE)
end

local function xpPerMinute(level)
  return XP_PER_MINUTE * (1 + REWARD_GROWTH * (level - 1))
end

-- Minutes from the start of the game to each level
local function timeline()
  local times = { 0 }
  for level = 1, LEVEL_CAP - 1 do
    times[level + 1] = times[level] + xpToNext(level) / xpPerMinute(level)
  end
  return times
end

local level, xp, minutes = 1, 0, 0
local log = {}

function love.load()
  for lv, t in ipairs(timeline()) do
    print(string.format("level %2d at %6.1f min", lv, t))
  end
end

function love.update(dt)
  if minutes >= 600 then return end   -- stop after ten hours
  local step = dt * SPEED
  minutes = minutes + step
  xp = xp + xpPerMinute(level) * step
  while level < LEVEL_CAP and xp >= xpToNext(level) do
    xp = xp - xpToNext(level)
    level = level + 1
    log[#log + 1] = string.format("level %d at %.0f min", level, minutes)
  end
  if level == LEVEL_CAP then xp = 0 end
end

function love.draw()
  love.graphics.print(string.format("Hour %.1f   Level %d", minutes / 60, level), 20, 20)
  if level < LEVEL_CAP then
    love.graphics.print("XP " .. math.floor(xp) .. " / " .. xpToNext(level), 20, 40)
  end
  for i, line in ipairs(log) do
    love.graphics.print(line, 20, 60 + i * 16)
  end
end

The printed timeline and the log should agree to within a minute. To try another formula, change only xpToNext and xpPerMinute.

Pitfalls

  • Judging the table by its totals. Total XP always looks steep. Convert to minutes per level before deciding anything.
  • Assuming one kind of player. Rushers earn less XP per minute than completionists. Run the timeline at half and double the rate and check both meet the content at a sensible level.
  • Forgetting that rewards grow. A curve tuned at a fixed XP rate plays much faster if later enemies pay more. Model the growth.
  • Storing levels instead of XP. Save total XP and derive the level, so you can retune the curve after release without breaking saves.
  • A cap with nothing after it. Move the cap, or turn extra XP into something else and say so on screen.

In real games

Old School RuneScape uses an exponential skill table: each level needs roughly 10 percent more experience than the one before. Level 99 needs 13,034,431 experience in total and level 92 needs 6,517,253, so the last seven levels take as much experience as everything before them. Players call 92 "halfway to 99". The curve makes the top levels a long-term goal rather than a steady rhythm.

Most Pokémon belong to one of a few experience groups. In the group called Medium Fast, the total experience to be at level n is n cubed, so level 100 needs 1,000,000. That is a total table: the experience for each single level grows roughly with the square of the level, while experience from battles grows with the level of the defeated opponent.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Take the level table from your game, or write one with the Lua above. Estimate XP per minute at three points: the start, the middle and the end. Make a column of minutes per level and a column of time reached. Mark the two or three places where your content expects a certain level. You are done when you can say the level a typical player has on arriving at each of those places, and the longest wait between two level-ups before the cap.

Takeaways

  • A level curve is a schedule of rewards. The player feels the minutes between level-ups, not the XP numbers.
  • Minutes per level is XP to the next level divided by XP per minute, and XP per minute usually grows too.
  • With rewards that grow by a fixed amount, a curve exponent of 1 levels the waits off, 1.5 stretches them gently and 2 or more makes them long.
  • Check the timeline against your content and your players' session length, and place the cap near the end of the content.
  • Stretching a free curve to sell boosters is a pressure tactic. Tune for the player who never pays.
