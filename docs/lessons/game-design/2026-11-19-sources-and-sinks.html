Sources and sinks: where game money comes from and where it goes

How currency enters and leaves a game economy, why prices climb when sources beat sinks, and how a sink that grows with wealth levels it off.

You return to an online game after a few months away. The sword that sold for 1,000 gold now sells for 7,500, veterans buy shop potions without looking at the price, and a friend who just started cannot afford anything worth having. Nobody raised a price on purpose. Gold went into the game faster than it came out.

Every coin has a way in and a way out

A source, also called a faucet, is any rule that creates currency from nothing: a monster drops gold, a quest pays a reward, a shopkeeper buys your loot. A sink, also called a drain, is any rule that destroys currency: a repair bill, a potion bought from a shopkeeper, a fee taken by the market.

Everything else only moves money around. When one player sells a sword to another, gold changes pockets but the total stays the same. That leaves one equation, at the bottom of the diagram.

What the player feels

Sources beat sinks. Gold piles up. Prices that players set, such as on a market, tend to follow how much gold is around, so they climb. This is inflation: the same gold buys less. Veterans feel rich, and fixed shop prices stop mattering to them, so those sinks stop working. A new player earns the same 60 gold an hour the veterans once did, but now faces prices set by people holding 30,000. They feel priced out.

Sinks beat sources. Gold drains to zero. Every repair bill feels like a fine for playing, nobody can save for a goal, and the economy feels like a grind. This is the opposite problem, and it is just as real.

Balanced. Wallets grow for a while and then level off. A sword that takes a new player about 30 hours of play to afford still takes about 30 hours months later. Goals stay goals.

Why fixed sinks cannot keep up

Sources usually scale with time played: more hours, more drops. Many sinks are flat amounts, like a repair bill of 40 gold an hour. If players earn 100 an hour and pay 40, they gain 60 every hour, forever. The wallet climbs in a straight line, and no flat bill bends it.

A proportional sink takes more as players hold more. A market fee is the classic one: rich players trade bigger amounts, so they pay bigger fees. Suppose players sell 10 percent of their wallet each hour and the market keeps 10 percent of each sale. That destroys 1 percent of the wallet every hour. The wallet stops growing where the two sides meet:

100 created = 40 repair + 1% of wallet, so the wallet levels off near 6,000.

(The demo takes the 1 percent after income and repair, so it settles at about 5,940.) Change any number and the wallet finds a new level instead of running away.

Starting points to tune

Write down gold created and gold destroyed per hour, once for a new player and once for a veteran. Give at least one sink that grows with wealth: a market fee of 5 to 10 percent is a reasonable place to start. Then pick one item and track how many hours a new player needs to afford it. If that number keeps rising over the life of your game, sources are winning. These are starting points, not rules.

Sinks players want, and sinks that are used against them

The kindest sinks are things players choose: cosmetics, housing, faster travel, a gamble on an upgrade. Sinks that feel like punishment, such as death penalties and repair bills, tend to make players avoid the activity that charges them. Flat bills also hit hardest at the bottom: 40 gold an hour is most of a new player's income and nothing to a veteran.

Be honest about the other use of sinks. In a game that sells its currency for real money, sinks can be tuned so players run short at the moment buying more is easiest. Draining a wallet on purpose to create that pressure is artificial scarcity. It works, and it works against the player.

When not to bother

In a single-player game without trading, you set the prices, so inflation cannot push them up; late-game gold piles mostly just make shops pointless. In a roguelike run that resets, the run's length bounds the wallet. Sources and sinks matter most where money lasts a long time and players set the prices.

Then switch to balanced, which only adds a 10 percent market fee, and watch both lines flatten. Try drained, then nudge the repair bill down until players stop being broke.

The code

The demo's model in LÖVE, with the same names and numbers. One game hour passes every 0.05 seconds. The marked lines are the source and the two sinks.

local INCOME = 100   -- source: gold from monsters per hour of play
local REPAIR = 40    -- fixed sink: repair bill per hour
local FEE = 0.10     -- proportional sink: market keeps 10% of each sale
local TRADED = 0.10  -- share of the wallet sold on the market each hour
local FLOOR = 1000   -- the sword never sells for less than this
local SHARE = 0.25   -- sellers ask a quarter of what players hold

-- One hour of play: returns the new wallet
local function playHour(wallet)
  local have = wallet + INCOME                 -- gold created
  local repair = math.min(REPAIR, have)        -- flat: same for rich and poor
  local fee = (have - repair) * TRADED * FEE   -- grows as the wallet grows
  return have - repair - fee
end

local function swordPrice(wallet)
  return math.max(FLOOR, wallet * SHARE)
end

-- Hours a new player, starting with 0 gold, needs to afford a price
local function hoursToAfford(price)
  local wallet, hours = 0, 0
  while wallet < price do
    wallet = playHour(wallet)
    hours = hours + 1
    if hours > 2000 then return nil end      -- never
  end
  return hours
end

local wallet, hour, timer = 0, 0, 0

function love.update(dt)
  timer = timer + dt
  while timer >= 0.05 and hour < 500 do
    timer = timer - 0.05
    wallet = playHour(wallet)
    hour = hour + 1
  end
end

function love.draw()
  local price = swordPrice(wallet)
  local wait = hoursToAfford(price)
  love.graphics.print("Hour " .. hour, 20, 20)
  love.graphics.print("Veteran holds: " .. math.floor(wallet), 20, 40)
  love.graphics.print("Sword price: " .. math.floor(price), 20, 60)
  love.graphics.print("New player needs: " .. (wait and (wait .. " h") or "never"), 20, 80)
end

Set FEE = 0 and the wallet grows by 60 every hour without end. A real economy has many sources and sinks, but each one fits one of these three lines: it adds a fixed amount, takes a fixed amount, or takes a share.

Pitfalls

  • Counting trades as sinks. Gold paid to another player is not destroyed. Only the fee on the trade is.
  • Forgetting hidden sources. Selling loot to a shopkeeper creates gold. So do duplication bugs, bots farming around the clock and generous refunds. One unchecked source can outpour every sink you designed.
  • Only flat sinks. They matter early and fade as players get rich, which is exactly when you need them most.
  • Balancing for the average player. Wealth is usually uneven, with a few players holding much of the gold. Check your numbers for a new player and for the richest players, not only the middle.
  • Sinks so strong that nobody can save. In the demo, income 100, repair 90 and a 10 percent fee levels the wallet just under 1,000, so the 1,000 gold sword is never affordable. A goal that the economy makes unreachable is worse than inflation.

In real games

EVE Online's developer, CCP Games, publishes a Monthly Economic Report that charts ISK, the game's currency, entering through faucets and leaving through sinks. Bounties paid by the game for destroying computer-controlled pirates have been among the large faucets.

World of Warcraft's auction house keeps a cut of each sale, commonly given as 5 percent at the faction auction houses. That cut is a sink that grows with what players trade, the same shape as the fee in the demo.

Takeaways

  • Sources create currency, sinks destroy it, and trades between players only move it.
  • Gold held changes by created minus destroyed. If that stays positive, prices set by players tend to climb.
  • Flat sinks fade as players get rich. A sink that grows with wealth, such as a market fee, lets the wallet level off.
  • Measure the hours a new player needs to afford a typical item. It should stay steady over the life of the game.
  • Sinks can be used to squeeze players toward paying. Prefer sinks they choose for themselves.
