Last-hit protection: the sliver that lasts

How making the last part of the health bar take more damage to empty turns near deaths into close escapes, how much to stretch it, and when not to.

Your health bar is a red sliver, you dodge two more shots, grab a pickup and limp out of the fight. It feels like the best moment of the session. Often that moment was not luck: the game quietly made the sliver harder to empty than it looked.

The bar is a promise, not a measurement

Players read a health bar as a measurement: if one hit took a tenth of the bar, the last tenth is one hit. But the designer draws the bar, and nothing forces the last part to behave like the first.

Last-hit protection is any hidden rule that makes the final portion of health last longer than it appears. The simplest version, the one in this lesson, gives the bottom of the bar a worth: while health is inside that bottom part, the sliver, each point takes more than one point of damage to remove.

The damage that lands above the sliver is applied normally. Only the part of a hit that reaches into the sliver is divided by the worth. So a hit of 12 that takes health from 30 to below 25 costs 5 normal points, then the other 7 become 3.5.

What the player feels

Without protection, a fight tends to end abruptly: the bar drops in even steps and the last step is death, before the player has reacted to the danger. With protection, the bar slows down right where the player starts paying attention, buying one or two extra hits of time to dodge, retreat or heal. They do not know why; they just remember surviving.

Tension and risk are different things. Tension comes from the bar looking nearly empty; protection lowers the real risk and leaves the look alone.

Starting points to tune

A sliver of the last 15 to 30 percent of the bar, with a worth of 1.5 to 2, is a gentle place to start. Above 3, players tend to notice that the bar has stopped moving. Pair it with a short invincibility after each hit, 0.3 to 0.6 seconds, so two shots in the same instant cannot skip the sliver. These are starting points, not rules.

Its cousins

The same idea comes in other forms. One-shot protection stops a single hit from taking a player from high health straight to zero; it leaves them at 1 instead. A soft first hit makes the first damage of a fight or a level smaller, so a player who has not yet learned an enemy is not punished at once. All of them move the moment of failure later, so the player has time to see it coming.

Try it

Survive a 30 second round with protection off, then play it again with protection on. Shots come from the edges with the same seeded timing every round, and a green cross heals 20. With "show the hidden rule" on, the bar marks the sliver and the real cost of the last hit is printed under it. An escape is counted when your health falls below 15 and you then heal back to 30 or last to the end. Then raise the worth to 4 and notice how the bar starts to look stuck.

The code

This is the demo's health code in LÖVE, with the same numbers. Moving the player, spawning shots and drawing are left out; call applyDamage when a shot touches the player and heal on a pickup. The marked lines are the protection.

local MAX_HP = 100
local SLIVER = 0.25       -- the last 25% of the bar is protected
local WORTH = 2           -- each point in the sliver costs 2 damage
local MERCY = 0.5         -- seconds of invincibility after a hit
local LOW, SAFE = 15, 30  -- near death below LOW, escaped at SAFE
local PROTECT = true      -- the demo's toggle

local player = { hp = MAX_HP, mercy = 0, low = false, dead = false }
local escapes = 0

local function applyDamage(dmg)
  if player.mercy > 0 or player.dead then return end
  player.mercy = MERCY
  if PROTECT then
    local above = math.max(0, player.hp - MAX_HP * SLIVER)
    local normal = math.min(dmg, above)
    player.hp = player.hp - normal - (dmg - normal) / WORTH
  else
    player.hp = player.hp - dmg
  end
  if player.hp < LOW then player.low = true end
  if player.hp <= 0 then player.hp = 0; player.dead = true end
end

local function heal(amount)
  player.hp = math.min(MAX_HP, player.hp + amount)
  if player.low and player.hp >= SAFE then
    escapes = escapes + 1  -- a close escape
    player.low = false
  end
end

function love.update(dt)
  player.mercy = math.max(0, player.mercy - dt)
end

local function displayedHealth()
  return math.ceil(player.hp)  -- what the bar and any number show
end

The whole technique is the marked line: split the hit at the edge of the sliver, and divide only the part below it. At the end of a round the demo also counts a survivor who is still below 15 as an escape.

Pitfalls

  • Showing exact numbers. If the interface prints "12 damage" and the bar drops by 6, players notice. Hide the numbers, or show the damage dealt to the bar rather than the damage rolled.
  • Hits that skip the sliver. Without invincibility after a hit, two shots on the same frame can carry the player through the sliver before it does anything. Keep a short mercy window, or cap how far one hit can go into the sliver.
  • Making death impossible. A worth so high that the sliver never empties removes the threat that made the escape exciting. Once players notice, they get reckless and the tension is gone for good.
  • Stacking every mercy rule. Protection, softer enemies, extra drops and invincibility on top of each other can flatten a fight.

When not to use it. In competitive multiplayer, hidden help for one player is hidden harm for another, and it must be the same for everyone or not exist. In tactics games and other games where players do the arithmetic, health is information, and lying about it breaks their plans.

Is this manipulation?

It is a lie told in the player's favour, to give them a story they enjoy. The same trick turns ugly when it is used against them, for example a game that rigs near misses to keep someone spending. Ask whose interest the hidden rule serves. If the honest answer is "ours, not theirs", do not ship it.

In real games

In September 2017, designer Jennifer Scheurle asked developers on Twitter to share hidden mechanics, and the thread was widely reported. One reply said that Assassin's Creed and Doom count the last bit of the health bar as more hit points than the rest, to give the feeling of only just surviving. I know this from a summary of a PC Gamer article about the thread, which I could not open in this session, so treat the details, including which entries in those series, as unconfirmed.

Some games make the same kind of rule visible instead. In Pokémon, the Focus Sash is a held item that leaves a Pokémon at 1 HP when an attack would knock it out from full health, a plain form of one-shot protection. In Hades, Death Defiance brings the player back with some health instead of ending the run. Both are told to the player and still produce close escapes.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Find the place in your game where the player takes damage, and route it through an applyDamage function like the one above. Add a debug key that turns protection on and off and prints the real damage of each hit. Play the hardest fight five times each way and count how often you survive with less than 15 percent health. You are done when you have a sliver size and worth where close escapes clearly happen more often, but you can still die.

Takeaways

  • The health bar is drawn by the designer; its last part does not have to be worth the same as its first.
  • Split each hit at the edge of the sliver and divide only the part below it by the worth.
  • Tension comes from how low the bar looks; protection lowers the real risk and leaves the look alone.
  • Start around the last 25 percent at worth 2, with a short invincibility after each hit, and keep death possible.
  • Leave it out of competitive play and number-driven games, and never point a hidden rule against the player.
