The flow channel and difficulty curves

Why challenge has to rise with the player's skill to avoid boredom and anxiety, and why a sawtooth difficulty curve tends to beat a straight ramp.

Your playtester breezes through the first five waves, checks their phone during the sixth, and quits in frustration at the ninth. The game did not change genre halfway. Its difficulty simply climbed at a different speed from the player's skill, first too slowly and then too fast.

Challenge against skill

Flow is the psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's name for the state of being fully absorbed in an activity. One of the conditions he describes is a balance: the task is hard enough to need your full attention, but not so hard that you feel you cannot cope.

Put that on a chart. Across the bottom is the player's skill, how well they can currently play. Up the side is challenge, how much the game is asking at the moment. Where challenge is far above skill, players tend to feel anxiety: stress, confusion, "this is unfair". Where skill is far above challenge, they tend to feel boredom. The band between them, along the diagonal, is the flow channel.

Why the channel matters to you

Skill is not fixed. Every minute of play moves the player to the right. If the challenge stays put, they slide into boredom. So difficulty has to rise over time just to stay in the same place, and your difficulty curve, challenge plotted against time or level, is your plan for keeping up.

The catch is that you do not know how fast any one player learns. A straight ramp tuned for you is a little too steep for a slower learner and a little too gentle for a faster one. Over ten waves, that small mismatch adds up and the player drifts out of the band.

Three shapes

  • Linear. Challenge rises by the same amount every wave. Simple, but it never lets go, and its slope suits only one kind of player.
  • Stepped. Challenge holds for a few waves, then jumps. The plateaus tend to drift toward boredom as the player learns, and each jump arrives as a shock.
  • Sawtooth. Challenge climbs for a few waves, drops back to a little below the last peak, then climbs again to a new high.

The sawtooth tends to work better for three reasons. The drop is a rest, which ties straight back to yesterday's lesson on pacing. The easier wave after a hard one lets players feel that they have improved: the wave that was a struggle a minute ago is now comfortable. And because it covers a range of challenge instead of one exact value, more players find some waves inside their own channel.

Jesse Schell discusses the flow channel in The Art of Game Design and makes a similar case: a path that alternates tension with easier stretches tends to hold interest longer than a steady climb.

Starting points, not rules

For a short wave-based game, try groups of three waves, each wave about 15 percent harder than the last, with the first wave of the next group set at the middle wave of the previous group. Make the final wave a clear new peak. Then playtest with someone better and someone worse than you, and tune.

Then raise the "Difficulty rise" slider and see which curve throws you into the anxiety corner first.

The code

The curves are tables of ten challenge values from 0 to 1, the same numbers as the demo. waveSettings turns a challenge value into rocks per second and how long a rock takes to cross the screen. endWave is the demo's crude skill estimate and the test for which zone the player is in.

-- Challenge from 0 to 1 for each of ten waves
local CURVES = {
  linear   = { 0.10, 0.18, 0.26, 0.34, 0.42, 0.50, 0.58, 0.66, 0.74, 0.82 },
  stepped  = { 0.10, 0.10, 0.10, 0.35, 0.35, 0.35, 0.60, 0.60, 0.60, 0.85 },
  sawtooth = { 0.10, 0.25, 0.40, 0.25, 0.40, 0.55, 0.40, 0.55, 0.70, 0.85 },
}
local RISE = 1.0      -- the "Difficulty rise" slider: scales how far each curve climbs
local CHANNEL = 0.15  -- half-width of the flow channel

local function challengeFor(curve, wave)
  local c = 0.1 + RISE * (curve[wave] - 0.1)
  return math.max(0, math.min(1, c))
end

-- What a challenge value means in this game
local function waveSettings(c)
  return { rocksPerSecond = 0.8 + 3.2 * c, crossTime = 2.6 - 1.4 * c }
end

-- Playtest tool: rough skill estimate from the hits taken in a wave
local skill = nil
local function endWave(c, hits)
  local guess = c + 0.2 - 0.1 * hits
  if skill == nil then
    skill = guess
  else
    skill = 0.5 * skill + 0.5 * guess   -- smooth out one lucky or unlucky wave
  end
  skill = math.max(0, math.min(1, skill))
  if c - skill > CHANNEL then return "anxiety" end
  if skill - c > CHANNEL then return "boredom" end
  return "flow"
end

At the start of wave n, call waveSettings(challengeFor(CURVES.sawtooth, n)) and feed the result to your spawner. Log the result of endWave during playtests and look for long runs of the same word outside "flow".

Pitfalls

  • Tuning for yourself. You know every pattern, so you sit far to the right of a new player. A curve that feels right to you is commonly too steep for everyone else. Watch fresh players.
  • Measuring only one thing. Hits taken is a crude stand-in for skill. A player who survives by hiding in a corner looks skilled and may be bored stiff.
  • Rests that drop too far. If the rest wave goes back to the very start, players feel progress was undone. Drop to around the middle of the last climb.
  • Treating the channel as a law. Flow is a useful model, not a measurement. Players differ, moods differ, and a short spike into anxiety can be the most memorable moment in a game.

When not to use it

Games about relaxing, such as cosy farming or creative tools, often aim below the channel on purpose. Some games are built around being punishing, and their players choose that. In competitive multiplayer the challenge comes from opponents, so matchmaking does the job a curve does here.

In real games

In many versions of Tetris, pieces fall faster at each new level, often every ten cleared lines: a stepped curve, with each speed held long enough to adjust before the next jump.

Jenova Chen built flow theory into his 2006 master's thesis, Flow in Games, and into flOw, made with Nicholas Clark. In flOw the player can swim deeper into harder layers or rise back to easier ones, so they choose their own place on the challenge axis.

Takeaways

  • Plot challenge against skill. Too much challenge tends to cause anxiety, too little boredom. The band between them is the flow channel.
  • Skill grows with play, so challenge has to rise just to keep the player in the channel.
  • A straight ramp only fits players who learn at one particular speed. Small errors in slope add up over many waves.
  • A sawtooth, climb then drop to below the last peak then climb higher, gives rests, lets players feel their improvement, and covers a range of skill.
  • Treat the model as a guide for playtesting, not as a measurement.
