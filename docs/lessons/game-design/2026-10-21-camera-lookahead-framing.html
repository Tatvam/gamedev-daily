Camera lookahead and platform snapping

How shifting the camera ahead of the player and moving it vertically only on landing shows players where they are going instead of where they have been.

You sprint right and a spike pit appears at the edge of the screen with no time to react. Half the picture was showing ground you had already crossed. Then you hop up a staircase and the whole level lurches up and down with every jump.

Where the camera points is a design decision

The last lesson's camera still aimed at the player, so half the screen showed the past. A player running right needs to see what is to the right; the ground behind them rarely matters.

This lesson adds two ideas to the follow camera. Lookahead moves the camera's aim point ahead of the player in the direction they face. Platform snapping changes the camera's height only when the player lands, so jumps do not move the screen.

Lookahead in three steps

First, decide which way is "ahead". The simplest choice is the direction the player last pressed, which this lesson calls facing. Velocity also works, but it drops to zero at every pause and pulls the camera back.

Second, compute a look target: facing * LOOK_DIST, for example 100 pixels to the right. The camera aims at the player's position plus this offset.

Third, and this is the step people skip, ease the offset itself. If the offset flips from +100 to −100 the moment the player taps left, the camera swings 200 pixels for a two-frame correction. Easing the offset with its own slow half-life (the time to cover half the remaining distance) means a short tap barely moves it, while a real change of direction swings it across in under a second.

local k = 1 - 2 ^ (-dt / LOOK_HALF_LIFE)
cam.look = cam.look + (facing * LOOK_DIST - cam.look) * k

Lookahead also cancels the lag that smoothing causes. A smoothed camera trails a running player by about speed × half-life ÷ 0.69: 38 pixels at the demo's 220 pixels per second and 120 ms half-life. Without lookahead that lag means the player sees less ahead than behind. With a 100 pixel lookahead the net view ahead is about 60 pixels more than centred.

Platform snapping

Vertically the question is different. Players need a steady horizon to judge jumps, so the camera remembers the height of the last surface the player stood on, the ground line, and aims at that instead of at the player.

  • While grounded, the ground line is the player's height.
  • While in the air, it does not change, so a jump does not move the screen.
  • If the player falls below the ground line, follow them down at once. Falls are where the player most needs to see what is below.
  • If a very high jump carries the player more than a margin above the ground line, follow them up by the amount past the margin, so they never leave the screen.

Ease the vertical move too, so the view glides up after a landing.

Starting points, not rules

Lookahead distance: about 15 to 25 percent of the view width (the demo uses 100 pixels and limits it to 30 percent of the width on narrow screens). Look half-life: 300 to 600 ms. Vertical half-life: 100 to 200 ms. Air margin: a little more than a normal jump height (the demo's jump is 122 pixels and its margin 130).

When not to use it

Lookahead assumes danger comes from the front. In a brawler or an arena game where enemies surround the player and they turn constantly, a swinging camera tends to be tiring and hides threats behind them; keep the player centred or frame the group of fighters instead. Platform snapping is wrong for levels built around vertical movement, such as tower climbs, swimming or flying, where height changes all the time and there is no ground to snap to. And in a single-screen game neither applies.

Try it

The autopilot runs a level with steps and quick turn-arounds. Start with both toggles off: the player trails behind centre while running, and the screen bobs with every hop. Switch on Lookahead and watch the bar at the top fill up on the side the player is heading. Then switch on Platform snap and watch the camera line in the graph go flat during jumps and step only on landings.

Then drag Look swing half-life to 50 ms and watch short taps swing the level.

The code

One function with the demo's names and numbers. It takes the player's centre, whether they are grounded, and their facing (1 or −1).

local LOOK_DIST = 100        -- pixels shown ahead of the player
local LOOK_HALF_LIFE = 0.4   -- seconds for the offset to swing half way
local CAM_HALF_LIFE = 0.12   -- horizontal smoothing (last lesson)
local Y_HALF_LIFE = 0.15     -- vertical smoothing
local AIR_MARGIN = 130       -- follow up only past this height
local cam = { x = 0, y = 0, look = 0, groundY = 0 }

local function ease(dt, halfLife)
  return 1 - 2 ^ (-dt / halfLife)
end

-- p.x, p.y: player centre (y grows downwards)
local function updateCamera(dt, p, viewW)
  -- 1. lookahead: ease the offset towards the facing side
  local dist = math.min(LOOK_DIST, viewW * 0.3)
  cam.look = cam.look + (p.facing * dist - cam.look) * ease(dt, LOOK_HALF_LIFE)
  local tx = p.x + cam.look

  -- 2. platform snap: aim at the last ground height
  if p.grounded then cam.groundY = p.y end
  local ty = cam.groundY
  if p.y > cam.groundY then
    ty = p.y                             -- falling below: follow
  elseif p.y < cam.groundY - AIR_MARGIN then
    ty = p.y + AIR_MARGIN                -- very high: keep on screen
  end

  cam.x = cam.x + (tx - cam.x) * ease(dt, CAM_HALF_LIFE)
  cam.y = cam.y + (ty - cam.y) * ease(dt, Y_HALF_LIFE)
end

function love.update(dt)
  updatePlayer(dt)     -- your movement code sets player.facing
  updateCamera(dt, player, love.graphics.getWidth())
end

Draw with the same love.graphics.translate offset as in the last lesson. If you keep a dead zone, apply it to the aim point, not the raw player position.

Pitfalls

  • An unsmoothed offset. Flipping the offset instantly on every turn makes the screen whip back and forth. Ease it, and slower than the camera itself.
  • Losing the player on small screens. Lookahead plus lag plus any dead zone must leave the player well inside the view. Limit the distance to a fraction of the view width, as the code does.
  • Snapping to thin air. Update the ground line only when the player is really standing, not on the first frame of a ledge grab or while sliding down a wall, or the view jumps to odd heights.
  • Hiding the fall. If the camera stays on the ground line while the player drops into a pit, they fall blind. Always follow downward at once.
  • Easing after a respawn. Set cam.look and cam.groundY directly instead of gliding across the level.

In real games

Showing more of the level in the direction of travel, and holding the vertical view steady until a landing, are both common in 2D platformers. Itay Keren's essay Scroll Back, adapted from his GDC 2015 Independent Games Summit talk, catalogues many variations of both ideas across side-scrolling games.

Unity's Cinemachine package has a lookahead setting on its 2D follow camera that predicts where the target is heading and leads it. Setting names have changed between versions, so check the documentation for yours.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Add cam.look and updateCamera to your camera. Start with LOOK_DIST at a fifth of your view width. Run at top speed and check that more of the screen lies ahead than behind. Then tap the opposite direction for a moment and raise LOOK_HALF_LIFE until the tap barely moves the view. Finally, hop up a staircase and confirm the camera only rises after each landing. You are done when running, turning and hopping all leave the screen calm and the road ahead visible.

Takeaways

  • A centred camera spends half the screen on ground the player has already crossed.
  • Lookahead aims the camera ahead of the facing direction. Ease the offset slowly so short taps do not swing the view.
  • Lookahead also cancels the lag a smoothed camera adds while running.
  • Platform snapping keeps the camera at the last ground height during jumps, follows falls immediately and follows very high jumps past a margin.
  • Skip both where danger comes from every side or where vertical movement is constant.
