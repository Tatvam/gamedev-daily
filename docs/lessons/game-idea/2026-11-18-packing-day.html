Packing Day: last in, first out

A moving-van puzzle where boxes fall into place, fragile items need nothing on top, and each room's things must come out first.

Eight things wait on the pavement and the van is barely big enough. Fitting them in is only half the job: at the new house the kitchen gets unpacked first, so the fridge had better not be stuck behind the bed, under the wardrobe.

The core loop: pick, turn, drop, then unload

Packing Day is a calm spatial puzzle. You see the van from the side. Its cab is on the left and its open back door is on the right. Each item is a shape made of square cells, like a furniture silhouette, and it belongs to one of three rooms, numbered in the order the new house needs them: 1 kitchen, 2 bedroom, 3 study.

You pick an item, turn it, choose a column and let go. The item falls straight down until it lands on the floor or on something else. That is gravity placement: you choose where an item goes across, but never how high it sits. Gaps under an overhang stay empty for good.

Every ten seconds the player drops one item. Every minute the van is full and unloads. Every session is a street of houses, each with a new van and new things.

The rules

  • Everything goes in. All eight items must be in the van before you can press Done.
  • Items fall. A dropped item slides straight down until it rests on something. Nothing may stick out of the roof.
  • Fragile means nothing on top. Items marked with a glass, the plates and the lamp, must have no cell of another item directly above them.
  • Rooms come out in order. At the new house the movers empty the kitchen items first, then the bedroom, then the study. An item can come out only when nothing rests on it and nothing sits between it and the door in its rows.
  • No timer. The rules are checked only when you press Done.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the friend who is unusually good at loading vans, who thinks about the far end of the trip. The constraint is that two goals pull against each other. Fitting wants big items on the floor and small ones in the gaps. Unloading wants the first room's things near the door and on top, and fragile things at the very top, where they waste space.

This is a stack puzzle in disguise: the last thing in is the first thing out. Players feel it without the word. After one failed unload they start loading backwards, study first, deep against the cab, and the kitchen last, by the door.

Gravity makes turning matter: an L-shaped desk dropped the wrong way round leaves a hole nothing can fill.

The hardest part to build is not the rules but the levels: many sets of items have no answer, so each van needs a solver that proves one exists.

Try it

Load all eight items, then press Done and watch the movers unload. If something is stuck, the van outlines what blocked it and you keep packing from where you were.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: the check that runs when the player presses Done. The van is a grid of cells, 8 wide and 4 tall, with row 1 at the roof and the door past column 8. Each placed item keeps a list of the cells it fills, in van coordinates. Each item also has room, fragile and inVan. Dropping and drawing are left out. The check empties the grid as it goes, so run it on a copy.

local W, H, grid = 8, 4, {}      -- grid[y][x] = the item in that cell, or nil
for y = 1, H do grid[y] = {} end
local function blocker(item)     -- what stops this item leaving now, or nil
  for _, c in ipairs(item.cells) do
    local x, y = c[1], c[2]
    local above = y > 1 and grid[y - 1][x]
    if above and above ~= item then return above end   -- resting on it
    for x2 = x + 1, W do                                 -- the way to the door
      local other = grid[y][x2]
      if other and other ~= item then return other end
    end
  end
  return nil
end
-- Fragile first, then rooms 1, 2, 3. Returns true, or the stuck item and its blocker.
local function unload(items)
  for _, it in ipairs(items) do
    if it.fragile then
      for _, c in ipairs(it.cells) do
        local above = c[2] > 1 and grid[c[2] - 1][c[1]]
        if above and above ~= it then return false, it, above end
      end
    end
  end
  for room = 1, 3 do
    repeat                           -- keep taking out whatever is free
      local moved = false
      for _, it in ipairs(items) do
        if it.room == room and it.inVan and not blocker(it) then
          for _, c in ipairs(it.cells) do grid[c[2]][c[1]] = nil end
          it.inVan, moved = false, true
        end
      end
    until not moved
    for _, it in ipairs(items) do
      if it.room == room and it.inVan then return false, it, blocker(it) end
    end
  end
  return true
end

The highlighted lines are the whole unloading rule: an item is free when nothing rests on any of its cells and no other item sits to the right of any of its cells. The repeat loop matters because items of the same room can block each other: the fridge may only come free once the table in front of it is out. It ends because every pass either removes an item or stops, and Lua lets until see the local moved declared inside the loop. The demo also follows a chain of same-room blockers to name the real culprit.

The drop itself is the familiar falling-block test: start the item just above the roof, move it down one row while every cell below it is empty and inside the van, and refuse the drop if any cell is still above row 1.

Pitfalls

  • Vans with no answer. Most random sets of items cannot be packed under these rules. I checked the demo's van with a brute-force search over every drop order: it has many solutions in an 8 by 4 van, but none at all in a 6 by 5 van with the same items, even though 30 cells are enough to hold their 29. Run a solver for every level you ship.
  • The verdict comes too late. Learning at the end that the first drop was wrong can feel unfair. Test it: count how often players restart from scratch after a failed unload. If most do, try a live hint that dims items already stuck.
  • Too much room. In a 7 by 5 van the same items have thousands of valid packings and the puzzle goes slack. Spare space is the difficulty dial.
  • Unreadable blockers. If testers cannot say why the fridge was stuck, animate the unloading, stop on the stuck item and outline what is in its way.

In real games

Packing shapes into a small space is well-explored puzzle territory, from falling-block games like Tetris, where pieces drop and turn as they fall, to games about packing a bag or a room. Tetris has no notion of what comes out later; its rows clear themselves.

What Packing Day adds is the second half of the trip, borrowed from ordinary logistics: loading a vehicle so the first stop's goods are nearest the door. The bet is that "pack it so it unpacks well" can carry a whole puzzle game.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the van grid, gravity drops with rotation, undo and the Lua check above. Draw items as coloured cells with a room number, and just print which item was stuck and why.

Give it to a friend with the five rules and the demo's eight items. The one question: after their first failed unload, do they start loading in reverse order, last room first? If they do, the game teaches its own strategy and has room to grow. If they keep retrying the same order, the failure message is not showing them why.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: one van, eight items, gravity drops, rotation, undo, and the animated unloading that stops on the stuck item.
  • One-month version: twenty vans, each proven solvable, with new item rules added one at a time, such as heavy items that cannot rest on boxes.
  • Cut first: the full town map and story of the move. One van and a stream of houses is the game.
  • Hardest part: the level solver. Brute force works for eight items in a small van; larger vans need pruning, such as refusing any drop that already blocks an earlier room.
