Rope Bridge in the Wind: build with something that only pulls

A bridge building game where your only material is rope, which pulls but never pushes, with a playable prototype and the rope physics in Lua.

A traveller waits at the edge of a gorge, and all you have is a coil of rope. Tie the sagging walkway up to the rocks so they can cross, then watch the wind come up from the river and lift your bridge off the ropes that were holding it.

The core loop: tie, test, see which ropes went slack

Rope Bridge in the Wind is a build-then-test game: you design a structure while nothing threatens it, then press a button and watch it face the real load. The twist is the material. A rope is a one-sided constraint: it stops two points moving further apart than its length, but does nothing when they move closer. Rope pulls. It never pushes.

That one rule decides every design. A rope tied to a rock above the deck holds it up against gravity. Wind blowing upward lifts the deck, the rope goes slack, and the deck rises as if that rope were not there. To hold the deck down you need a second rope, tied to something below.

The rules

  • The deck. Six planks hang between two cliff edges, joined at five knots. The deck is rope too, and a little longer than the gap, so on its own it sags well below the edges.
  • Tying. Pick a peg on the rocks and a knot on the deck. The rope is cut to hold that knot exactly at deck level, so a rope from above lifts a sagging knot, and a rope from below stays slack until something pushes the knot up.
  • The safe band. A shaded strip around deck level. During a test, if any knot stays outside it for more than 0.4 seconds, the bridge bucks and throws the traveller into the river.
  • The test. A traveller walks across, pressing down on the planks under them. Two updrafts and one downdraft hit the bridge at the same moments every time.
  • Score. You have 600 units of rope. A design that crosses is scored by the rope it used, and less is better.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the patient rigger who reads where the forces will come from. The constraint is the material: every rope solves one direction of trouble, so a design that looks finished in calm air can be half a bridge. The wind turns the player's own ropes against their expectations, and the slack line shows them why.

The hardest part to build is tuning, not code. Whether a level is fair depends on numbers like deck slack and gust strength, so write a script early that tries every combination of ropes on a level and prints which ones cross. For this prototype it tried every design of up to four ropes that fits the budget. With no rope the walker falls in under a second. No design that uses only the high pegs crosses: hangers alone survive calm air and fail at the first updraft. The cheapest designs that cross use two ropes, about 330 units: one hanger and one stay.

Try it

Press Test with no ropes to see the sag. Then tie peg A to knot 3 and test again: the walker gets further, until the first updraft lifts the deck and the rope goes loose. Add a rope from the river rock E and watch which rope tightens in each gust.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE. Knots and pegs are points; pegs have pinned = true. Each knot keeps its last position in px and py: in Verlet integration a point moves as far as it moved last step, plus its forces. One link function serves planks and ropes, because both are rope.

local G, STEP, DAMP, ITER = 600, 1 / 60, 0.99, 16
local DECK_Y, PLANK = 190, 64.6               -- plank is 2% longer than its gap
local UP, DOWN, WOBBLE = 22, 30, 0.4          -- safe band and grace time
local knots, ropes, acc, wobble, testTime = {}, {}, 0, 0, 0 -- yours: fill knots, ropes
local function gustLift(t) return 0 end       -- yours: updraft at time t
local function thrown() end                   -- yours: during a test, the walker falls

local function link(a, b, rest)               -- pulls when stretched, never pushes
  local dx, dy = b.x - a.x, b.y - a.y
  local d = math.sqrt(dx * dx + dy * dy)
  if d <= rest then return end              -- slack: do nothing at all
  local wa = a.pinned and 0 or (b.pinned and 1 or 0.5)
  local wb = b.pinned and 0 or (a.pinned and 1 or 0.5)
  local k = (d - rest) / d
  a.x, a.y = a.x + dx * k * wa, a.y + dy * k * wa
  b.x, b.y = b.x - dx * k * wb, b.y - dy * k * wb
end

local function step(t)
  local out = false
  for _, p in ipairs(knots) do                -- p.load is the walker's weight here
    if not p.pinned then
      local vx, vy = (p.x - p.px) * DAMP, (p.y - p.py) * DAMP
      p.px, p.py = p.x, p.y
      p.x, p.y = p.x + vx, p.y + vy + (G + p.load - gustLift(t)) * STEP * STEP
    end
  end
  for _ = 1, ITER do
    for _, r in ipairs(ropes) do link(r.peg, r.knot, r.rest) end
    for i = 1, #knots - 1 do link(knots[i], knots[i + 1], PLANK) end
  end
  for _, p in ipairs(knots) do
    if p.y - DECK_Y > DOWN or DECK_Y - p.y > UP then out = true end
  end
  wobble = out and wobble + STEP or math.max(0, wobble - STEP)
  if wobble > WOBBLE then thrown() end
end

function love.update(dt)                      -- fixed steps: every test is the same
  acc = acc + dt
  while acc >= STEP do step(testTime); testTime, acc = testTime + STEP, acc - STEP end
end

The highlighted line is the whole game. Remove it and rope pushes too, so a single hanger becomes a rigid strut and the wind stops mattering. To tie a rope, set rest to the distance from the peg to the knot's place at deck level, (homeX, DECK_Y). Reset testTime to 0 when the player presses Test.

Pitfalls

  • The test feels random. If gusts change from run to run, a player cannot tell whether a change helped. Fix the gust timing per level and run the physics on a fixed step. Test early: does an unchanged design give the same result five times in a row?
  • Invisible slack. The lesson of each failure is "this rope went loose". If slack and taut ropes look the same, players blame the physics. Check that a tester can point to the rope that failed.
  • One magic design. If only one combination crosses, the game becomes guessing. The search script shows how many designs work; keep it in the tens or hundreds, and use the rope score to separate good from great.
  • Jitter. Two tight ropes pulling one knot to different places fight, and the knot shakes. Measuring every rope to the same deck-level spot keeps them in agreement.

In real games

Bridge building under a physics test is an established genre: build from a limited budget, then a vehicle or walker crosses and the simulation decides whether it holds. Those games usually offer several materials, some stiff enough to push.

Rope Bridge in the Wind narrows that to one material that only pulls, and adds a load that changes direction. The rule comes from real engineering: cables work only in tension, so cable structures often need extra cables to stop them lifting or swaying in the wind. Here the player discovers that for themselves.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with five knots, two pegs above and one below, a key to tie a hard-coded rope, and a key to start the test. Draw the safe band and the ropes, straight when taut and dashed when slack. Add one updraft at two seconds.

Hand it to a friend with only "make the bridge hold, rope from a peg to a knot". The one question to answer: after their first bridge fails in the updraft, does their next attempt add a rope from below? If it does, the slack rope taught them the rule. If they keep adding ropes from above, the slack is not visible enough yet.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: the prototype with eight gorges of different widths and peg layouts, each checked by the search script, and a rope target per gorge.
  • One-month version: ropes that snap when stretched too hard, travellers of different weights, gusts that push sideways as well as up, a replay with a slow-motion view, and a shared best score per gorge.
  • Cut first: rope snapping. It adds a second way to fail before players have learned the first, and the tension is hard to show clearly.
  • "Rope pulls, never pushes" is the design. Every feature should give the player another reason to ask which side a force comes from.
