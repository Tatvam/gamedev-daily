Heartbeat Runner: your pulse is the metronome

A rhythm runner where every step must land on your own heartbeat, and sprinting makes that beat faster. Playable prototype and Lua code.

In most rhythm games the song sets the tempo and you keep up. In Heartbeat Runner the tempo is your hero's heart: every stride must land on a beat, and every sprint makes the next beats come faster and the timing tighter, until you either reach a bench to catch your breath or collapse on the track.

The core loop: step, push, breathe

You see a runner from the side on a 160 metre course. A heart at the top of the screen beats, and a strip below it shows the next few beats sliding toward a line, like a heart monitor running in reverse. The runner only moves when you press a step key close to a beat. The allowed distance from the beat is the timing window.

Each step is a choice between two keys. A walk step moves you one metre and lets your heart settle by one beat per minute. A sprint step moves you 2.2 metres but raises your heart rate by five. Because a faster heart means more beats per second, sprinting pays twice: longer strides and more of them. That is the bait.

Every ten seconds you are tapping along with your pulse and deciding, step by step, whether to push. Every minute you finish a course, usually in two or three bursts between benches. Every session is a training plan: a string of courses with different bench placements, where your best times are the score.

The rules

  • Step on the beat. A press counts if it is within the timing window of the nearest heartbeat. Each beat can carry only one step.
  • The window is a share of the beat. It is 22 percent of the time between beats, never less than 70 milliseconds. At a resting 72 beats per minute that is about 180 milliseconds either side; at 180 it is about 73.
  • Missing hurts. A press off the beat, or a second press on the same beat, is a stumble: no stride, and a fright that adds three beats per minute. Mashing the keys makes things worse.
  • Standing still calms you. After 0.6 seconds without a step your heart slows by 3 beats per minute each second, or by 15 on a bench.
  • At 190 you collapse. You lie on the track for 2.5 seconds while your heart falls back toward 140. You lose time, never the run.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the athlete who knows their own body: pushing right to the edge and pulling back at the last moment. The constraint is that the tempo is not a song chosen by the designer but a number the player raises and lowers. Your heart rate is your speed, your difficulty and your stamina bar at the same time.

That makes the risk visible and felt. You do not read that you are tired; you hear the beat speed up and feel the window shrink under your fingers. Benches become places you plan for, and the question every few seconds is "one more sprint step, or walk now?"

The hardest part to build is input timing that feels fair. Small delays in reading keys and drawing frames add up, and at 180 beats per minute a 50 millisecond error is most of the window. Budget time for a calibration screen.

Try it

Find the beat by walking for a few steps, then sprint and watch the strip squeeze together as your heart speeds up. Try to reach a bench before you hit 190, stand still on it, and then go again. Finish the 160 metres as fast as you can.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE. The heart is a counter, phase, that grows by one for every beat. A press is judged against the nearest whole number. Drawing is left out. The names and numbers are the same as in the demo.

local REST_BPM, COLLAPSE_BPM = 72, 190
local bpm, phase, distance = REST_BPM, 0.3, 0
local lastBeat, idle, down = -1, 0, 0
local RESTS = { {55, 63}, {105, 113} }     -- benches, in metres
local function inRest(d)
  for _, r in ipairs(RESTS) do
    if d >= r[1] and d <= r[2] then return true end
  end
  return false
end
function love.update(dt)
  phase = phase + dt * bpm / 60            -- the heart is the metronome
  idle = idle + dt
  if down > 0 then                         -- collapsed: lie still, heart eases to 140
    down = down - dt
    bpm = math.max(140, bpm - 20 * dt)
  elseif idle > 0.6 then                   -- standing still calms you, benches faster
    bpm = math.max(REST_BPM, bpm - (inRest(distance) and 15 or 3) * dt)
  end
end
local function step(sprint)
  if down > 0 then return end
  local beat = math.floor(phase + 0.5)     -- the nearest heartbeat
  local err = (phase - beat) * 60 / bpm    -- seconds early (-) or late (+)
  local window = math.max(0.07, 0.22 * 60 / bpm)
  if math.abs(err) > window or beat == lastBeat then
    bpm = bpm + 3                          -- stumble: no stride, and a fright
  elseif sprint then
    lastBeat, idle = beat, 0
    distance, bpm = distance + 2.2, bpm + 5
  else
    lastBeat, idle = beat, 0
    distance, bpm = distance + 1, math.max(REST_BPM, bpm - 1)
  end
  if bpm >= COLLAPSE_BPM then down = 2.5 end
end
function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "space" or key == "z" then step(false) elseif key == "x" then step(true) end
end

The highlighted lines are the whole rhythm judge. Because phase counts beats rather than seconds, changing bpm never makes the heart skip or jump: the next beat simply arrives sooner. Dividing by bpm / 60 turns the distance from the beat back into seconds, so the window can be compared in real time.

Two details in the demo are worth copying. It shows several future beats on the strip, predicted at the current heart rate, so the player can see the tempo change before they feel it. And it judges a key press at the moment the key went down, not at the start of the frame, which removes up to one frame of error.

Pitfalls

  • Sprinting is always right, or never. If the time saved by sprinting is more than the time lost resting, players sprint into every bench; if less, they walk everywhere and the heart never matters. Test it: time an all-walk run and a well-paced run. In this prototype walking the whole course takes over two minutes and a paced run under one. Keep the gap large but make collapsing cost real time.
  • Unfair timing. Audio and display delay vary by device. Without a way to shift the beat earlier or later, a good player can miss everything at high tempo. Watch whether testers report "early" or "late" much more often than the other; a strong lean means your offset is wrong, not their hands.
  • A silent heart. The prototype is visual only. Rhythm is far easier to follow by ear, so the real game needs a heartbeat sound, which the reader must switch on themselves on the web. Also test with the sound off to keep it playable for deaf players.
  • Mashing. If a missed press costs nothing, players hammer the key. The stumble penalty fixes it here; check that it stays harsh enough when the window is wide.
  • Boredom at low tempo. At 72 beats per minute a walk is slow. If testers sit through long walks, place benches so walks are short.

In real games

Moving to a beat is well explored. Crypt of the NecroDancer is a dungeon game where every move must land on the beat of the music, and the Rhythm Heaven series is built from short timing games. Running by pressing buttons goes back at least to Konami's arcade Track & Field, where you pressed two run buttons quickly in turn.

In those rhythm games the tempo belongs to the soundtrack. Heartbeat Runner hands the tempo to the player and ties it to effort, so difficulty is something you choose with every step. I am not aware of a game that does exactly this, but heart rate and stamina are common in sports games, so similar ideas may well exist.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with a circle for the runner, a pulsing circle for the heart, a click sound on each beat, and a print of the heart rate and time. Use one course with two benches.

Give it to a friend without explaining the benches. The one question: by their third run, do they start walking before the bench on purpose, instead of collapsing first? If they do, the heart rate is readable and the choice is real. If they keep collapsing, make the danger louder: a faster, harder beat sound and a red strip above 160.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: one course, walk and sprint, benches, collapse, a heartbeat sound and a best time.
  • One-month version: ten courses with hills that raise the heart rate, a timing calibration screen, ghost runners of your best time, and music that follows your pulse.
  • Cut first: characters, gear and upgrades. The heart and the benches are the game.
  • Hardest part: making timing feel fair on every device. Measure input delay early and add an offset setting.
