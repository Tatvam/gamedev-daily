Beat Typist: every letter lands on the beat

A typing rhythm game where each letter must land on a beat and long words become half-beat drum fills. Playable prototype and Lua code.

Your fingers can type "snare" in a quarter of a second. Here they must type it like a drummer, one letter on each beat, while more words pile up and long ones turn into fast fills on the half beat.

The core loop: slow your fingers down to the beat

Most typing games reward speed. This one rewards timing. A steady beat pulses on screen. Words wait in a queue, and you always type the front word. Each letter must be pressed on a beat, one after another, with no beat skipped. The word is a short drum part, and your keyboard is the drum kit.

Two choices belong to the player. First, when to start: the first letter may land on any beat, so you can rest between words to breathe. Second, how to handle fills: a word of six letters or more is a fill, a quick run of notes that drummers play between sections. Its letters fall on every half beat, the point halfway between two beats. A fill is harder to keep steady but clears the queue twice as fast per letter and scores double.

Every ten seconds you type one or two words and see each letter land early, late or right on the beat. Every minute the queue grows faster and you decide whether to rest or chain words back to back. Every session is a set of songs, each with its own tempo and word list.

The rules

  • One letter per grid point. Short words take one letter per beat. Fills take one letter per half beat. Both must start on a full beat.
  • The timing window. A letter within 0.12 seconds of its grid point counts. Within 0.05 seconds it is perfect and worth 3 points; otherwise 1.
  • A word breaks easily. A wrong letter, a letter typed off the beat, or a grid point passing with no letter sends the word back to its first letter and resets the combo. The word stays at the front.
  • Finishing pays. A finished word adds one point per letter, two for a fill.
  • The queue is the danger. A new word arrives every few beats, a little sooner after each word you finish. If more than six words are waiting, the song stops. Finish twenty words to win.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the drummer who keeps perfect time while the band speeds up around them. The constraint is the opposite of every typing tutor: you are not allowed to go fast. A skilled typist has words stored as quick bursts in their fingers, and the game asks them to spread those bursts out evenly. That fight between habit and beat is the fun, and it means fast typists have no head start over slow ones.

The free start matters too. Nothing scrolls toward you to force a moment. You choose the downbeat of every word, so resting is a decision, and the growing queue makes it a costly one.

The hardest part to build is fair timing. Keyboards, browsers and screens all add delay, and a game that judges to within 50 milliseconds needs a calibration step and must time key presses precisely, not just once per frame.

Try it

Start the beat, wait for the count-in, then type the front word one letter per beat. Watch the lane under the word: the letters sit on the beats where they are due, and each press leaves a mark showing how early or late it was. Then try a fill and feel the gap between letters halve.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: the first letter snaps to the nearest beat, every later letter is due a fixed step after it, and the update breaks the word if a due time passes. Spawning words, the queue limit and drawing are left out. The names and numbers are the demo's.

local BPM = 80
local BEAT = 60 / BPM                   -- seconds per beat
local GOOD, PERFECT, FILL = 0.12, 0.05, 6
local time = -4 * BEAT                  -- a four beat count-in before zero
local score, combo = 0, 0
local queue = { "kick", "groove" }      -- words waiting, front first
local progress, start = 0, 0            -- letters typed, beat the word began on

local function step(word)               -- fills move on the half beat
  if #word >= FILL then return 0.5 end
  return 1
end

local function due()                    -- when the next letter must land
  return (start + progress * step(queue[1])) * BEAT
end

local function breakWord()
  progress, combo = 0, 0
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  local word = queue[1]
  if not word or #key ~= 1 or time < -GOOD then return end
  if progress == 0 then start = math.floor(time / BEAT + 0.5) end
  local off = time - due()              -- below zero: early, above zero: late
  if key ~= word:sub(progress + 1, progress + 1) or math.abs(off) > GOOD then
    breakWord()                         -- a typo, or off the beat
    return
  end
  progress, combo = progress + 1, combo + 1
  score = score + ((math.abs(off) <= PERFECT) and 3 or 1)
  if progress == #word then             -- finished: fills pay double
    score = score + #word / step(word)
    table.remove(queue, 1)
    progress = 0
  end
end

function love.update(dt)
  time = time + dt
  if progress > 0 and time > due() + GOOD then
    breakWord()                         -- a beat passed with no letter
  end
end

love.keypressed names letter keys "a" to "z", which is why a one-character check is enough here. The demo also measures the time between frames when a key arrives. In LÖVE the clock only moves once per frame, so at 60 frames per second a press can be judged up to 17 milliseconds wrong. That is a third of the perfect window.

Pitfalls

  • The beat is hard to feel in silence. A flashing circle is a weak metronome. If testers drift late by the same amount on every letter, they need sound. Make the click and the music the default in the real game, with a calibration screen.
  • One mistake costs too much. Breaking the whole word is clear, but on a ten-letter fill it can feel cruel. Watch whether testers start avoiding fills. If they do, keep the typed letters and only reset the combo, or let a fill resume on the next beat.
  • The reader stops reading. At slow tempos testers may type from memory and stop looking at the word. That is fine. If they start mistyping familiar words, the habit fight is working; if they mistype strange words, the list is too hard to read in rhythm.
  • The queue never matters. If testers never see more than two waiting words, there is no reason to chain words or risk fills. Tune the arrival rate until a good player reaches four or five waiting at least once per song.
  • Touch players get a different game. Tapping removes spelling and leaves pure rhythm. It is good for a demo, but decide early whether phones are a real target, because the game without spelling may be too thin.

In real games

Typing games are an established genre. The Typing of the Dead from Sega turned a light-gun zombie shooter into one where every shot is a typed word, and Epistory builds a whole adventure on typing. In both, as far as I know, the reward is for typing accurately and fast enough.

Rhythm games are their own large genre, and nearly all of them use a chart: notes placed at fixed times that scroll toward a hit line. Beat Typist has no chart. The player picks the start of every word, and the spelling decides the pattern. There are likely indie games that combine typing and rhythm; I have not surveyed them, so I will not claim this idea is the first.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with a flashing circle on each beat, the front word drawn large and a printed offset in milliseconds after every letter. Add a click sound on each beat, and use a fixed list of ten words.

Give it to three people, at least one of them a fast typist. The one question: do fast typists rush? If their offsets lean early on familiar words, the core tension exists and the game is worth building. If everyone simply waits and taps, the words are not pulling at their fingers. Try common words and more fills, then test again.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: one tempo, the beat grid, fills on the half beat, the queue, word breaking and a score.
  • One-month version: songs with real music and their own word lists, a lag calibration screen, tempo changes inside a song, and a results screen that shows which letters you rush.
  • Cut first: power-ups, multiple lanes and story. Each one pulls the eye away from the word and the beat.
  • Hardest part: timing that feels fair. Time key presses precisely and build the offset display before tuning any windows.
