Lighthouse Sweep: one beam, many ships, one button

A one-button night-sea game where you swing a lighthouse beam, and only the ships inside the light can steer around the rocks to harbour.

It is night, the sea is full of rocks, and every ship out there is sailing blind. You keep the lighthouse. You have one beam and one button, and whichever ship you light can see its way home while all the others drift toward the rocks.

The core loop: spot, sweep, save

The player never steers a ship. The only thing they control is the angle of the lighthouse beam, and the button moves it in one direction only:

  • Hold and the beam swings clockwise at a steady speed.
  • Release and it swings back the other way at the same speed.
  • It stops at the ends of its arc. To hold the light on one ship you tap in a rhythm.

Ships come in from the open sea at the top and head for the harbour mouth at the bottom. Each ship follows one simple rule. A lit ship, one inside the beam, turns toward the harbour and steers around any rock ahead of it. A dark ship keeps its current heading and cannot see anything, while a steady current pushes it sideways. Left in the dark, it ends on a rock, a cliff or the harbour wall.

The rules

A ship that reaches the harbour mouth scores one. Three wrecks end the night. A new ship arrives every 6.5 seconds at first, and the gap shrinks by a quarter of a second for every ship saved, down to 3 seconds. The spawns come from a fixed seed, so every night plays the same and players improve by learning it.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is being the keeper: calm, watchful, responsible for strangers. The game is about attention, deciding which of several problems gets your one resource right now. Every second spent on one ship is a second the others spend drifting.

The constraint that makes it interesting is geometry. The beam is a wedge, so a far ship is easy to hold while a ship near the harbour crosses the beam quickly and needs fine taps. Two ships in line with the lighthouse can be lit together, and good players wait for those alignments.

The hardest part to build is the ship steering. A lit ship must always do the obvious thing; if a ship in full light hits a rock, the player feels cheated.

Try it

Click or tap the sea, then hold Space, Up or W, or keep your finger or mouse button pressed on the canvas. Nothing moves until your first press. The first ship is heading straight for the middle rock: put the light on it and watch it turn away.

The code

This is the mechanic in LÖVE: the beam eases toward a swing speed, a ship is lit when its bearing from the lighthouse falls inside the beam, and only lit ships turn. The numbers are the ones the prototype uses, in a sea 400 units wide and 300 tall with y pointing down. Angles are in radians, and the beam angle is 0 when it points straight up.

local SWING, EASE = 1.4, 10       -- beam speed (radians per second), easing
local LIMIT, HALF = 1.5, 0.13     -- end of the arc, half width of the beam
local SPEED, TURN, CURRENT, LOOK = 20, 2, 6, 45
local LX, LY, HX, HY = 166, 284, 200, 280  -- lighthouse, harbour mouth
local beam, beamSpeed, ships, rocks = 0, 0, {}, {}  -- ships { x, y, h }, rocks { x, y, r }
local function wrap(a) return (a + math.pi) % (2 * math.pi) - math.pi end

local function isLit(s)  -- bearing from the lighthouse, 0 is straight up
  return math.abs(wrap(math.atan2(s.x - LX, LY - s.y) - beam)) < HALF
end

local function steer(s, dt)
  local wx, wy = HX - s.x, HY - s.y
  local len = math.sqrt(wx * wx + wy * wy)
  wx, wy = wx / len, wy / len                   -- toward the harbour
  local fx, fy = math.cos(s.h), math.sin(s.h)   -- forward
  for _, r in ipairs(rocks) do
    local rx, ry = r.x - s.x, r.y - s.y
    local dist = math.sqrt(rx * rx + ry * ry)
    if rx * fx + ry * fy > 0 and dist < r.r + LOOK then  -- rock ahead
      local side = (ry * fx - rx * fy >= 0) and -1 or 1  -- push away from it
      local w = (r.r + LOOK - dist) / LOOK * 2
      wx, wy = wx - fy * side * w, wy + fx * side * w
    end
  end
  local turn = wrap(math.atan2(wy, wx) - s.h)
  s.h = s.h + math.max(-TURN * dt, math.min(TURN * dt, turn))
end

function love.update(dt)
  local held = love.keyboard.isDown("space", "up", "w") or love.mouse.isDown(1)
  beamSpeed = beamSpeed + ((held and SWING or -SWING) - beamSpeed) * math.min(1, dt * EASE)
  beam = beam + beamSpeed * dt
  if math.abs(beam) > LIMIT then beam, beamSpeed = math.max(-LIMIT, math.min(LIMIT, beam)), 0 end
  for _, s in ipairs(ships) do
    if isLit(s) then steer(s, dt) end  -- in the dark the heading never changes
    s.x = s.x + (math.cos(s.h) * SPEED + CURRENT) * dt
    s.y = s.y + math.sin(s.h) * SPEED * dt
  end
end

Left out for space: spawning, and the checks that wreck a ship when it comes within its radius of a rock (3 units plus the rock's radius), leaves the sides of the sea, or crosses the harbour wall more than 24 units from the mouth. Lua's % takes the sign of the divisor, so wrap also works for negative angles.

Pitfalls

  • Lit ships that still crash. Simple steering has blind spots, such as a rock dead ahead cancelling the push; the prototype always pushes to one side to avoid that. Test early with a bot that keeps one ship lit from every spawn point. If a fully lit ship ever wrecks, the rule is broken, not the player.
  • One ship at a time is a chore. If ships arrive slowly, the game is just "follow the ship". The fun starts when two ships need the light at once. If a new player is bored for a minute, ships arrive too slowly.
  • The beam is hard to park. Holding the light still takes tapping, which is the skill of the game, but too fast a swing turns it into a fight. Put SWING on a key and try 1, 1.4 and 2 radians per second with three people.
  • Unreadable danger. Players must see which ship is in trouble before it happens. The dotted course line does that job; test without it to see if players can still plan.
  • Light through rocks. Here the beam passes through rocks. Blocking it adds shadows to play around but can make ships unreachable. Try it only once the basic game is fun.

In real games

Lighthouse Sweep sits near two families. The first is games where you guide units you do not directly control. Lemmings (DMA Design, 1991) is the classic: its creatures walk forward by themselves and the player changes what individuals do by assigning jobs. The second is the traffic and routing genre, where one collision is the cost of attention spent elsewhere.

The difference here is that the player gives no orders at all. The light only grants the ability to see, and the ships decide for themselves. Because the beam is one moving wedge, the choice of whom to help is a single angle, which is why the game fits on one button. I do not know of a published game built on exactly this rule, but I have not searched thoroughly, so check before calling it new.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build what the Lua above does: circles for rocks, triangles for ships, a wedge for the beam, and a harbour line at the bottom. Hand-place three ships on courses that all end on rocks and start them at the same time. Skip spawning and scoring.

Hand it to someone with only this instruction: "save them". The one question to answer: when two ships are in trouble at once, does the player feel a real choice about which to save? If they hesitate, then commit, you have the core of the game. If the answer is always obvious, move the rocks until it is not.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: the prototype above with a few hand-made sea charts, a foghorn sound on each wreck and a bell on each arrival, and a saved best score.
  • One-month version: a campaign of nights where the sea changes: tides that cover and uncover rocks, ships of different sizes that turn at different rates, fog that shortens the beam, and a storm that reverses the current halfway through.
  • Cut first: an upgrade shop and a keeper's story. The game is the moment of choosing which ship gets the light, so spend the month on that.
  • The hardest part is steering that never fails a lit ship. Test it with a bot before tuning anything else.
