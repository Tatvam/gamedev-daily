Ghost of Your Last Try: a heist crew made of your past runs

A puzzle platformer where every rewind leaves a ghost that repeats your last run, so you team up with yourself. Playable prototype and Lua code.

You are a burglar who works alone, but every door in the vault needs two people on two switches. So you rewind ten seconds, and the run you just made keeps going beside you as a ghost: your first failure holds a switch, your second becomes a step to climb, and your third try walks out the door.

The core loop: run, rewind, plan

Ghost of Your Last Try is a side-view puzzle platformer. Each room has pressure plates, a door that opens only while every plate is pressed, and an exit behind the door. A loop is one attempt of at most ten seconds. When you rewind, or the ten seconds run out, the run you just made becomes a ghost: a see-through copy that repeats exactly what you did, in time with every new loop.

Every ten seconds the player makes one run with a single job in mind. Every minute they have built a small crew of ghosts and escape a room. Every session brings rooms with more plates, doors that need to be held at different moments, and fewer ghost slots.

The rules

  • Loops. A loop lasts up to ten seconds. The clock starts when you first move, and every ghost starts its replay at that same moment.
  • Ghosts. A ghost replays your recorded positions, frame by frame. When its recording runs out, because you rewound early, it holds its last pose until the loop ends. That is how a ghost "stays" on a plate.
  • Ghosts are solid on top. You can stand on a ghost's head and ride it, but you walk through it from the side. Ghosts never react to you: the past cannot be pushed.
  • Three slots. At most three ghosts exist. A fourth rewind makes the oldest one fade.
  • The goal. Reach the exit. The score is how many ghosts you made; each room has a par.

The puzzle is a timetable. The diagram shows the plain solution to the demo's room, one job per ghost. The door is open only where the plate bars overlap, and your live run must reach the door inside that window.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the lone thief who is secretly a whole crew. The constraint is that every crew member is a run you already made and can never edit. You cannot fix ghost 1; you can only rewind and build around it. That turns mistakes into material, and it makes the par score interesting: the best solutions give one ghost two jobs at two different moments.

The mood is busy but forgiving. Nothing can kill you, and a bad run costs ten seconds at most.

The hardest part to build is keeping replays exact. A ghost must be in the same place at the same tick in every loop, or plans fall apart. The prototype records positions on a fixed timestep (60 steps per second, never tied to the frame rate), so frame drops cannot change a replay.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: record one pose per fixed step, and play each ghost back by the same tick number. Movement here is floor only, to keep the focus on recording; the demo adds the ledge, the plates, the door and standing on ghosts.

local STEP, LOOP_TICKS, MAX_GHOSTS = 1 / 60, 600, 3   -- 600 steps = 10 s
local SPEED, GRAVITY, JUMP, GROUND_Y = 180, 1500, 520, 292   -- 292 = floor 320 - height 28
local player = {x = 40, y = GROUND_Y, vy = 0}
local recording, ghosts, tick, acc = {}, {}, 0, 0

local function ghostAt(g, t)    -- a ghost that ran out holds its last pose
  return g[math.max(1, math.min(t, #g))]
end

local function rewind()         -- this run becomes a ghost
  if #recording == 0 then return end
  ghosts[#ghosts + 1] = recording
  if #ghosts > MAX_GHOSTS then table.remove(ghosts, 1) end   -- oldest fades
  recording, tick, acc = {}, 0, 0
  player.x, player.y, player.vy = 40, GROUND_Y, 0
end

local function movePlayer(s)
  local key = love.keyboard.isDown
  local dir = (key("right") and 1 or 0) - (key("left") and 1 or 0)
  if key("space") and player.y == GROUND_Y then player.vy = -JUMP end
  player.vy = player.vy + GRAVITY * s
  player.x = player.x + dir * SPEED * s
  player.y = math.min(GROUND_Y, player.y + player.vy * s)
  if player.y == GROUND_Y then player.vy = 0 end
end

function love.update(dt)
  acc = acc + dt
  while acc >= STEP do          -- fixed steps keep every replay identical
    acc = acc - STEP
    tick = tick + 1
    movePlayer(STEP)
    recording[tick] = {x = player.x, y = player.y}
    if tick >= LOOP_TICKS then rewind() end
  end
end

function love.keypressed(k) if k == "r" then rewind() end end

function love.draw()
  for _, g in ipairs(ghosts) do
    local p = ghostAt(g, tick)    -- every ghost reads the same tick
    love.graphics.rectangle("line", p.x, p.y, 20, 28)
  end
  love.graphics.rectangle("fill", player.x, player.y, 20, 28)
end

The two highlighted lines are the whole trick: write the pose under the tick number, and read it back under the same tick number in every later loop. The demo uses the same numbers, but waits for your first key before starting the clock.

Pitfalls

  • Recording inputs instead of positions. Replaying key presses looks cheaper, but a ghost then lives in a world that has changed: a door that was open in its run may be shut now, and the ghost walks into it and drifts off its plan. Positions never drift. Record inputs only if you want that drift as a feature, and then test what happens when a ghost is blocked.
  • Planning without seeing. If players cannot tell where a ghost will be at second six, planning becomes trial and error. Test: ask a tester to predict when the door will open. If they cannot, add tick marks to the ghost paths or a door-open bar on the loop clock.
  • One solution, then nothing. If every room is "one ghost per plate", the idea is used up in three rooms. The fun is in the par: rooms where a ghost can do two jobs, or where order matters. Check each room for a solution that beats the plain one.
  • Ghosts frozen in mid-air. Rewind during a jump and the ghost holds its last pose in the air, which makes a floating step. The prototype allows it. Decide early whether that is a bug or a trick, because players will find it and build plans on it.
  • Fiddly timing. A plan that fails because you jumped four frames late is not a puzzle. Keep windows wide (the demo's door stays open for seconds) and let a ghost that ran out keep holding its pose.

In real games

Recorded past selves are a known idea. In Cursor*10, a browser game by Nekogames, you play the same short climb several times and each earlier cursor replays its moves, so they can hold buttons for you. Braid has a world where a shadow of the player repeats actions that were rewound. Super Time Force turns rewinds into extra shooters fighting alongside you.

Ghost of Your Last Try leans on two less common choices: ghosts are platforms you can ride, and you only get three, with the oldest one fading. That makes the score about economy, doing a room with fewer selves, rather than about reflexes. Other games may well have done this combination; I have not checked them all.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above and add one plate on the floor and one door rectangle that is solid unless a ghost stands on the plate. Skip the ledge, riding ghosts and the slot limit.

Give it to a friend and say only "get through the door". The one question: do they work out that they need their own past run, without being told? If they rewind on purpose to leave a ghost on the plate within two minutes, the core idea reads. If not, make the ghost's replay more visible, for example with a flash when the loop restarts.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: the demo's rules in ten small rooms, each with a par, plus ghost paths. One plate type and one door type.
  • One-month version: doors that must be held at different moments, moving platforms that ghosts ride too, a timeline scrubber to preview your ghosts, and a level editor that tests rooms by replaying stored solutions.
  • Cut first: enemies and combat. They make replays fragile and pull the game toward reflexes, which fights the planning.

Next

Twist to try: let ghosts be solid from the sides too, so the live run can be blocked by its own past, and a careless early run becomes a wall you have to plan around.
