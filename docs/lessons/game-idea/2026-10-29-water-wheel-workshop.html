Water Wheel Workshop: light a village with water you never touch

A calm physics toy where you arrange gutters so falling water spins a wheel and lights a village, with a playable prototype and the wheel code in Lua.

Night is falling on a hill village, and its only power is a spring pouring from a pipe at the top of the hill. You have three wooden gutters and a water wheel. Pour the water onto the wheel's centre and nothing happens. Land it just past the top and the windows start to glow, one by one.

The core loop: place, pour, read the splash

Water Wheel Workshop is a puzzle toy about guiding a stream. Water leaves a spout as hundreds of small droplets (particles that fall under gravity and slide along whatever they land on). You never touch the water. You only drag and turn three gutters, straight planks the water runs along. At the bottom of the hill stands a wheel joined to a dynamo, a small generator that turns spin into electricity. The village lights up as the dynamo charges.

Every ten seconds the player nudges a gutter and watches where the stream lands. Every minute they find a faster route. Every session they power a new village with a new hill and new pieces.

The rules

  • Pieces. Three gutters of the same length. Drag the middle of a gutter to move it, drag one of its ends to turn it.
  • Water. The sluice (the gate on the spout) is closed at the start. Once you open it, droplets pour at a steady rate and land in the river at the bottom unless something catches them.
  • The wheel. A droplet that touches the rim pushes it along the direction the droplet is moving across the rim, and the wheel pushes back on the droplet. Friction slowly brings the wheel to rest.
  • The dynamo. It is wired one way round: only clockwise spin charges it. A wheel turning backwards spins freely and charges nothing.
  • The goal. Fill the power meter to 100 percent. Every 20 percent lights one more house, and the meter slowly drains if the wheel slows down. Your score is the time from opening the sluice to the whole village being lit.

The interesting rule is the wheel, which only turns when pushed off centre. Water falling onto the very top pushes straight at the axle and does almost nothing. Water landing just right of the top runs down the right side and drags the rim clockwise. Water on the left side turns it the wrong way. So the puzzle is never "get water to the wheel" but "get water to the right edge, moving the right way".

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the village millwright (the person who builds and keeps mills running), working by lantern light to bring power home. The constraint is that the water is never yours to steer: you shape the route and watch, so every nudge is a small experiment. The mood is calm. There is no way to lose, only a stream that could be doing more.

The hardest part to build is water that reads as water. These droplets do not push on each other, so a pool is just many dots on one spot. That is fine for a stream, but players will build cups and V shapes that trap water, and the game needs a rule for that. The prototype gives each droplet fourteen seconds of life and keeps at most 260, so a trap never stalls the game.

Try it

Press Open sluice. The stream already reaches the wheel, but it lands on the left side, so the rim turns red and the wheel spins backwards. Now drag gutter 3 up from the river until the water from gutter 2 lands just to the right of the top, and watch the windows.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: what happens when a droplet touches the wheel, and how the wheel turns its spin into power. Screen coordinates point down, so a positive omega (the wheel's spin, in radians per second) is clockwise on screen.

local WX, WY, WR, DROP = 430, 215, 48, 3        -- wheel centre, radius, drop radius
local GRIP, MASS, FRICTION, DRAG = 0.15, 0.06, 0.3, 0.12
local wheel = { omega = 0, angle = 0 }          -- omega > 0 is clockwise
local power = 0                                 -- percent

local function hitWheel(d)                      -- d has x, y, vx, vy
  local ox, oy = d.x - WX, d.y - WY
  local dist = math.sqrt(ox * ox + oy * oy)
  if dist >= WR + DROP or dist < 0.000001 then return end
  local nx, ny = ox / dist, oy / dist
  d.x, d.y = WX + nx * (WR + DROP), WY + ny * (WR + DROP)   -- sit on the rim
  local vn = d.vx * nx + d.vy * ny
  if vn < 0 then d.vx, d.vy = d.vx - nx * vn, d.vy - ny * vn end
  local tx, ty = -ny, nx                        -- clockwise along the rim
  local slip = (d.vx * tx + d.vy * ty) - wheel.omega * WR
  local dv = slip * GRIP
  d.vx, d.vy = d.vx - tx * dv, d.vy - ty * dv
  wheel.omega = wheel.omega + dv * MASS / WR    -- what the drop loses, the wheel gains
end

local function updateWheel(dt)                  -- call on a fixed step, 1 / 120 s
  local slow = (FRICTION + DRAG * math.abs(wheel.omega)) * dt
  if math.abs(wheel.omega) <= slow then wheel.omega = 0
  elseif wheel.omega > 0 then wheel.omega = wheel.omega - slow
  else wheel.omega = wheel.omega + slow end
  wheel.angle = wheel.angle + wheel.omega * dt
  local charge = math.max(0, wheel.omega) * 3 - 4          -- dynamo: clockwise only
  power = math.min(100, math.max(0, power + charge * dt))
end

The highlighted lines are the mechanic. slip is how much faster the droplet moves along the rim than the rim itself, so a droplet falling straight at the axle gives almost nothing. Call hitWheel for every droplet and updateWheel once, inside the same fixed-step loop that moves the droplets. The - 4 in charge is the drain: the wheel needs more than 1.3 radians per second before the village gains any light.

Pitfalls

  • A sweet spot too small to find. If only one position in a hundred works, players stop experimenting and start hunting pixels. In my tests of this prototype, the spots that light the village for gutter 3 form a band roughly 40 to 60 pixels wide. Test: give a new player a level and time how long until the first window lights. If it is over three minutes, widen the wheel or the gutters.
  • No feedback until success. A meter stuck at zero says nothing about how close the player is. The rim colour and turning spokes make a near miss readable. If testers say "nothing is happening" while the wheel turns backwards, the feedback is too quiet.
  • Pools and traps. Gutters set in a V catch water forever. Without a lifetime limit, droplets pile up until the game slows down. Build a cup on purpose in your first test and check the frame rate after a minute.
  • Fast droplets passing through thin gutters. A droplet that moves further in one step than a gutter is thick can skip through it. Run the physics on a small fixed step (this prototype uses 1/120 second) and keep gutters thicker than one step of the fastest drop.

In real games

Guiding a flow you cannot control directly is well-explored puzzle territory: games about leading walkers who march on their own, routing fluid through pipes, or drawing lines to deflect falling objects. Contraption builders, where you arrange parts and then watch a machine run, sit next door.

Water Wheel Workshop narrows that space to one question about force: where on the wheel does the water land, and which way is it moving? Reaching the wheel is only half the puzzle; the angle is the other half.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with droplets falling from a fixed point, a single gutter you move with the arrow keys and turn with two keys, and a number on screen showing wheel.omega. Draw the wheel with four spokes so its spin is visible.

Hand it to a friend with only "make the wheel spin clockwise". The one question to answer: after their first try spins it the wrong way, do they move the water to the other side on purpose? If they do, the wheel rule reads clearly and the idea is worth building out. If they move the gutter at random, add a clear arrow on the wheel showing the direction that charges.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: eight hand-made hills that vary the spout, the number of gutters and the wheel size, with a best time per hill.
  • One-month version: new pieces that each change the stream in one way (a splitter, a funnel, a bucket that tips when full), two wheels feeding one village, and a slow day and night cycle where demand rises after dark.
  • Cut first: real fluid simulation. Droplets that push on each other look better but cost weeks and make results less predictable.
  • The mechanic is where force lands, not where water goes. Every new piece should give the player another way to change the angle of the push.

Next

Twist to try: let the spout's flow rise and fall like a tide, so a route that works at full flow overshoots the wheel at low flow and the player needs a layout that works for both.
