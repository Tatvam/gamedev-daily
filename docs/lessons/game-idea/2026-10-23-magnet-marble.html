Magnet Marble: steer a ball you are not allowed to touch

A physics puzzle where you guide a rolling marble only by flipping magnets between pull and push, with a playable prototype and the force code in Lua.

A steel marble rolls across a quiet table. You cannot nudge it, aim it or stop it. All you can do is flip the five magnets around it between pull and push, before the launch or while it rolls, and watch it bend its path towards the hole or into a pit.

The core loop: set the field, launch, nudge, learn

Magnet Marble is a physics puzzle: a puzzle where the answer comes out of a simulation rather than a fixed rule. Each table has a launch pad, a goal hole, one or two pits and five magnets. Every magnet is either attract (it pulls the marble towards it) or repel (it pushes the marble away). That one switch per magnet is the whole control scheme.

The launch is always the same shot, so only the pattern of pulls and pushes, and the timing of any mid-roll flip, changes between tries.

The rules

  • The pull. Each magnet pulls or pushes with a strength that falls off with the square of the distance, an inverse-square force. A small softening number stops it going to infinity at the centre, and it fades to zero at a visible range ring 150 units out.
  • Rolling. The marble slows a little all the time (rolling friction) and bounces softly off the edges and the magnets.
  • Sticking. If the marble touches an attracting magnet slowly, it sticks. Flip that magnet to repel and it pops free. Leave it and the try ends.
  • Ending a try. The marble drops into the goal, falls in a pit, stays stopped or stuck too long, or runs out its 20 seconds. Its path stays on the table as a faint ghost trail for the next try.
  • Score. Clear three tables. The score is the total number of launches. Magnets keep their setting between tries, so you build on what you learned.

Why the force falls off so fast

The inverse-square rule makes a magnet a local event. In the prototype, a magnet at 40 units pulls about 15 times harder than at 100 units, so most steering happens on close passes. That lets the player reason about the path one magnet at a time.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the stage magician: the ball moves, your hands never touch it. The constraint is five switches and nothing else, which turns each try into a small experiment with the ghost trail as the lab notebook. Flipping mid-roll adds a second skill, so a careful planner and a quick-handed rescuer can both clear a table.

The hardest part to build is not the physics, which is twenty lines, but the tables: they need testing by a script, not just by eye (see Pitfalls).

Try it

Launch once without changing anything and watch where the marble goes wrong. Flip one magnet near that spot, launch again, and compare the new trail with the ghost of the old one. Then try flipping a magnet while the marble rolls.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: summing the pulls, applying friction, and stepping at a fixed rate so the same setup always gives the same roll. Constants and the first table match the prototype. bounce() is your own function for edges, magnets, sticking, pits and the goal.

local K, SOFT, RANGE, DRAG, MAXSPEED = 500000, 20, 150, 0.25, 400
local STEP = 1 / 120
local ball = { x = 50, y = 185, vx = 190, vy = 58 }
local magnets = {                            -- pol: 1 attract, -1 repel
  { x = 140, y = 153, pol = 1 }, { x = 269, y = 244, pol = 1 },
  { x = 295, y = 134, pol = 1 }, { x = 322, y = 299, pol = 1 },
  { x = 507, y = 215, pol = 1 },
}
local acc = 0

local function bounce() end                   -- yours: edges, magnets, pits, goal

local function step(h)
  local ax, ay = 0, 0
  for _, m in ipairs(magnets) do
    local dx, dy = m.x - ball.x, m.y - ball.y
    local d2 = dx * dx + dy * dy
    local d = math.sqrt(d2)
    if d > 0 and d < RANGE then
      local f = 1 - d2 / (RANGE * RANGE)      -- fades to 0 at the ring
      local a = K * m.pol * f * f / (d2 + SOFT * SOFT)
      ax, ay = ax + a * dx / d, ay + a * dy / d
    end
  end
  local damp = math.exp(-DRAG * h)            -- rolling friction
  ball.vx = (ball.vx + ax * h) * damp
  ball.vy = (ball.vy + ay * h) * damp
  local speed = math.sqrt(ball.vx * ball.vx + ball.vy * ball.vy)
  local cap = math.min(1, MAXSPEED / speed)   -- never faster than MAXSPEED
  ball.vx, ball.vy = ball.vx * cap, ball.vy * cap
  ball.x, ball.y = ball.x + ball.vx * h, ball.y + ball.vy * h
  bounce()
end

function love.update(dt)
  acc = acc + math.min(dt, 0.1)
  while acc >= STEP do step(STEP); acc = acc - STEP end
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  local i = tonumber(key)                     -- "1" to "5" flip a magnet
  if i and magnets[i] then magnets[i].pol = -magnets[i].pol end
end

The fixed step matters here: with a variable step, the same setup can roll differently on fast and slow computers, and a solution that depends on frame rate is not fair. On the first table, flipping magnet 4 alone is enough.

Pitfalls

  • Chaos hides the cause. Several inverse-square pulls make the path very sensitive. While building the prototype, one table that worked was broken by rounding the magnet positions to whole numbers, a change of less than half a unit. If players cannot see why a flip changed the outcome, the puzzle feels random. The fix used here: keep only tables with a winning roll under 7 seconds that still wins when the launch speed changes by 5 percent either way.
  • Brute force wins. Five two-way switches give 32 setups, so a patient player can try them all. Count the winners with a script: the prototype's tables have two to four each, and both "all pull" and "all push" fail. Later tables can add a third magnet state to grow the space.
  • The marble gets stuck forever. Without the sticking rule, the marble can orbit a pulling magnet for a long time. Detect slow contact, freeze the marble, and end the try after a short delay unless the player flips.
  • Unreadable fields. Draw the range rings, show direction with ticks as well as colour, and keep the ghost trail. If testers flip magnets far from the path, the field is not readable yet.

In real games

Guiding something by shaping the world around it, rather than steering it, is a well-explored corner of puzzle design. In Lemmings (DMA Design, 1991) you assign jobs such as digging or building, but you never steer the walkers themselves. Marble games such as Marble Madness (Atari Games, 1984) take the opposite approach: you push the ball directly. Magnet Marble sits closer to the first idea. The player is never on the board, and the only thing they change is the field the marble rolls through.

The softening term is borrowed from gravity simulations, where it stops two close bodies from flinging each other at absurd speed.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with circles for everything, a range ring per magnet, and a trail stored as a list of points. Make one table by hand, then write a loop that tries all 32 setups and prints the winners.

Hand the game to a friend and say only "click the magnets, then launch". The one question to answer: after a failed try, can they say which magnet to flip next and why? If they point at a magnet near where the trail went wrong, the mechanic is readable. If they flip at random, the forces are too strong, too far-reaching or too hidden.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: the prototype above with ten hand-checked tables, a par number of launches per table, and a short sound and flash when the marble drops in.
  • One-month version: forty tables with new pieces introduced one at a time: a magnet that is fixed and cannot be flipped, a magnet that switches by itself on a timer, walls, and a second marble that must share the same field.
  • Cut first: the second marble. It doubles the chaos for a small gain. Fixed and timed magnets add more puzzles for less work.
  • The hardest part is level design, not physics. Build the solver script before the level editor.
