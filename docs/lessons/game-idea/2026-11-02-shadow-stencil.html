Shadow Stencil: paint a wall with the dark you cast

A calm puzzle game where you move one lamp until the shadows of fixed blocks match a pattern on the wall, with a playable prototype and Lua code.

You are a sign painter who has lost every brush, and all you have left is a lamp. The blocks in the room never move, so the only way to paint the wall is to find the one spot where their shadows fall in exactly the right places.

The core loop: move, compare, nudge

Shadow Stencil is a top-down puzzle. A room holds a few fixed blocks and one lamp. Along the far wall runs a row of cells, and each cell is either lit or in shadow. Above the wall hangs a target strip: the pattern of lit and dark cells the client ordered. You may only move the lamp. When the wall matches the strip, the stencil is done.

Every ten seconds the player sweeps the lamp, watches the shadows slide, and compares the wall with the strip. Every minute they finish a wall. Every session they meet new rooms with more blocks, tall and short shapes, and later a second lamp.

The rules

  • The lamp. It moves freely inside a lamp area at the near side of the room. It cannot pass the blocks or touch the wall.
  • The cells. The wall is split into 16 cells. A cell is dark when the straight line from the lamp to the middle of that cell passes through any block. Otherwise it is lit.
  • The goal. Make every cell match the target strip. Each target is made by a real lamp position, so every wall can be solved.
  • The score. The time taken over three walls.

The whole puzzle comes from two facts about shadows, shown below. Move the lamp toward a block and its shadow grows. Move the lamp sideways and the shadow slides the other way. Every block obeys both facts at once, but by different amounts: a block near the wall barely changes, and a block near the lamp swings wildly.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the craftsperson who works with light instead of paint. The constraint is that one small object, the lamp, controls every shadow at once, so fixing one cell often breaks another. The mood is quiet and slow: there is no fail state, no clock pressure in the core game, and every move is undoable by moving back.

The answer is not a list of moves but a small patch of floor. Players find it by feel at first, then by reasoning once they notice which shadow moves fastest.

The hardest part to build is the level design, not the code. A target picked by hand may have no lamp position that makes it, or a patch so tiny that finding it is luck. The safe method, used in the prototype, is to choose a lamp position first, record the pattern it makes, and then measure how big the matching patch of floor is. In the demo the patches are about 1040, 850 and 480 square pixels, so each wall is harder than the last.

Try it

Sweep the lamp from side to side first, and watch which shadow moves fastest. Then move it toward and away from the blocks and watch the shadows grow and shrink. The red outlines mark the wall cells that are wrong. Try a wall with the outlines turned off: it becomes a real puzzle.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE, with the demo's numbers for the first wall. blocked asks whether the line from the lamp to a point on the wall passes through a box. It clips the line against the box's left and right sides, then its top and bottom, and keeps the part inside all four; if nothing is left, the line misses. This is the Liang–Barsky method, and it needs no square roots.

local WALL_X, WALL_Y, CELL, CELLS = 40, 44, 35, 16
local blocks = { {x = 130, y = 140, w = 50, h = 30},
                 {x = 290, y = 160, w = 40, h = 40},
                 {x = 440, y = 130, w = 60, h = 24} }
local target = "1110000011100011"     -- "1" means this cell must be dark
local lamp, solved = {x = 480, y = 270}, false

local function blocked(ax, ay, bx, by, b)   -- does line a-b cross box b?
  local t0, t1 = 0, 1
  local p = {ax - bx, bx - ax, ay - by, by - ay}
  local q = {ax - b.x, b.x + b.w - ax, ay - b.y, b.y + b.h - ay}
  for i = 1, 4 do
    if p[i] == 0 then
      if q[i] < 0 then return false end       -- parallel and outside
    elseif p[i] < 0 then t0 = math.max(t0, q[i] / p[i])   -- enters here
    else t1 = math.min(t1, q[i] / p[i]) end                -- leaves here
  end
  return t0 <= t1                             -- some part is inside
end

local function countMatches()
  local n = 0
  for i = 1, CELLS do
    local cx = WALL_X + (i - 0.5) * CELL       -- middle of cell i
    local dark = false
    for _, b in ipairs(blocks) do
      dark = dark or blocked(lamp.x, lamp.y, cx, WALL_Y, b)
    end
    if dark == (target:sub(i, i) == "1") then n = n + 1 end
  end
  return n
end
function love.update(dt)
  local key = love.keyboard.isDown
  local speed = key("lshift") and 30 or 120
  local dx = (key("right") and 1 or 0) - (key("left") and 1 or 0)
  local dy = (key("down") and 1 or 0) - (key("up") and 1 or 0)
  lamp.x = math.max(40, math.min(600, lamp.x + dx * speed * dt))   -- lamp area
  lamp.y = math.max(240, math.min(340, lamp.y + dy * speed * dt))
  solved = countMatches() == CELLS
end

The two highlighted lines are the whole rule: one ray per cell, from the lamp to the cell's middle, and the cell is dark if any block stops it. The demo uses the same numbers, and the lamp starts at (480, 270), where 8 of the 16 cells are wrong.

Pitfalls

  • Hot and cold with no thinking. With wrong cells marked, players may just wiggle the lamp until the red goes away. Test: hide the marks and the match count and see if testers still solve the first wall. If they cannot, the shadows are not readable enough; draw them on the floor more strongly.
  • Patches too small to find. A wall whose answer is a 5 pixel patch is a test of patience. Measure every level's patch with a script, as in the hardest-part note above, and never ship one under about 20 by 20 pixels at your lamp speed.
  • Cells flicker at shadow edges. When a shadow edge sits on a cell's middle, the cell flips back and forth with tiny moves. Draw the true shadow edge on the wall too, so the player sees why.
  • One kind of thought. After three walls, "sweep, then nudge" may be all there is. When testers stop looking at the blocks, the next mechanic is due: a second lamp, a block pushed once, or a mirror.

In real games

Shadows as the answer to a puzzle are well explored. In Shadowmatic the player rotates an abstract object in front of a light until its shadow forms a recognisable shape. In Contrast the main character can step into shadows on a wall, and moving light sources changes the shapes she can walk on.

Shadow Stencil moves the light rather than the object, keeps the objects fixed, and reduces the result to a row of cells you can compare at a glance. I have not seen that exact combination as the centre of a game, but I cannot be sure no one has made it, so take it as a direction, not a promise of a gap.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above. Draw the blocks with love.graphics.rectangle("fill", ...), the lamp with love.graphics.circle, and two rows of 16 small rectangles for the target strip and the wall. Skip the floor shadows, the timer and the second and third walls.

Hand it to a friend and say only "make the bottom row match the top row". Do not mark wrong cells. The one question: can they explain which way to move the lamp before they move it? If after two minutes they say something like "closer makes it wider", the rule reads. If they only wiggle, add the floor shadows and test again.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: one lamp, twelve walls made by the "pick a lamp spot first" method, the floor shadows, and a best time per wall.
  • One-month version: a second lamp (a cell is dark only if no lamp reaches it), round pillars, blocks you may push once, a level editor that measures each patch, and a gallery of finished signs.
  • Cut first: soft shadows and real lighting. They look lovely, but blurred edges make a cell's state ambiguous, and that breaks the puzzle.
  • The idea is one control that moves every answer at once. New features should add new ways for shadows to interact, not more things to control.

Further reading

  • You-Dong Liang and Brian A. Barsky, A New Concept and Method for Line Clipping (ACM Transactions on Graphics, 1984). The clipping method behind blocked.
  • Christer Ericson, Real-Time Collision Detection (Morgan Kaufmann, 2005). Its chapter on intersection tests covers segments against boxes with the slab method.
  • Amit Patel, 2D Visibility (Red Blob Games). An interactive article on working out what a light can see in a 2D room, the natural next step for floor shadows.
  • Jesse Schell, The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses (CRC Press). The lenses on puzzles and on feedback help when deciding how much to mark wrong cells.
