Orbit Hopper: let go at the right moment

A one-button space climber where you orbit a shrinking planet and press to fly off along the tangent, with a playable prototype and the orbit code in Lua.

Your little ship circles a planet on its own, and the planet is shrinking under you. One press lets go of its gravity and sends you flying in a straight line into the dark. Get the timing right and the next planet catches you. Get it wrong and you drift off into space.

The core loop: circle, aim, let go, get caught

The player never steers. The ship circles the planet by itself, and the only input is the moment you let go. That makes it a timing aim: the direction you fly in is decided by where you are on the circle when you press, so aiming and timing are the same action.

  • Orbiting: the ship moves around the planet's capture ring, the dashed circle where its gravity holds you, at a constant speed.
  • Press: the ship leaves along the tangent, the straight line that touches the circle at the ship's position. It keeps the speed it had.
  • Flying: nothing pulls the ship until it crosses another planet's capture ring. Then that planet catches it, and the ship starts circling it, clockwise or anticlockwise depending on which side it came in from.

The rules

The planet you are circling shrinks at a steady rate. As it shrinks, its capture ring shrinks with it, so your circle gets tighter. Your speed stays the same, so a tighter circle means more laps per second, and the moment you need to hit gets shorter. When the planet gets too small it breaks up and throws you off along whatever tangent you happen to be on. Waiting is never safe for long.

Three things end the run: drifting for more than 2.6 seconds without being caught, leaving the side of the play field, or falling below the bottom of the screen. The score is the highest point you reached.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the slingshot: the feeling of letting go at exactly the right instant and sailing across the gap. The constraint is that you cannot correct anything after the press. Every hop is a single decision, made with full information, and the result is visible within a second.

The shrinking planet is what turns a calm puzzle into an action game. Without it the best plan is to wait for a perfect line every time. With it, the player has to weigh a safe-looking shot now against a better one a lap later on a faster, tighter circle.

The hardest part to build is the level generator. Planets must be placed so that the next one is always reachable from the current ring within the drift time, and so that two capture rings never overlap. The prototype keeps the planets in rows at least 115 units apart, with rings at most 50 units in radius, which guarantees both.

Try it

Click or tap the canvas to focus it. The ship circles the bottom planet and waits. Press Space, Up or W, or tap the canvas, when the dashed aim line points through the next ring. Nothing shrinks until your first press.

The code

This is the whole mechanic in LÖVE: circle the current planet, let go along the tangent, and get caught by the first ring you cross. The numbers are the ones the prototype uses. Positions are in world units, y points down, and angles are in radians.

local SPEED, GAP, SHRINK, MINR = 120, 26, 2.2, 7  -- speed, ring gap, shrink rate, smallest planet
local planets = { { x = 150, y = 0, r = 22 } }       -- add more { x, y, r } above this one
local ship = { x = 150, y = -48, vx = 0, vy = 0, a = -math.pi / 2, dir = 1 }
ship.planet = planets[1]

local function release()
  ship.vx = -math.sin(ship.a) * SPEED * ship.dir  -- along the tangent
  ship.vy =  math.cos(ship.a) * SPEED * ship.dir
  ship.planet = nil
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "space" and ship.planet then release() end
end

function love.update(dt)
  local p = ship.planet
  if p then
    p.r = p.r - SHRINK * dt                          -- the planet shrinks under you
    if p.r < MINR then p.gone = true; release() return end
    local ring = p.r + GAP
    ship.a = ship.a + ship.dir * SPEED / ring * dt   -- same speed, tighter circle
    ship.x = p.x + math.cos(ship.a) * ring
    ship.y = p.y + math.sin(ship.a) * ring
  else
    ship.x = ship.x + ship.vx * dt
    ship.y = ship.y + ship.vy * dt
    for _, q in ipairs(planets) do
      local dx, dy = ship.x - q.x, ship.y - q.y
      local ring = q.r + GAP
      if not q.gone and dx * dx + dy * dy < ring * ring then
        ship.planet, ship.a = q, math.atan2(dy, dx)  -- caught
        ship.dir = (dx * ship.vy - dy * ship.vx >= 0) and 1 or -1
        break
      end
    end
  end
end

The sign of dx * vy - dy * vx, a 2D cross product, tells you which side of the planet the ship came in on, so the orbit carries on in the direction the ship was already moving. A freshly released ship cannot be caught again by its own planet: it starts exactly on the ring and a tangent line only moves away from the centre. Left out for space: generating planets as you climb, the camera, the drift timer, and the three ways to lose.

Pitfalls

  • Unreadable aim. Without the dashed aim line, players tend to press when the ship faces the target, which is a quarter turn too late on a circle. Test with and without the line. If players miss without it, keep it, or shorten it as the game goes on so that reading the circle becomes the skill.
  • Waiting is always right. If planets shrink too slowly, the best play is to circle until the perfect line comes round, and the game turns into a slow wait. Put SHRINK on a key and try 1.5, 2.2 and 3.5 with three people. You want them to feel they could have waited one more lap, but chose not to.
  • Unfair spawns. One unreachable planet ends a good run through no fault of the player. Run a bot that releases at every possible angle and checks that at least one hits the next ring. If any layout fails, change the generator, not the player's skill.
  • Overlapping rings. If two capture rings overlap, the ship gets passed between planets without a press, and the player loses the feeling of control. Keep a minimum spacing between planet centres larger than the sum of their largest rings.
  • A twitchy fast orbit. On a small planet the ship laps quickly, and the right moment can be shorter than a frame or two. Read the press on the frame it happens, not on the next update, and test on a slow machine.

In real games

Orbit Hopper sits in the territory of one-button timing games, where the whole game is choosing the instant to act. Circling one point and jumping off toward another is a common idea in small mobile and web games, and swinging games, where you let go of a rope to fly to the next anchor, are close neighbours: in both, a circular motion turns timing into direction.

It also borrows from orbital mechanics games such as Kerbal Space Program (Squad, 2015), where getting into and out of orbit is the core skill. Orbit Hopper is much simpler: there is no real gravity between planets, only a ring that catches you, so the player can predict every flight from a straight line.

What this idea adds is the shrinking planet, which gives every hop its own clock and makes waiting a cost. I do not know of a published game built on exactly this rule, but I have not searched thoroughly, so check before calling it new.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build what the Lua above does: circles for planets, dashed circles for the rings, a triangle for the ship, and a dashed aim line along the tangent. Place five planets by hand in a zigzag going up the screen. Skip shrinking, the camera and scoring.

Hand it to someone and say only "get to the top". The one question to answer: after a miss, does the player know why they missed? If they say "I pressed too late" without being told, the aim reads well and you can add shrinking next. If they blame the game, fix the aim line before anything else.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: the prototype above with a gentle difficulty curve (planets get smaller and further apart with height), a soft click on every catch, a trail behind the ship, and a saved best height.
  • One-month version: planets with personalities: some spin you faster, some reverse your direction, some drift sideways, and a few rare large ones that shrink slowly and act as rest stops. Add a daily seed so everyone climbs the same sky.
  • Cut first: real gravity between planets. It looks clever, but bent flight paths break the straight-line aim, which is what makes the game readable.
  • The hardest part is a generator that never builds an unreachable sky. Test it with a bot before tuning anything else.

Next
