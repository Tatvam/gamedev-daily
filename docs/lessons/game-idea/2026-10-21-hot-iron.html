Hot Iron: hold to heat, let go to strike

A one-button smithing game where holding heats the metal and letting go strikes it, with a playable prototype and the heat rules in Lua.

You are the village smith, and every customer wants a better blade than the last. Hold the button and the iron glows hotter and hotter; let go and the hammer falls, so the only question in the whole game is when to stop heating.

The core loop: heat, judge, strike

Hot Iron is a one-button game: the only input is a button you hold or release. Holding pushes the metal into the forge and heats it. Releasing pulls it out onto the anvil and strikes it once. There is no way to stop heating without striking. Letting go is the commitment.

Each strike has a target band, a range of heat where the metal works well. A blade takes three strikes, each with its own band.

The rules

Heat runs from 0 (cold) to 100 (white hot).

  • Heating speeds up. While you hold, heat rises at 20 per second plus 40 for every second you have been holding. A tap barely warms the metal; a long hold races.
  • Cooling is slow. Not holding, the metal loses 8 heat per second. Each strike takes another 10.
  • Scoring a strike. Within half a band width of the target is Clean (2 points). Within a full band width is Rough (1 point). Anything further is too cold or too hot (0 points).
  • Orders. The first customer wants 2 of a possible 6 points, the next 3, and so on up to 6. The clean half width starts at 12 heat and shrinks by 1.5 with every blade sold, down to 4.
  • Reputation. A blade below the grade asked for is rejected and costs one of three reputation marks. Lose all three and the shop closes. Your score is blades sold.

The heat curve below is the whole game. The first strike needs a long hold from cold. The second target is lower, so the smith waits for the metal to cool, then taps.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the craftsperson's eye: knowing by feel when the iron is ready. The constraint is that you can only add heat, and the only way to stop is to commit.

The speed-up turns a timing test into a decision. From cold, a high target means a long hold that is racing when it arrives. From warm, a short tap is easy to control. So it can be worth waiting for the metal to cool to just under the band, then tapping.

The hardest part is not the code but tuning the curve: steep enough that cold starts feel risky, gentle enough that a careful player can land the late, narrow bands.

Try it

Click or tap the canvas, then hold to heat and let go to strike. When a target is below the current heat, wait for the gauge to fall to just under the band and tap. Once you can sell a few blades, switch to metal colour only and judge by the glow.

The code

The whole mechanic in LÖVE: heating with a speed-up, cooling, and scoring a strike when the button comes up. The names and numbers match the prototype, but the targets here are fixed to the graph's; the prototype picks them from a seeded random generator. finishBlade is your own function that sells or rejects the blade and starts the next one.

local HEAT_BASE, HEAT_RAMP, COOL, STRIKE_COOL = 20, 40, 8, 10
local heat, holdTime, holding = 0, 0, false
local blade = { targets = { 70, 50, 80 }, half = 12, strike = 1, points = 0 }

local function strike()
  local d = math.abs(heat - blade.targets[blade.strike])
  if d <= blade.half then
    blade.points = blade.points + 2                -- clean
  elseif d <= blade.half * 2 then
    blade.points = blade.points + 1                -- rough
  end                                              -- else too cold or too hot
  heat = math.max(0, heat - STRIKE_COOL)           -- the anvil drains heat
  blade.strike = blade.strike + 1
  if blade.strike > 3 then finishBlade(blade.points) end
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "space" then holding, holdTime = true, 0 end
end

function love.keyreleased(key)
  if key == "space" and holding then              -- letting go is the strike
    holding = false
    strike()
  end
end

function love.update(dt)
  if holding then
    holdTime = holdTime + dt
    heat = math.min(100, heat + (HEAT_BASE + HEAT_RAMP * holdTime) * dt)
  else
    heat = math.max(0, heat - COOL * dt)
  end
end

The highlighted heating line is where the feel lives. Because holdTime resets on every press, each hold starts gently and speeds up, whatever the current heat. Set HEAT_RAMP to 0 and the game becomes a plain stopwatch test; raise it and cold starts become a gamble.

Pitfalls

  • One safe strategy. If letting the metal go cold and heating from zero always works, players will do only that. A gentle order timer keeps the choice alive.
  • The band hides the metal. With a gauge on screen, players stare at the bar and never at the glowing iron, which has all the charm. Test colour-only mode early. If nobody can play it, make the colour steps clearer rather than adding interface.
  • Unfair narrow bands. At full speed, a clean zone 8 heat wide passes in about a tenth of a second. That is a reaction test, not judgement. Make sure a slower approach always exists.
  • Late feedback. Show each strike's result as the hammer lands, and say whether it was too hot or too cold, so players know which strike went wrong.
  • Early losses. One bad blade early can feel like most of a run gone. If many runs end in the first minute, open with an order that cannot be failed.

In real games

The hold and release meter is an old idea in sports games. Many golf games used a swing meter where one press starts a bar moving, a second sets the power and a third sets the accuracy. Hot Iron keeps the "stop the bar at the right place" core but adds two changes: the bar speeds up the longer you hold, and the start point depends on what you did last.

Smithing appears in many role-playing and crafting games, often as a short timing minigame; I have not checked how specific games score it. The theme comes from the real craft: smiths judge the heat of iron and steel partly by its colour, from dull red to yellow and white, and working metal that is too cold risks cracking it. The numbers here are made up for play, not taken from metallurgy.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with a rectangle for the metal coloured by heat, a gauge with the band beside it, and three squares for strike results. Pick targets randomly between 35 and 85, and log the heat at each press, the hold time and the points.

Give it to someone and say only "hold to heat, let go to strike, aim for the band". The one question to answer: do players ever choose to wait for the metal to cool instead of striking straight away? If every strike is a hold from wherever the heat happens to be, try a steeper HEAT_RAMP. If they pause and watch the gauge fall, the game has its decision.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: the prototype above with a clear colour ramp for the metal, a sparks effect on clean strikes, a sold-blades high score and an optional anvil clang that plays only after the player turns sound on.
  • One-month version: a queue of customers wanting different items (a nail takes one strike, a sword five), metals that heat and cool at different speeds, and forge upgrades.
  • Cut first: the upgrades. Different metals give more variety for less work.
  • The hardest part is tuning HEAT_RAMP against the band widths. Plot the heat curve for a cold start and check where each band falls on it.

Next

Twist to try: let a clean strike also widen the next band a little and a wasted one narrow it, so a good rhythm carries forward and a mistake follows you through the rest of the blade.
