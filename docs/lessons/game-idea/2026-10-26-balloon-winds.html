Balloon Winds: steer by choosing your altitude

A hot air balloon game where you cannot steer and only rise or sink into wind layers, with a playable prototype and the balloon physics in Lua.

Your balloon has no rudder, no engine and no steering wheel. All you have is a burner to rise and a vent to sink, and a sky stacked with winds that each blow their own way. To land in the right field, you have to find the right layer of air and leave it at the right moment.

The core loop: read the layers, pick one, leave it on time

Balloon Winds is a navigation puzzle played in real time: the player never moves sideways directly, but chooses which horizontal force acts on them by choosing a height. The sky is split into four wind bands, horizontal layers that each blow left or right at a fixed speed. Drifting streaks and a number show each band's direction and speed.

Up is the burner, down is the vent, and that is all. Sideways movement comes from where you are, not what you press.

The rules

  • Heat, not speed. The burner does not push the balloon up. It heats the air inside the envelope, and the heat creates lift. Heat at the halfway mark is neutral. Above it the balloon climbs, below it the balloon sinks, and the air cools slowly by itself. This lag, the delay between pressing and moving, is the heart of the game.
  • Wind. The balloon's sideways speed eases towards the speed of the band it is in. A new band takes hold over a second or so.
  • Fuel. Each flight has 60 units of fuel, and the burner uses 7 per second. The vent is free. With no fuel, the balloon can only cool and come down.
  • Landing. Touching the ground faster than 22 pixels per second is a hard landing, and the flight is lost. A soft landing in the green field scores. A soft landing elsewhere is allowed, and you can take off again if you have fuel.
  • Score. A landing scores up to 100 for closeness to the flag (50 at the edge of the field) plus the fuel you have left. Three flights make a session. Drifting off either edge of the map ends the flight.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the quiet expert pilot who reads the sky better than anyone. The constraint is that steering is indirect: one axis of input controls the other axis of movement through the world's own rules. It rewards looking ahead rather than fast hands, and the mood is calm, with tension only near the ground.

The hardest part to build is the set of skies. The lag makes routes hard to judge by eye, so write a small autopilot that holds a chosen band, sinks at a chosen point, and tries every exit point. Each of the prototype's three skies was checked this way and has a window of at least 60 pixels of exit points that land in the field.

Try it

On the first flight, climb into the second band and ride it right. Start venting a little before the field, and watch the slow ground wind carry you back. On the third flight that ground wind is strong, so you must overshoot further.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: heat with lag, lift from heat, wind by band, and the landing check. The numbers and the winds are those of the first flight. Altitude is measured in pixels above the ground, and landed(hard) is your own function that scores, crashes, or lets the balloon rest.

local BAND, TOP = 65, 260                 -- band height and ceiling, pixels above ground
local HEAT, VENT, COOL = 0.6, 0.8, 0.05   -- heat change per second
local LIFT, VDRAG, HDRAG = 60, 0.8, 1.2
local BURN, SAFE = 7, 22                  -- fuel per second, fastest soft landing
local winds = { -15, 35, -30, 55 }        -- [1] is nearest the ground; + blows right
local b = { x = 100, alt = 0, vx = 0, vz = 0, heat = 0.45, fuel = 60, ground = true }
local function landed(hard) end           -- yours: score, crash, or rest

local function windAt(alt)
  return winds[math.min(#winds, math.floor(alt / BAND) + 1)]
end

function love.update(dt)
  if love.keyboard.isDown("up") and b.fuel > 0 then
    b.heat = b.heat + HEAT * dt
    b.fuel = math.max(0, b.fuel - BURN * dt)
  elseif love.keyboard.isDown("down") then
    b.heat = b.heat - VENT * dt
  else
    b.heat = b.heat - COOL * dt
  end
  b.heat = math.max(0, math.min(1, b.heat))
  local lift = (b.heat - 0.5) * LIFT        -- above half heat it climbs
  if b.ground then
    if lift <= 0 then return end
    b.ground = false
  end
  b.vz = b.vz + (lift - b.vz * VDRAG) * dt
  b.vx = b.vx + (windAt(b.alt) - b.vx) * HDRAG * dt
  b.x, b.alt = b.x + b.vx * dt, b.alt + b.vz * dt
  if b.alt > TOP then b.alt, b.vz = TOP, math.min(0, b.vz) end
  if b.alt <= 0 then
    local hard = -b.vz > SAFE
    b.alt, b.vz, b.vx, b.ground = 0, 0, 0, true
    landed(hard)
  end
end

Two lines carry the feel. Lift comes from heat, not from the key, so the balloon keeps climbing after you let go, and sideways speed eases towards the band's wind instead of jumping to it. With VDRAG at 0.8, full heat settles at a climb of about 37 pixels per second and no heat at a fall of about 37, well past the safe 22, so venting all the way down is a crash. To draw, use groundY - b.alt as the screen height.

Pitfalls

  • Lag that feels like broken controls. If the balloon answers too slowly, players think the key is broken. A heat bar with a neutral mark and an instant flame answer the input even when the movement does not. Test early: if players hammer the key, raise HEAT.
  • Unreadable winds. If players cannot tell which way a band blows without entering it, the game becomes trial and error. Ask a tester to name each band's direction before their first flight.
  • One right answer. If only one exit point works, the game feels fussy. Print the autopilot's winning windows; narrow ones need a weaker ground wind or a wider field.
  • Long dull cruises. Twenty seconds in one band with nothing to decide is boring. Keep maps narrow and give reasons to change bands more than once.

In real games

Steering through the environment is well explored ground: sailing games make the wind the main force the player works with, and gliding games make height a resource spent to travel.

The idea also comes from the real thing: balloon pilots do steer, roughly, by changing height to catch winds blowing in different directions, since the balloon itself has no way to steer. Balloon Winds turns that into the whole game. What makes it different from a sailing game is that the player cannot angle against the wind at all; the only choice is which wind to be in, and when to leave it.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with a circle for the envelope, a rectangle for the field, four coloured bands, and a heat bar. Hard-code the first sky. Add drifting lines for the winds.

Hand it to a friend and say only "Up is the burner, Down is the vent, land on the green". The one question to answer: on their second attempt, do they start descending before they reach the field? If they do, they have read both the lag and the ground wind, and the core idea works. If they keep overshooting the same way, the lag or the ground wind is not visible enough yet.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: the prototype with ten hand-made skies, each checked by the autopilot script, a par score per sky, and a small celebration on landing.
  • One-month version: a journey of linked fields across a country, with winds that shift during a flight, birds in some bands, fuel stops, and a forecast screen before each leg.
  • Cut first: winds that change during a flight. They break the solver script and make bad landings feel unfair. Fixed bands per sky give plenty of puzzles.
  • Keep one axis of input. A sideways fan would make it a different, weaker game.

Next

Twist to try: make the bands drift up or down slowly over the flight, so the ride you planned at the start sinks under you and you must chase the wind you need.
