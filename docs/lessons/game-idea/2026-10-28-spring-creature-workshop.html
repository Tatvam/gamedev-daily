Spring Creature Workshop: build a body, then teach it to walk

A sandbox where you build creatures from points, springs and pulsing muscles and race them, with a playable prototype and the muscle physics in Lua.

You stick four dots together, mark one line as a muscle, and press Run. Your creature twitches on the spot for ten seconds. Then you move one muscle half a beat later, and the same body gets up and marches off the screen.

The core loop: build, run, watch the feet

Spring Creature Workshop is a sandbox racing game where you never steer. You build a body from points (small masses that gravity pulls down) joined by springs (links that try to keep their length). Some links can be muscles: springs whose length grows and shrinks on a steady rhythm. Then you press Run, and the creature has ten seconds to get as far right as it can.

The player does not move the creature. They design its rhythm. Every few seconds they change one link and run again. Every minute they find a gait (a pattern of steps) that is better than the last. Every session they take a favourite creature through a series of tracks.

The rules

  • Body. Up to ten points and as many links as you like between them. Points are the only things that touch the ground.
  • Muscles. Any link can be a muscle. Muscles stretch and shrink by a quarter of their length, following a sine wave (a smooth up-and-down curve). There are two kinds: A moves on the beat, and B moves half a beat later, so B is long while A is short.
  • Rhythm. One slider sets the tempo for every muscle, in beats per second.
  • Grip. A point on the ground resists sliding, and it resists harder the more weight presses on it. A foot carrying the body grips; a foot that is only brushing the ground slides.
  • The race. Ten seconds. The score is how far the middle of the creature moved to the right, in metres. Going left counts as negative.

The grip rule is what makes walking possible. A single muscle between two feet pulls them together, then pushes them apart. Both feet carry the same weight, so they slide by the same amount and the creature shuffles on the spot. To move, the body must shift its weight onto one foot at the moment the muscle pulls, so the light foot slides and the heavy foot stays planted. Getting that timing right is the whole game.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the inventor in a shed, watching a strange machine come to life. The constraint is that the only thing you control is timing. You cannot push the creature or choose where it steps. A body that looks clumsy can win, and a sensible-looking one can walk backwards, which makes every run a small surprise you caused yourself.

The hardest part to build is the physics feeling fair. Stiff springs that fight each other can shake and inject energy, and a creature that vibrates across the ground at high speed is a bug that feels like a cheat. This prototype limits every point's speed and keeps muscle changes smooth. Before publishing, I ran 300 random creatures through it to check that none produced invalid numbers and that no run went wildly further than a creature that actually steps.

Try it

Press Run first. The starter crate has one muscle between its feet, and it shuffles in place. Now tap the right leg, the link from the right foot up to the top right corner, until it turns into a dashed B muscle, and run again. Then try an A muscle on the same leg, and watch which way it goes.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE. Each point stores its position and its last position px, py: in Verlet integration a point keeps moving by as much as it moved last step. Every link is then nudged toward its rest length several times per step, and a muscle simply has a rest length that changes with time.

local G, STEP, ITER, DAMP, VMAX = 900, 1 / 60, 12, 0.99, 8
local GROUND, AMP, STIFF, MU = 250, 0.25, 0.5, 1
local points, links, beat, t, acc = {}, {}, 1.0, 0, 0  -- yours: fill from the editor
local function restLength(l)          -- l.kind is "spring", "A" or "B"
  if l.kind == "spring" then return l.rest end
  local shift = (l.kind == "B") and math.pi or 0       -- B is half a beat late
  return l.rest * (1 + AMP * math.sin(2 * math.pi * beat * t + shift))
end
local function step()
  for _, p in ipairs(points) do
    local vx, vy = (p.x - p.px) * DAMP, (p.y - p.py) * DAMP
    local speed = math.sqrt(vx * vx + vy * vy)
    if speed > VMAX then vx, vy = vx * VMAX / speed, vy * VMAX / speed end
    p.px, p.py, p.push = p.x, p.y, 0
    p.x, p.y = p.x + vx, p.y + vy + G * STEP * STEP
  end
  for _ = 1, ITER do
    for _, l in ipairs(links) do
      local a, b = l.a, l.b; local dx, dy = b.x - a.x, b.y - a.y
      local d = math.max(math.sqrt(dx * dx + dy * dy), 0.000001)
      local k = (d - restLength(l)) / d * 0.5 * STIFF
      a.x, a.y, b.x, b.y = a.x + dx * k, a.y + dy * k, b.x - dx * k, b.y - dy * k
    end
    for _, p in ipairs(points) do      -- push: how far the ground lifted it
      if p.y > GROUND then p.push, p.y = p.push + p.y - GROUND, GROUND end
    end
  end
  for _, p in ipairs(points) do        -- grip grows with how hard it pressed
    if p.push > 0 then
      local vx = p.x - p.px
      local grip = math.min(math.abs(vx), MU * p.push)
      p.x, p.py = p.px + vx - (vx > 0 and grip or -grip), p.y
    end
  end
  t = t + STEP
end
function love.update(dt)               -- fixed steps: the same creature always runs the same
  acc = acc + dt
  while acc >= STEP do step(); acc = acc - STEP end
end

The highlighted line is the muscle. The grip block below it is what turns muscle motion into travel: a point that pressed hard into the ground this step (a large push) can cancel a lot of sideways motion, and a point that barely touched cannot. Distances are in pixels, and the demo counts 50 pixels as one metre. Store each link's rest as the distance between its points when the player creates it.

Pitfalls

  • Nothing works for the first ten minutes. If every early creature shuffles in place, players decide the game is broken. Ship starter bodies that almost walk and need one change. Test: give a new player the starter and time how long it takes them to see their first real step. Aim for under two minutes.
  • Vibration exploits. Links that fight each other jitter, and jitter plus friction can slide a creature along faster than any gait. Players will find it and it will dominate. Cap point speed, keep muscle changes smooth, and run a batch of random creatures to see whether the top distances come from jitter or from steps.
  • Too many knobs. Per-muscle tempo, strength and phase sliders sound generous but turn design into random search. Two muscle kinds and one tempo keep every change readable. Watch a tester: if they change several things between runs, they have stopped reasoning.
  • Random results. If the same creature scores differently on two runs, players cannot learn. Run the physics on a fixed step and never use randomness inside a run.

In real games

Building a body from masses and springs and watching it move is a known area for web toys and physics sandboxes, and there are games where creatures evolve their own walking patterns over many generations. Ragdoll physics in action games uses a related idea: a body of joints and constraints that the physics engine moves.

Spring Creature Workshop keeps the human as the designer instead of handing the work to an evolutionary search, and it squeezes the controls down to two muscle timings and one tempo. The interest comes from reading why a foot slipped, not from tuning dozens of values.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with a hard-coded four-point crate, a key that changes the right leg between spring, A and B, a key that restarts the run, and a line showing the distance travelled. Draw muscles thicker than springs.

Hand it to a friend with only "make it walk right". The one question to answer: after their first failed run, can they say which foot was slipping? If they can, the game is readable and worth extending. If they only try random changes, make the grip visible, for example by drawing planted feet as filled dots and sliding feet as hollow ones.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: the prototype with five tracks (flat, a gentle slope, a step, a gap, a low ceiling), a saved creature per track, and a ghost of your best run.
  • One-month version: a shared gallery of creatures, races between two players' designs, heavier and lighter points, and a slow-motion replay with the grip shown on each foot.
  • Cut first: creature sharing. It needs servers and moderation, and the game is already fun alone once the player understands grip.
  • The mechanic is timing, not building. Every feature should give the player another reason to ask "which foot carries the weight right now?"
