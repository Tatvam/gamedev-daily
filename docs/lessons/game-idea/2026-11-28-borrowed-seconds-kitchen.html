Borrowed Seconds Kitchen: steal time from one pan to save another

A cooking game where you never touch the food, only move seconds between pan timers, and every second you take must come from another dish.

You are a cook with a stopped pocket watch, and you can pull five seconds out of one pan and push them into another. Four dishes, four waiters on their way, and the seconds are never free: every dish you rescue cooks some other dish faster.

The core loop: spot the mismatch, find a donor, move five seconds

There is a stove with four pans. Each pan holds a dish with two clocks: the cook timer, the seconds until the dish is done, and the pickup, the seconds until its waiter arrives to take it. Both count down at the same speed, so on their own they never drift apart. Only you can change that.

Your one verb is the transfer: drag from one pan to another and five seconds move from the first pan's cook timer to the second's. The donor loses five seconds and finishes sooner. The receiver gains five and finishes later. The total of all cook timers never changes.

Every ten seconds you read the stove: which dish is off, and which can give or take five seconds? Every minute you run one service of a dozen dishes. Every session is a week of services, each adding a new kind of dish or a new rule.

The rules

  • The window. A dish is perfect if it is done when its waiter arrives and has not burnt yet. A done dish burns five seconds after it finishes. So it must finish in the five seconds before pickup: that span is its finish window.
  • Transfers are zero-sum. One drag moves exactly five seconds from donor to receiver. Both pans must hold a dish. If the donor is pushed five or more seconds past done, it burns on the spot.
  • Outcomes. Done in the window: perfect. Still cooking at pickup: sent back raw. Done too early: burnt before the waiter comes. Either way, a new dish arrives a moment later.
  • Goal. Serve as many of the twelve dishes perfectly as you can.

Reading one pan

Each pan is drawn as a timeline running to the right from "now". The green band is the finish window, ending at the pickup mark. The dot is the moment the dish will be done. Since everything slides left at the same speed, the dot only moves relative to its band when you transfer.

What makes it different

Cooking games usually test your hands. Here the cook never moves. The player fantasy is the kitchen wizard who bends time, and the constraint is that time is a fixed pot shared by every pan. The interesting moments are loans: a dish that is fine today gives five seconds to save a neighbour, and you plan to pay it back from the next dish that arrives with seconds to spare. If that dish never comes, your loan burns.

The code is small. The hardest part to build is the dealer that chooses each new dish. If it deals at random, the books often cannot balance and losses feel unfair; if it always deals the exact fix, there is nothing to plan. The prototype tilts each new dish toward balancing the stove, with one step of random error.

Try it

The kitchen starts closed, so you can study the first four pans. The risotto will burn, the steak and the dumplings will be raw. Two transfers fix all of them. Then press Start and keep up as new dishes arrive: look for dots outside their bands and dishes that can lend.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: two clocks per pan, a transfer that keeps the total fixed, and a judge that runs when either clock runs out. The need function tells the dealer, or a hint system, how many transfers a pan needs. Names and numbers match the demo; drawing and input are left out.

local CHUNK = 5    -- one transfer moves five seconds
local WINDOW = 5   -- a done dish burns five seconds later
local pans = {     -- cook: seconds until done, pickup: seconds until the waiter
  { dish = "Risotto", cook = 10.0, pickup = 22 },
  { dish = "Steak",   cook = 18.5, pickup = 16 },
}
local served, lost = 0, 0

local function finish(pan, perfect)
  pan.dish = nil
  if perfect then served = served + 1 else lost = lost + 1 end
end

local function transfer(from, to)
  local a, b = pans[from], pans[to]
  if from == to or not a.dish or not b.dish then return end
  a.cook = a.cook - CHUNK            -- the donor finishes sooner
  b.cook = b.cook + CHUNK            -- the receiver later; the total stays the same
  if a.cook <= -WINDOW then finish(a, false) end  -- took too much: burnt
end

local function need(pan)             -- transfers to receive (negative: to give)
  return math.floor((pan.pickup - pan.cook) / CHUNK)
end

function love.update(dt)
  for _, pan in ipairs(pans) do
    if pan.dish then
      pan.cook = pan.cook - dt
      pan.pickup = pan.pickup - dt
      if pan.cook <= -WINDOW then finish(pan, false)        -- burnt
      elseif pan.pickup <= 0 then finish(pan, pan.cook <= 0) -- raw if not done
      end
    end
  end
end

Check need with the risotto: pickup 22 minus cook 10 is 12, divided by 5 is 2.4, rounded down is 2. Two transfers in take it to 20, which is done two seconds before the waiter. The steak gives floor(-2.5 / 5) = -1: it must give one. Because the window is exactly as wide as a transfer, every dish has one right number of transfers.

Pitfalls

  • It becomes a sum, not a game. If every new dish arrives with an exact partner, players stop reading the stove and just count. Keep some imbalance so loans are needed, and test whether players ever say "I'll pay it back later".
  • It becomes unwinnable. If the dealer is fully random, the stove's total need drifts, and some dishes are doomed the moment they arrive. Log the sum of need over the shift; if it often goes beyond two in either direction, tilt the dealer harder.
  • The board is unreadable. Two numbers per pan is too much to compare at speed. Draw the gap, not the clocks: a dot and a band. In testing, point at a pan and ask "raw, perfect or burnt?"
  • Waiters in a pile. If several pickups land within a second, the player cannot fix them all in time. Space pickups at least two seconds apart.

In real games

In Overcooked, food left on the stove burns and can start a fire, and players race around a kitchen to chop, cook and plate. Restaurant time-management games such as the Diner Dash series are about serving customers before their patience runs out. In both, you are the bottleneck.

Borrowed Seconds Kitchen removes movement entirely and makes time itself the resource, a fixed amount you move between dishes. I do not know of a game built on exactly this zero-sum transfer, but I have not played every cooking game, so one may exist.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with four rows of rectangles, a dot and a band per pan, and keys 1 to 4 for donor then receiver. Skip art: just count perfect and lost dishes, and use the demo's tilted dealer.

Give it to a friend and say only "serve the dishes perfectly". The one question: do they ever take seconds from a pan that was already fine, on purpose, to save another? That loan is the whole game. If they only ever fix pairs that cancel, the dealer is too kind; deal more lopsided dishes and test again.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: four pans, one service of twelve dishes, the tilted dealer, and a score of perfect dishes.
  • One-month version: a week of services; dishes that cannot donate (a soufflé), dishes with wider windows (a stew), a fifth pan unlocked mid-week, and tables whose dishes must all go out together.
  • Cut first: customers, tips, upgrades and decoration. If moving seconds is not fun with four dots, none of that will save it.
  • Hardest part: the dealer. Spend your tuning time on how new dishes are chosen, not on the timers.
