Parcel Loop: deliver before the town forgets

A time loop delivery game where a small town resets every minute and only your memory of everyone's schedule carries over. Playable prototype and Lua code.

You are the courier of a town stuck in the same sixty seconds. Three parcels, three people who are only out of doors for a few seconds each, and when the minute ends the parcels are back on your counter and nobody remembers you, so the only thing you get to keep is knowing where everyone will be.

The core loop: watch, remember, intercept

The town is a small grid of streets seen from above. You carry three parcels, one each for Ada, Bram and Cleo. A parcel is handed over the moment you stand next to its owner while they are out on the street. Indoors they are out of reach.

It is dusk, so you only see people within a few blocks of your lantern. The houses are marked on the map, but not when anyone comes out. That gap is the game. A time loop is a stretch of time that repeats from the same starting state; here it lasts sixty seconds, and it resets everything except the player.

Every ten seconds you are walking a street, scanning the edge of your light and asking "who should be near here, right now?" Every minute you play one loop: a scouting run while you are still learning, a planned route once you know enough. Every session is a week of towns, each with new parcels for the same people, so the schedule you learned on Monday pays off again on Tuesday.

The rules

  • The town runs on a script. Each resident follows a fixed schedule: a list of times and street corners, with stretches spent indoors. The schedule is identical in every loop.
  • Hand-over is automatic. Come within 0.7 of a tile of an outdoor resident and their parcel is delivered. No button, no menu: being in the right place at the right time is the whole skill.
  • The clock waits for you. A loop starts on your first step, so you can always plan before you move.
  • Sixty seconds, then reset. At 60 seconds the town, the residents and your parcels return to where they began, and you stand at the post office again.
  • Goal. Deliver all three parcels inside one loop. After that, deliver them sooner.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the local who knows the town better than it knows itself: you are on the corner when Cleo opens her door, every time. The constraint is that nothing you do survives the reset. There are no upgrades, no keys, no map markers. Progress lives only in the player's head, so the first loop is a scouting trip and the fifth is a performance.

Delivery is a good fit for this because it turns the schedule into a route-planning puzzle with no combat or dialogue to write. Three people with short outdoor windows in different corners already force a choice of order.

The hardest part to build is not the code. It is authoring schedules that are learnable: windows short enough to need a plan, long enough to hit once you have one, and placed so that at least two orders work. Expect to spend most of your time with a timeline sheet, not a code editor.

Try it

Treat the first loop as a scouting run: walk around, see who appears where and at what time on the clock. When the town resets, use what you saw to plan a route that catches all three. If you get stuck, switch off the fog to watch everyone at once.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: a schedule lookup and a reset that forgets everything except the loop count. Each schedule is a list of keyframes, moments where the resident is at a known corner. Between two keyframes they walk in a straight line; after a keyframe marked inside they are behind a door until the next one. Drawing and grid walking are left out. The names and numbers are the demo's.

local LOOP, REACH = 60, 0.7
local residents = {
  { name = "Ada",  keys = { {t=0, c=0, r=0}, {t=9, c=6, r=0, inside=true} } },
  { name = "Bram", keys = { {t=0, c=12, r=2, inside=true}, {t=20, c=12, r=2},
                            {t=22, c=12, r=4}, {t=32, c=6, r=4, inside=true} } },
  { name = "Cleo", keys = { {t=0, c=2, r=8, inside=true}, {t=26, c=2, r=8}, {t=28, c=0, r=8},
                            {t=31, c=0, r=6, inside=true}, {t=48, c=10, r=0}, {t=50, c=8, r=0},
                            {t=53, c=8, r=3, inside=true} } },
}
local courier = { x = 6, y = 8 }
local clock, loopNumber, waiting, delivered = 0, 1, true, {}
local function moveCourier(dt) end        -- walk the street grid at 3.5 tiles per second
local function positionAt(keys, t)
  local k = 1
  while keys[k + 1] and keys[k + 1].t <= t do k = k + 1 end
  local a, b = keys[k], keys[k + 1]
  if a.inside then return nil end         -- indoors: nobody to hand a parcel to
  if not b then return a.c, a.r end
  local f = (t - a.t) / (b.t - a.t)
  return a.c + (b.c - a.c) * f, a.r + (b.r - a.r) * f
end
local function resetTown()
  clock, delivered, waiting = 0, {}, true -- the town forgets...
  courier.x, courier.y = 6, 8
  loopNumber = loopNumber + 1             -- ...and only the count of loops goes on
end
function love.update(dt)
  if waiting then return end              -- the clock starts on your first step
  clock = clock + dt
  if clock >= LOOP then resetTown() return end
  moveCourier(dt)
  for i, p in ipairs(residents) do
    local x, y = positionAt(p.keys, clock)
    if x and not delivered[i] and (x - courier.x)^2 + (y - courier.y)^2 < REACH^2 then
      delivered[i] = true
    end
  end
end
function love.keypressed(key) waiting = false end

The highlighted function is the whole town. Because a position is computed from the clock, not stepped forward frame by frame, the town cannot drift between loops: second 26 is the same second 26 every time. That is what makes the player's memory trustworthy, and it lets you jump to any moment for testing by setting clock directly.

Pitfalls

  • The first loop feels like a waste. If nothing happens during scouting, players feel punished for not knowing. Make the first loop give something: in the demo, a player who heads north meets Ada almost at once. Test it by watching a new player's first loop: do they deliver at least one parcel?
  • Memory overload. Three people with one or two windows each is easy to hold in your head; ten people with five windows each is homework. If testers start writing notes on paper, either add an in-game notebook or cut people. Count how many windows a player can recall after two loops.
  • Waiting is the best strategy. If the winning plan is "stand at a door for twenty seconds", the loop turns dull. Space windows so the good route keeps you moving. Time your own best run and note how many seconds you stood still.
  • A single solution. When only one order works, the puzzle becomes a lock with one key and replays add nothing. Aim for at least two winning orders per town. Check with a schedule sheet like the one above before you build the level.
  • Unfair misses. A parcel missed by a hair after a 50 second plan stings. A generous reach (0.7 of a tile here) and a clear delivery flash matter more than they seem.

In real games

Time loops where knowledge is the progress are well explored. In Outer Wilds the solar system resets after about twenty-two minutes and what you learned is all you keep. The Legend of Zelda: Majora's Mask repeats three days in a town whose residents follow their own schedules, and helping them means being in the right place at the right hour. Minit gives you sixty seconds per life.

Parcel Loop takes that idea and shrinks it to one minute, one map and one verb. There is no story to uncover and nothing to collect between loops: the schedule itself is the puzzle, and delivery is simply the reason to be on a corner at a time. I have not checked every small time loop game, so a design very close to this may already exist.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with a grid of streets, circles for the residents, a square for the courier and the loop clock printed on screen. Skip the fog at first. Write one town with three residents on paper as a schedule sheet before you type a number.

Give it to a friend with no explanation beyond "deliver the parcels". The one question: on their second loop, do they go somewhere on purpose before they can see anyone there? If they do, they are playing from memory and the idea works. If they only react to what is on screen, add the fog, or make the windows shorter.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: one town, three residents, three parcels, fog, a loop clock and a best delivery time.
  • One-month version: five towns across a week, residents who react when you deliver (a delivered parcel changes where someone goes next), and an optional notebook that fills with sightings.
  • Cut first: dialogue, story and any upgrades that persist between loops. They weaken the rule that only your memory carries over.
  • Hardest part: schedule authoring. Build a small timeline view of every resident early; it is your level editor.
