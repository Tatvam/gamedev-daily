Paper Plane Wind Tunnel: tune the fold, then let go

A tinkering game where you tune a paper plane's wing, nose weight and throw to land on targets, with a playable prototype and the flight code in Lua.

It is the last day of term, the school hall is empty, and you have a stack of paper and a box of paper clips. Your plane swoops up, kisses the ceiling and drops at your feet. One more clip on the nose and a gentler throw, and it glides the whole length of the hall straight into the chalk circle.

The core loop: fold, throw, compare

Paper Plane Wind Tunnel is a tuning game. You never steer the plane in the air. Before each throw you set three things, then you let go and watch. The flight path stays on screen as a faint ghost (a copy of the last attempt), so every new throw is compared with the one before it.

Every ten seconds the player changes one setting and throws. Every minute they land a target and move to a further one. Every session they work through a new room with a new set of targets and a new limit on paper.

The rules

  • Wing angle. How far the wings are bent up against the airflow, from 0 to 16 degrees. More angle means more lift (the push from the air at right angles to the flight) and more drag (the push against the flight). Past 12 degrees the wing stalls: the air stops flowing smoothly over it and most of the lift vanishes.
  • Nose clips. Zero to three paper clips. Each clip tips the nose down by 2 degrees and makes the plane 25 percent heavier, so it needs more speed to fly.
  • Throw strength. Launch speed from 3 to 12 metres per second, always from shoulder height and slightly upwards.
  • The hall. 24 metres long with a ceiling at 3.5 metres. A plane that touches the ceiling loses half its speed.
  • The goal. Land in four floor targets in order, at 4, 8, 12 and 16 metres. Each throw uses one sheet of paper. Your score is the number of sheets, and lower is better.

The interesting part is that the three settings fight each other. A hard throw does not simply go further. With a lot of lift, extra speed turns into climb: the plane swoops up, slows, drops its nose and dives, a rise-and-fall pattern pilots call the phugoid. In a low hall that swoop ends at the ceiling. So "throw harder" is often the wrong answer, and "add a clip, then throw harder" is the right one.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the schoolyard expert who knows exactly which fold flies furthest. The constraint is that you only touch the plane before it leaves your hand, and each setting changes more than one thing. That makes every throw a small experiment, and the ghost turns it into a fair test. The mood is quiet and playful: no timer, no enemies, just paper and a long empty room.

The hardest part to build is the flight model's tuning, not its code. The physics is a dozen lines. Making a range of settings where most changes give a readable result, with a few surprises (stalls, ceiling bumps) and no settings that are useless, took most of the time on this prototype. Plan for a spreadsheet or a script that flies every combination and prints where each one lands.

Try it

Press Throw with the starting settings. The plane overshoots the 4 metre target. Before you throw again, change only one slider, then watch the new path against the dashed ghost. Later, try 12 degrees of wing with no clips and a strong throw, and see what the ceiling does to it.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: turning the three settings into a plane, then moving it one fixed step at a time. Units are metres and seconds, with y pointing up. Q rolls air density and wing area into one tuning number.

local G, Q, STEP = 9.8, 0.6, 1 / 120
local STALL, CD0, K = 12, 0.06, 0.12        -- stall angle, base drag, drag from lift
local NOSE, CLIP_MASS, CEILING = 2, 0.25, 3.5
local LAUNCH = math.rad(10)                 -- thrown slightly upwards

local function liftCoef(alpha)              -- alpha in degrees
  if alpha > STALL then return 0.5 end      -- stalled: most lift is gone
  return 0.1 * alpha
end

local function throw(wing, clips, power)
  local alpha = wing - NOSE * clips
  local cl = liftCoef(alpha)
  return { x = 0.3, y = 1.6, landed = false,
           vx = power * math.cos(LAUNCH), vy = power * math.sin(LAUNCH),
           mass = 1 + CLIP_MASS * clips, cl = cl, cd = CD0 + K * cl * cl }
end

local function stepPlane(p)
  local speed = math.sqrt(p.vx * p.vx + p.vy * p.vy)
  local k = Q * speed / p.mass              -- force grows with speed squared
  local ax = k * (-p.cd * p.vx - p.cl * p.vy)       -- drag back, lift sideways
  local ay = k * (-p.cd * p.vy + p.cl * p.vx) - G
  p.vx, p.vy = p.vx + ax * STEP, p.vy + ay * STEP
  p.x, p.y = p.x + p.vx * STEP, p.y + p.vy * STEP
  if p.y > CEILING then                     -- bumped the ceiling
    p.y = CEILING
    if p.vy > 0 then p.vy = 0 end
    p.vx = p.vx * 0.5
  end
  if p.y <= 0 then p.y, p.landed = 0, true end
end

local plane = throw(6, 1, 6)                -- the demo's starting settings
local acc = 0
function love.update(dt)
  acc = math.min(acc + dt, 0.1)
  while acc >= STEP do
    if not plane.landed then stepPlane(plane) end
    acc = acc - STEP
  end
end

The highlighted lines are the mechanic. Lift is the velocity turned a quarter turn, so it always acts across the flight path, never along it. Because alpha stays fixed during a flight, a fast plane gets so much lift that it climbs until it slows down, which is where the swoop comes from. The demo also bounces the plane off the back wall and stops it at the far wall, which this snippet leaves out. With the starting settings it lands at about 7.2 metres.

Pitfalls

  • Settings that feel like a combination lock. If only one exact combination hits a target, players stop reasoning and start trying every value. In this prototype the 16 metre target can be hit by about 20 slider combinations out of roughly 1,300. That is tight on purpose for the last target; the first three accept well over a hundred each. Test: write a script that throws every combination and counts the hits per target before you ever playtest.
  • Chaos near the ceiling. Flights that scrape the ceiling land in places that are hard to predict from the settings. Some players enjoy that; others feel cheated when a ceiling bump scores a hit. Watch whether testers try to use the ceiling on purpose. If nobody does, raise it or make a bump always end the flight.
  • Changing two things at once. Players who move every slider between throws learn nothing from the ghost. Count how many sliders a tester changes per throw. If it is usually more than one, consider letting the player change only one setting per sheet.
  • Hidden numbers. Angle of attack is never shown, so a stall can feel random. The prototype prints "Stalled" during the flight. If testers still cannot explain a sudden drop, show a small side view of the plane's nose angle before each throw.

In real games

Setting values and then watching a result you cannot touch is old territory. Artillery games such as Scorched Earth ask for an angle and a power, then let you correct your next shot from where the last one landed. Golf games work the same way with club and swing. Kerbal Space Program takes the build-then-launch idea much further, with whole craft designed before each flight.

Paper Plane Wind Tunnel sits between those. Like an artillery game it has a few settings and a landing spot, but you are not aiming: you are changing how the projectile flies, and the settings interact in ways a straight line of power cannot. More power can mean a shorter flight.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with three keys for each setting (up and down), a throw key, a single floor target and the ghost of the last path. Draw paths with love.graphics.line from a list of stored points.

Give it to a friend with only "land on the mark". The one question to answer: after a miss, can they say out loud which setting they will change and why? If they can, the settings read clearly and the idea is worth building. If they shrug and move sliders at random, the effects are too tangled: try removing nose clips and see whether two settings are enough.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: one hall, eight targets including one on a table and one through an open window, a sheet count per target and a best score.
  • One-month version: new fold shapes that change the lift and drag numbers, rooms with fans and open doors that add a steady breeze, and a fold book that records the settings of every successful throw.
  • Cut first: real rotation physics. A plane that tumbles and recovers looks great, but a fixed angle of attack already gives swoops and stalls for a fraction of the work.
  • The mechanic is the comparison, not the flight. Every feature should make it easier to see what one change did.

Next

Further reading

  • Frederick W. Lanchester, Aerodonetics (1908). The book that named and described the phugoid, the swoop-and-dive motion of a glider.
  • NASA Glenn Research Center, Beginner's Guide to Aeronautics. Plain explanations of lift, drag, angle of attack and stall.
  • Glenn Fiedler, Fix Your Timestep! (Gaffer On Games). Why a fixed physics step makes the same settings land in the same place every time.
  • Steve Swink, Game Feel: A Game Designer's Guide to Virtual Sensation (Morgan Kaufmann, 2008). On making a physical response readable to the player.
