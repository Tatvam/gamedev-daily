One-Switch Lift: run a building with a single lever

A one-button management game where the lift drives itself and your only control flips its direction, with a playable prototype and the lift logic in Lua.

You are the lift operator in an old five-storey department store, and the lift drives itself: it stops wherever someone needs it and turns round at the top and bottom. Your lever does one thing, it sends the car the other way. Everyone in the building is waiting on that one choice.

The core loop: read the building, choose a direction

One-Switch Lift is a one-button game: the only input is a single press. The lift moves between floors at a steady speed. It stops by itself when a rider wants to get out, or when someone is waiting and there is room. At the top and bottom floors it turns round by itself. A press reverses the lift right away, even between floors.

Every passenger has a patience timer, a countdown that starts when they arrive and keeps running while they ride. Fast trips earn a bigger tip. When the timer runs out the store gets a complaint. Three complaints and you are sent home. Your score is the tips you collect.

The rules

  • Movement. The lift travels 1.25 floors per second, so one floor takes 0.8 seconds. Each stop keeps the doors open for 1 second.
  • Stops. The lift stops at a floor if a rider wants to get out there, or if someone is waiting there and the car has fewer than 4 riders. Riders get out first, then waiting passengers get in, whichever way they want to go.
  • The lever. A press reverses the direction. During a stop it changes the way the lift will leave. Pressing at the top or bottom floor does nothing, because there is only one way to go.
  • Patience and tips. Each passenger has 20 seconds for the whole journey, waiting and riding together. Delivered in under 10 seconds, they tip 2; under 20, they tip 1. A waiting passenger whose time runs out takes the stairs and complains. A rider whose time runs out complains and tips nothing.
  • The sale floor. One of floors 2 to 4 has a sale, shown as a tinted band, and it moves every 30 seconds. 45 percent of new passengers go from the ground floor to the sale, 25 percent from the sale back down, and the rest travel between random floors.
  • Busier over time. A new passenger arrives every 3.6 seconds at first, and 0.08 seconds sooner for each delivery, down to one every 1.2 seconds. At most four people wait on one floor; more than that and newcomers do not join the queue.

One decision, worked through

Here is the kind of moment the game is made of. The lift is at floor 3 going up. Inside, one rider wants floor 1. On floor 2 a fresh shopper wants floor 1 too. On floor 5 someone else heading for floor 1 has 4 seconds of patience left before they take the stairs.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the operator who knows the building better than any machine. The constraint is that you never choose where to stop, only which way to face. That keeps every press meaningful: there is nothing to do with your hand except decide.

Never pressing at all is a real strategy. The lift then sweeps the whole building top to bottom, which is a fair schedule. The game is about beating that sweep: shuttling between the ground floor and the sale instead of riding to an empty top floor, or going the long way to rescue someone about to give up. The tips reward the first habit and the complaints punish forgetting the second.

The hardest part to build is not the movement but readability: with four riders and up to twenty people waiting, the player must see at a glance where everyone is going and who is about to leave.

Try it

Press to open the store, then let the lift run for a while without touching anything. Once you see how it sweeps, start reversing early when nobody needs the floors ahead, and shuttle between the ground floor and the sale floor. Watch the colours: red bars and red riders are your next decision.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: moving between floors, deciding whether to stop, turning at the ends, and the lever. Floors are numbered 0 to 4 in code and shown as 1 to 5. The names and numbers match the prototype. serveFloor is your own function that lets riders out, adds their tips and lets waiting passengers in; the patience timers are a plain loop that adds dt to each person's wait.

local FLOORS, SPEED, DOOR_TIME, CAP = 5, 1.25, 1.0, 4
local lift = { pos = 0, dir = 1, doors = 0, riders = {} }
local waiting = { {}, {}, {}, {}, {} }    -- waiting[f + 1] holds people on floor f

local function wantsStop(f)
  for _, p in ipairs(lift.riders) do
    if p.target == f then return true end    -- someone gets out here
  end
  return #waiting[f + 1] > 0 and #lift.riders < CAP
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key ~= "space" then return end
  local d = -lift.dir
  local blocked = (lift.pos <= 0 and d == -1) or (lift.pos >= FLOORS - 1 and d == 1)
  if not blocked then lift.dir = d end       -- the only control
end

function love.update(dt)
  if lift.doors > 0 then
    lift.doors = lift.doors - dt
    serveFloor(lift.pos)                     -- out first, then in
    return
  end
  local nextPos = lift.pos + lift.dir * SPEED * dt
  local f = lift.dir == 1 and math.floor(lift.pos) + 1 or math.ceil(lift.pos) - 1
  if (lift.dir == 1 and nextPos >= f) or (lift.dir == -1 and nextPos <= f) then
    nextPos = f                              -- arrive exactly on the floor
    if wantsStop(f) then lift.doors = DOOR_TIME end
    if f == 0 or f == FLOORS - 1 then lift.dir = -lift.dir end
  end
  lift.pos = nextPos
end

The floor f is always the next one ahead, so the floor the lift just left is never checked again unless you reverse back through it. One frame moves the lift far less than a floor, so it crosses at most one floor per update; if dt can grow large, cap it first.

Pitfalls

  • Doing nothing wins. If the automatic sweep scores as well as a thoughtful player, the button is pointless. This happened while building the prototype above: with passengers spread evenly over the floors and a score of one point per delivery, a script that never pressed did about as well as a script that turned back whenever nothing was ahead. Uneven demand (the sale floor) and tips for speed opened a gap: over eight seeded runs the turning script averaged roughly 30 percent more tips. Test this early with two simple scripts before you test with people.
  • Too much to read. The decision depends on many small numbers. If players press at random, they cannot see the situation. Try colouring people by direction, or showing only the most urgent patience bar on each floor.
  • Flip-flopping. Players under stress may press twice by accident and lose track of which way the lift is going. Make the direction arrow large and give each press a clear visual response.
  • Unfair spikes. A burst of shoppers on a far floor can end a run with no way to save it. The prototype caps each queue at four; spacing out arrivals helps too.

In real games

Lifts have a long history in games as both setting and system. Taito's arcade game Elevator Action (1983) used lifts as moving platforms in an action game. Maxis's SimTower (1994) made building and running a tower's lifts part of a management game. Elevator Saga is a browser programming game where you write code to control lifts. One-Switch Lift is closer to the last idea than the first two, but takes away the code and the menus and leaves one live decision.

Outside games, operating systems textbooks describe a disk scheduling method called SCAN, also called the elevator algorithm: the disk head sweeps one way across the disk, then back. Left alone, this lift does the same, and the player's job is to beat it.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with rectangles for the car and floors, circles for people with their target floor written on them, and a patience bar under each waiting person. Spawn people on a timer with a seeded random generator, and log every press with the time and the lift's position.

Give it to someone and say only "the button reverses the lift". The one question to answer: can the player explain why they pressed? Ask after each run. If the answer is "someone was about to leave" or "nobody needed the top floors", the decision is readable. If it is "I don't know, it felt slow", the screen is hiding the information they need.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: the prototype above with a lift-bell flash on every delivery, a short shift timer instead of endless play, and a high score. My scripted test runs lasted three to seven minutes, long for one session.
  • One-month version: a week of shifts with different rush patterns built on the sale floor idea (morning arrivals, lunch on the café floor, closing time all going down), passengers with different patience, and a second car with its own lever.
  • Cut first: the second lift. The rush patterns already give each day a new puzzle with the same single control.
  • The hardest part is readability: the player must see every destination and every patience bar at a glance.
