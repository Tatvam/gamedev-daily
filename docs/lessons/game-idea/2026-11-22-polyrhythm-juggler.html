Polyrhythm Juggler: two hands, two tempos

A juggling rhythm game where each ball falls on its own period, so your two hands keep two different beats at once. Playable prototype and Lua code.

Your left hand catches a high, slow ball every second and a half. Your right hand catches a low, quick one every second. Neither hand can follow the other, and when a third ball starts crossing between them, you have to stop counting and feel the pattern.

The core loop: two rhythms, one body

A polyrhythm is two or more steady beats of different speeds played at once. The simplest is three against two: while one beat counts three, the other counts two. It is hard to tap with two hands at first and gets easier with practice. That learning is the game.

Each ball has a home hand and a period, the time from one catch to the next. You press the left or right key as a ball lands in that hand, and the catch throws it back up. There is no scrolling track of notes: the ball is the note, and its height tells you its tempo.

Every ten seconds you catch about sixteen balls and see on the accuracy bars which hand drifts. Every minute you earn the third ball with a streak, lose it on a drop, and earn it back. Every session is a set of routines for a crowd, each with a new ratio such as four against three.

The rules

  • Two balls, two periods. The left ball returns every 1.5 seconds and the right ball every 1.0 second. Both land together every 3 seconds.
  • The catch window. A press within 0.15 seconds of a landing catches the ball. Within 0.06 seconds it is a perfect catch worth 3 points; otherwise it is worth 1.
  • Throws stay on schedule. An early or late catch does not shift the next landing, so errors show on the bar instead of piling up.
  • Pressing at nothing breaks the streak. A press with no ball in that hand's window is a fumble: it resets the streak and costs 2 points, so mashing never pays.
  • A streak of 12 adds a third ball. It crosses between the hands every 1.25 seconds.
  • A drop costs a life and the third ball. The dropped ball rejoins after one cycle. Five drops end the show; a streak of 40 wins it.

Height is tempo

Under constant gravity g, a ball in the air for T seconds rises g·T²/8, so twice the period means four times the height. The prototype uses one gravity for every ball:

local t = math.max(0, (time - b.thrown) / b.period)  -- 0 at the throw, 1 at the landing
local y = MAX_H * (b.period / 1.5)^2 * 4 * t * (1 - t)

Because every ball falls the same way, the eye learns one sense of "about to land" and uses it for all of them.

What makes it different

Most rhythm games put every note on one shared timeline. Here each hand has its own, and the skill is independence: keeping one hand steady while the other does something else. The fantasy is the street performer who makes it look effortless. The constraint is that the balls, not you, set the tempo.

The hardest part to build is the feel of the catch window. Input lag and how the ball is drawn at the landing both change whether a catch feels fair.

Try it

Catch each ball as it reaches its hand. Check the bars to see whether one hand rushes, then earn the third ball with a streak of 12 and feel the crossing ball break up the beat you had settled into.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: each ball stores when it lands, a key press checks only that hand's balls, and the next landing is one period after the last scheduled landing. Drawing, lives and the win check are left out. The names and numbers are the demo's.

local GOOD, PERFECT, UNIT = 0.15, 0.06, 0.25
local UNLOCK = 12
local KEYS = { f = "left", left = "left", j = "right", right = "right" }
local time, streak, score, drops = 0, 0, 0, 0
local balls = {
  { hand = "left",  period = 1.5, thrown = 0, arrives = 1.5, cross = false },
  { hand = "right", period = 1.0, thrown = 0, arrives = 1.0, cross = false },
}

local function rethrow(b)                 -- thrown back by the hand that caught it
  if b.cross then b.hand = (b.hand == "left") and "right" or "left" end
  b.thrown = b.arrives                   -- on schedule, early or late
  b.arrives = b.arrives + b.period
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  local hand, caught = KEYS[key], false
  if not hand then return end
  for _, b in ipairs(balls) do
    local off = time - b.arrives         -- below zero: early, above zero: late
    if b.hand == hand and math.abs(off) <= GOOD then
      caught, streak = true, streak + 1
      score = score + ((math.abs(off) <= PERFECT) and 3 or 1)
      rethrow(b)
    end
  end
  if not caught then                     -- a fumble: pressing at nothing
    streak, score = 0, math.max(0, score - 2)
  end
  if streak >= UNLOCK and #balls == 2 then
    local t = math.ceil(time / UNIT + 0.2) * UNIT   -- join on the beat grid
    balls[3] = { hand = "left", period = 1.25, thrown = t, arrives = t + 1.25, cross = true }
  end
end

function love.update(dt)
  time = time + dt
  local dropped = false
  for _, b in ipairs(balls) do
    if time > b.arrives + GOOD then      -- it fell past the hand
      drops, streak, dropped = drops + 1, 0, true
      b.arrives = b.arrives + b.period   -- it lies on the floor for one cycle
      rethrow(b)
    end
  end
  if dropped then balls[3] = nil end     -- the third ball goes back in the bag
end

Every period is a whole number of UNIT steps (6, 4 and 5 of them), and the third ball joins on that grid. So two landings in one hand either coincide, and one press catches both, or are at least a quarter of a second apart. Without the grid, near misses between landings would cost balls for no reason the player could see.

Pitfalls

  • It is simply too hard for many people. If new players quit in the first minute, the game never gets to teach them. Offer a mode where one hand plays itself while the player learns the other. Time how long non-musicians take to reach a streak of 12.
  • The eyes cannot follow two arcs. If the hands are far apart, the player misses whichever ball they are not looking at. Keep both arcs inside one glance. If a tester's head moves left and right, the arcs are too far apart.
  • Mashing wins. Without the fumble rule, pressing both keys constantly catches everything. Mash for thirty seconds yourself: you should lose the streak quickly.
  • Lag looks like bad timing. If every tester's bars lean late by the same amount, the problem is input or display lag, not the players. Add a calibration screen before tuning the window.
  • The third ball feels like noise. If the crossing ball's rhythm does not fit the other two, the pattern stops being learnable. Keep all periods on one grid and the cycle short. Here the three-ball pattern repeats every 15 seconds; if testers find it chaotic, try a shorter cycle.

In real games

Rhythm games usually give the player one chart to follow, even when several limbs play it, as on the drum kit controller of Rock Band. Polyrhythm itself is old and common in music, for example in West African drumming and in classical piano music.

Jugglers write patterns in siteswap notation, where each throw is a number of beats. In the usual two-handed form, where the hands take turns, odd numbers cross to the other hand and even numbers return to the same hand. This game borrows the idea that a throw's height is a length of time, but not siteswap's rules, since its hands do not take turns. I do not know of a game built on two hands keeping two separate periods, but I have not checked every rhythm game, so something close may exist.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with two circles for hands and two balls drawn with the height formula, and print each catch's offset in milliseconds. Leave out the third ball and lives.

Hand it to three people for two minutes each. The one question: after two minutes, can they tap the combined pattern on the table without the game? If they can, the game teaches a real skill. If they only play while staring at the balls, they are reacting rather than feeling the rhythm: slow the tempo or bring the arcs closer, and test again.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: two hands, two balls, three against two, the catch window, fumbles, drops and the accuracy bars.
  • One-month version: routines with new ratios, a lag calibration screen, music that plays the combined pattern, and a crowd that reacts to streaks.
  • Cut first: extra hands, power-ups and juggling tricks. Every new kind of ball must fit the grid and be learnable.
  • Hardest part: making the catch feel fair. Build the accuracy bars before tuning any numbers.
