Drawbridge Duty: one bridge, two queues, one button

A one-button traffic game where holding raises a bridge for boats and releasing lowers it for carts, with a playable prototype and the bridge rules in Lua.

You run the only bridge in town. Boats pile up on the river and carts pile up on the road, and you have a single lever. Hold it and the bridge rises. Let go and it comes down. Everyone is in a hurry, and nobody can cross while the other side is crossing.

The core loop: watch, choose a side, let them through

Drawbridge Duty is a queue juggling game: two lines of customers want one shared resource, the bridge, and the player decides who gets it and for how long.

The button is not just "up" or "down". Holding raises the bridge a little at a time, and boats need different amounts of room. A rowboat slips under a bridge raised a quarter of the way; a tall ship needs it almost fully open. So the question is not only who goes next but how high to go, because every bit of height costs time on the way down.

The rules

The bridge has a lift, a number from 0 (flat, road open) to 1 (fully raised). Holding the button raises it to full in 1.2 seconds. Releasing lowers it to flat in 1 second. Each boat has a mast height on the same scale, and it may pass only when the lift is at least that high.

  • Carts drive onto the bridge only when it is completely flat.
  • Boats stop at the buoys and wait until the lift reaches their mast height.
  • No accidents. While a cart is on the deck the bridge will not rise, and while a boat is under it the bridge will not drop below that mast. The game shows "locked" instead of punishing you, so the only enemy is time.
  • Patience. Boats share one patience bar and carts share another. Each bar drains by 0.04 per second for every vehicle stuck waiting on that side. Each vehicle that crosses refills its side by 0.12.
  • The end. When either bar empties, the run is over. Your score is the number of vehicles that crossed. Traffic arrives faster the longer you last.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is the bridge keeper: one small person whose single lever controls a whole town's traffic. The constraint is that the button costs time in both directions, and time spent moving the bridge serves nobody. Good play means grouping vehicles: let three carts roll over in a row, then open once for two boats.

The boat heights turn an on and off switch into a judgement of degree. A rowboat is a cheap favour. A tall ship is a big decision, because opening that high shuts the road for more than two seconds.

The hardest part to build is the queue movement: each vehicle must stop at the line, follow the one in front without overlapping, and know when it has committed to crossing. The locks and patience bars both depend on it.

The code

This is the heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: the lift, the rule at the stop line, the locks, and the two patience bars. The numbers and names are the ones the prototype uses. Moving the queues and drawing are your own functions and are left out.

local RAISE, LOWER = 1 / 1.2, 1 / 1.0  -- lift per second: up in 1.2 s, down in 1 s
local DRAIN, REFILL = 0.04, 0.12       -- per waiting vehicle per second, per crossing
local lift, score = 0, 0               -- lift 0 = flat for carts, 1 = fully raised
local patience = { boat = 1, cart = 1 }

local function mayEnter(v)             -- asked when a vehicle reaches its stop line
  if v.kind == "cart" then return lift <= 0.01 end
  return lift >= v.mast                -- rowboat 0.25, sailboat 0.55, tall ship 0.9
end

local function updateBridge(dt, cartOnDeck, mastUnder)
  local holding = love.keyboard.isDown("space") or love.mouse.isDown(1)
  if holding and not cartOnDeck then
    lift = math.min(1, lift + RAISE * dt)
  elseif not holding and lift > mastUnder then
    lift = math.max(mastUnder, lift - LOWER * dt)  -- never drop onto a mast
  end
end

local function updatePatience(dt, waitingBoats, waitingCarts)
  patience.boat = patience.boat - DRAIN * waitingBoats * dt
  patience.cart = patience.cart - DRAIN * waitingCarts * dt
  return patience.boat <= 0 or patience.cart <= 0  -- true ends the run
end

local function crossed(v)              -- call once when a vehicle clears the bridge
  score = score + 1
  patience[v.kind] = math.min(1, patience[v.kind] + REFILL)
end

function love.update(dt)
  local cartOnDeck, mastUnder = scanDeck()     -- mastUnder is 0 when no boat is under
  updateBridge(dt, cartOnDeck, mastUnder)
  local wb, wc = moveQueues(dt, mayEnter, crossed)
  if updatePatience(dt, wb, wc) then gameOver() end
end

moveQueues moves every vehicle forward, stops it behind the one in front or at the line when mayEnter says no, and returns how many on each side are stuck. Count only stuck vehicles: a cart still driving up to the bridge is not annoyed yet.

Pitfalls

  • One side always wins. If boats are rare, the best strategy is to keep the bridge down and open only when a boat bar gets low, and the game turns into waiting. Log how much of each run the bridge spends flat. If it is over 80 percent, add boats or make carts arrive in bursts.
  • No real choice. With light traffic you serve everyone and the game is a chore. The fun is when both bars are falling and you must pick. Tune spawn rates until that happens within 30 seconds.
  • Unfair failure. If a bar can empty while the player does everything right, the game feels rigged. Check the worst case: four carts waiting while a tall ship crosses drains 0.16 per second for about four seconds. That hurts but can be recovered. Recompute it whenever you change a number.
  • Invisible locks. When the bridge refuses to move, players think the button is broken. Show the lock clearly in the moment, in words and colour, as the prototype does.

In real games

Drawbridge Duty belongs to a family of games about serving queues against a patience meter. Diner Dash (PlayFirst, 2004) has the player seat and serve restaurant customers whose patience runs out, and Mini Metro (Dinosaur Polo Club, 2015) ends the game when a station becomes too crowded. Both are about spreading limited attention across many demands.

The mechanism is real: a bascule bridge has a deck that tips up on a hinge to let ships pass, and Tower Bridge in London is a famous example. The timings in this game are invented for play.

What this idea changes is that the whole game is one shared resource with continuous height, so the button is a dial rather than a switch. I do not know of a published game built on exactly this rule, but I have not searched thoroughly, so check before calling it new.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with rectangles only: a blue band for the river, a grey strip for the road, carts as squares, boats as long rectangles with a number for their mast height. Use only rowboats and sailboats. Print the time the bridge spends flat at the end of each run.

Hand it to someone and say only "keep everyone moving". The one question to answer: in the first minute, does the player ever hesitate over which side to serve? If they never hesitate, traffic is too light or too lopsided, so tune the spawn rates before adding anything. If they hesitate and then smile or groan, the core works.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: the prototype above with a creaking bridge sound that plays only after the player turns sound on, simple drivers and sailors who wave when served, and a saved best score.
  • One-month version: a week of shifts with different traffic, such as market day full of carts or a regatta full of tall ships, plus special vehicles like a doctor's cart that must not wait at all.
  • Cut first: a second bridge. It sounds like a natural sequel, but it doubles the attention load and breaks the one-button promise.
  • The hardest part is the queue movement. Build the stop line and the following rule before any art.
