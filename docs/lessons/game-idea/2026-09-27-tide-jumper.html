Tide Jumper: a runner where the button moves the sea

A one-button runner where the button never jumps. Holding it raises the sea, and your floating hero rides the tide over walls and under ceilings.

A small hero on a raft runs along a flooded canyon, and you cannot make them jump. All you have is the sea: hold the button and the water swells to carry them over a wall of spikes, let go and it drains so they duck under the next one.

The core loop: read, hold, float

Most one-button runners give the button to the hero. Here the button belongs to the world. The runner moves right by itself at a steady speed and always sits on the surface of the water. The tide is the height of that surface, from the floor of the canyon to just under its roof. It is the only thing the player controls.

  • Hold and the tide rises at a steady rate.
  • Release and it drains, a little slower than it rose.
  • The tide does not snap to a new speed. It eases in over about a sixth of a second, which makes it feel like water rather than a lift.

The rules

The canyon has three kinds of obstacle, each lined with spikes on the side that faces the runner. Touching any spike ends the run.

The goal is distance. The speed creeps up from 1.6 to 2.6 world units per second, so the gaps between decisions shrink. The course is generated from a fixed seed, so it is the same every run and players get better by learning it.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is being the moon: you are the force that carries the hero. A jump is a single event; the tide is a continuous state, a value that keeps its history. Where the sea is now depends on everything you did in the last second, so the game is about planning a curve rather than timing a tap.

The constraint that makes it work is the gate. Walls and ceilings alone are just "hold" and "release". A gate asks you to hold the water still, which you can only do by tapping in a rhythm. That rhythm is the skill the whole game is built on.

The hardest part to build is not the code. It is the course generator: it must never place two obstacles so close that the tide cannot physically travel between their safe bands in time.

Try it

Click or tap the canyon, then hold Space, Up or W, or keep your finger or mouse button pressed on the canvas. Nothing moves until your first press. The first three obstacles are a wall, a ceiling and a gate, one of each.

The code

This is the mechanic in LÖVE: the tide eases toward a rising or draining speed, the runner advances, and each obstacle is checked as a band of safe tide heights. The numbers are the ones the prototype uses. Heights are shares of the canyon (0 is the floor, 1 the roof), and distance is in world units.

local RISE, FALL = 0.9, -0.7  -- tide speed, canyon heights per second
local SIZE = 0.12             -- runner height as a share of the canyon
local TOP = 1 - SIZE          -- highest the tide can carry the runner
local HALF = 0.15             -- runner half width, world units

local tide, tideSpeed = 0.35, 0
local distance, alive = 0, true
local obstacles = {}  -- each like { x = 5, w = 0.5, lo = 0.3, hi = 1 }

local function holding()
  return love.keyboard.isDown("space", "up", "w") or love.mouse.isDown(1)
end

function love.update(dt)
  if not alive then return end
  -- the button never moves the runner, only the sea
  local target = holding() and RISE or FALL
  tideSpeed = tideSpeed + (target - tideSpeed) * math.min(1, dt * 6)
  tide = tide + tideSpeed * dt
  if tide < 0 or tide > TOP then
    tide = math.max(0, math.min(TOP, tide))
    tideSpeed = 0
  end
  local speed = math.min(2.6, 1.6 + distance * 0.012)
  distance = distance + speed * dt
  for _, o in ipairs(obstacles) do
    local overlaps = distance + HALF > o.x and distance - HALF < o.x + o.w
    if overlaps and (tide < o.lo or tide + SIZE > o.hi) then
      alive = false  -- touched the spikes below or above
    end
  end
end

A wall is { lo = 0.4, hi = 1 }, a ceiling is { lo = 0, hi = 0.6 }, and a gate has both limits. Drawing is left out: map a height h to the screen with floorY - h * canyonHeight and draw the runner with its bottom on the water line.

Pitfalls

  • Unfair gaps. If a low ceiling follows a tall wall too closely, no input can survive. When generating, work out the worst-case distance the tide has to travel between the two safe bands, divide by the drain or rise speed, add about half a second of reaction time, and never place the next obstacle closer than that. Test it with a bot that steers for the middle of each band: if it ever dies, the generator is wrong.
  • Water that feels like a lift. With no easing the sea moves like a platform on rails, and the fantasy of moving the sea is gone. With too much easing it feels sluggish and gates become luck. Put the easing rate on a slider and have three people play at 3, 6 and 12 before you choose.
  • Gates that are too narrow. Hovering is the core skill, but if the window is thinner than one tap's worth of movement, it becomes impossible. Measure how far the tide moves in the shortest tap a player makes and keep every window at least twice that.
  • Players expect a jump. Almost every one-button runner jumps, so the first press will surprise people. Because the run only starts on that first press, the sea is already rising when it begins. Put a low wall right after the start so the first hold is rewarded and the rule is learned by doing.
  • Only three shapes. Walls, ceilings and gates may go stale after ten minutes. New shapes should change the tide, not add buttons: a staircase of walls, a gate that drifts.

In real games

This idea sits between two well explored families. The first is the one-button auto-runner, where the character runs by itself and a single input decides everything. Canabalt (Adam Saltsman, 2009) is a well known example, with the button used to jump. Flappy Bird (2013) is another single-input game, where each tap lifts the bird against gravity, and it shares Tide Jumper's feel of holding a height with repeated taps.

The second family is games where you shape the world instead of steering a character. Tide Jumper borrows that indirect control but runs it in real time.

The difference is that the tide is shared by everything on screen. That opens design space a jump does not have: obstacles that float, enemies that drown, pickups that only surface at high water. I do not know of a published game built around a player-controlled sea level in a runner, but I have not searched exhaustively, so check before you announce it as a first.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build only what the Lua above does: a rectangle of water, a runner on its surface, and hand-placed walls and ceilings with no art. Skip the generator; type ten obstacles into a table. Add a key that cycles the easing rate between 3, 6 and 12.

Then give it to someone else without explaining the button. The one question to answer: does holding the sea still through a gate feel like a skill they want to get better at? If they die in the gates and immediately try again, you have a game. If they shrug, widen the gates or change the easing before building anything else.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: the prototype above with a seeded generator, a best distance saved to a file, a splash when the tide changes direction, and a death sound.
  • One-month version: ten hand-built stages, each adding one tide-shaped idea (floating crates that ride the same water, drains that pull the tide down), a daily seed, and a moon phase that sets how fast the sea can move.
  • Cut first: story, characters and multiple heroes. The whole game is the feel of the water, so spend the time there.
  • The hardest part is a fair generator. Check every gap against how fast the tide can move.

Next

Twist to try: let the sea rise and fall on its own on a slow tidal rhythm, and make the button only push against it, so the same wall is easy on the flood and hard on the ebb.

Further reading

  • Steve Swink, Game Feel: A Game Designer's Guide to Virtual Sensation (2008). On why the response curve of a control matters as much as what it does, which is the whole of the tide's easing.
  • Daniel Cook, The Chemistry of Game Design (2007). Describes games as loops of skill atoms, a useful way to reason about the read, hold, float loop.
  • Jesse Schell, The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses. Its lenses on simplicity and on the player's fantasy fit a one-mechanic game like this.
