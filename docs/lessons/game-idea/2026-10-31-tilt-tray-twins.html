Tilt Tray Twins: one tilt, two marbles, two holes

A physics puzzle where one tilting tray steers a slippery and a grippy marble at once, with a playable prototype and the grip code in Lua.

You hold a wooden tray with a steel ball and a rubber ball on it, and each has its own hole. Tip the tray gently and only the steel ball slides; tip it hard and both go, so you have to think about who moves first.

The core loop: read, tip, settle

Tilt Tray Twins is a top-down physics puzzle. You never touch a marble. Your only control is the angle of the tray, and every marble on it feels that same angle. The marbles differ in grip: the least tilt that gets a marble rolling from rest. Steel has very little grip and slides at a slight tip. Rubber has a lot and stays put until the tray is steep.

That one difference is the whole puzzle. A gentle tilt is a command for the steel marble alone. A steep tilt is a command for both. Every level is a question of order: which marble do you send first, and where do you park the other one?

Every ten seconds the player reads where each marble will go, tips the tray, and lets things settle. Every minute they clear a tray with both marbles in their holes. Every session they work through a set of trays that add walls, more marbles and new materials.

The rules

  • One tilt for everything. The tray tips in any direction, from flat to 100 percent. Every marble feels the same tilt.
  • Grip. Steel starts rolling above 6 percent tilt. Rubber needs more than 35 percent. While a marble rolls, its grip keeps slowing it, so rubber stops quickly when you ease off and steel coasts a long way.
  • Weight. Steel weighs twice as much as rubber. Weight does not change how fast a marble rolls down a slope; it only matters when marbles collide, and then steel shoves rubber aside.
  • Holes. Each marble has a hole of its own colour. A marble that rolls over the wrong hole drops in and pops back to its start, which counts as a slip. A filled hole is flat, so other marbles roll over it.
  • The goal. Both marbles in their own holes. The score is total time across the trays, plus how many slips you made.

What makes it different

The player fantasy is a steady hand: the waiter carrying a full tray, or the child with a pocket maze puzzle, who knows exactly how far to tip. The constraint is that you steer everything at once with one control, and the marbles answer it differently. That turns a dexterity toy into a planning puzzle. The mood is calm and tactile, with no enemies and no countdown, only the clock you set for yourself.

This is not real physics. A real ball on a hard tray rolls down any slope at all. Grip here is a game rule, loosely like a marble on thick felt, where the soft surface soaks up energy and a small slope is not enough to start it. Players accept the rule quickly if each material looks and sounds like what it does.

The hardest part to build is the level design, not the code. The physics is thirty lines. Making trays where the order of moves matters, where a wrong order is recoverable, and where a keyboard player and a touch player can both solve them, takes most of the work.

Try it

On the first tray, tip steeply toward the bottom left and watch the steel marble fall into the orange hole and pop back. Then restart, and try a gentle tip down and to the right first. Watch the rubber marble sit still while steel finds its own way round the wall.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: one tilt applied to every marble, with grip deciding whether it moves. Tray units are the same as the demo (a tray 400 by 280), and tilt is set from the mouse or keys. Walls, holes and marble collisions are left out.

local G, STEP = 700, 1 / 120   -- units per second squared at full tilt
local tilt = { x = 0, y = 0 }  -- each axis -1..1, from mouse or keys
local marbles = {
  { x = 170, y = 140, vx = 0, vy = 0, mass = 2, grip = 0.06 }, -- steel
  { x = 230, y = 140, vx = 0, vy = 0, mass = 1, grip = 0.35 }, -- rubber
}

local function stepMarble(m, dt)
  local tx, ty = tilt.x, tilt.y
  local t = math.sqrt(tx * tx + ty * ty)
  local speed = math.sqrt(m.vx * m.vx + m.vy * m.vy)
  if speed < 0.5 then
    if t <= m.grip then         -- not steep enough: it stays put
      m.vx, m.vy = 0, 0
      return
    end
    -- starting from rest: grip pushes back along the tilt
    m.vx = m.vx + G * (tx - m.grip * tx / t) * dt
    m.vy = m.vy + G * (ty - m.grip * ty / t) * dt
  else
    -- rolling: grip pushes back against the motion
    local ox, oy = m.vx, m.vy
    m.vx = m.vx + G * (tx - m.grip * ox / speed) * dt
    m.vy = m.vy + G * (ty - m.grip * oy / speed) * dt
    if m.vx * ox + m.vy * oy <= 0 then m.vx, m.vy = 0, 0 end -- grip stopped it
  end
  m.x = m.x + m.vx * dt
  m.y = m.y + m.vy * dt
end

local acc = 0
function love.update(dt)
  acc = math.min(acc + dt, 0.1)
  while acc >= STEP do
    for _, m in ipairs(marbles) do stepMarble(m, STEP) end
    acc = acc - STEP
  end
end

The highlighted lines are the mechanic. Grip is a constant push against the motion, the same size whatever the speed, so it acts like a threshold at rest and a brake while rolling. The last check stops a marble instead of letting the brake push it backwards. mass is unused here; the demo uses it only when two marbles collide.

Pitfalls

  • Thresholds the player cannot feel. If the gap between "steel only" and "both" is invisible, a mistake looks like the game cheated. The prototype draws the rubber threshold as a dashed ring and colours the tilt arrow. Test: hide the ring for one tester and keep it for another, then compare how often each tips past it by accident.
  • Pointer and keys play different games. A touch player can hold any tilt; a keyboard player has eight directions and two strengths. A level that needs a 15 degree angle is unfair on keys. Solve every level with the keyboard first.
  • Dexterity creeping in. If clearing a tray depends on holding a tilt for exactly the right fraction of a second, the puzzle turns into a reflex test. Holes in corners and along walls let walls do the aiming. Watch for testers who know the answer and still fail.
  • One solution, found by flailing. Two marbles in an open tray can often be solved by tipping wildly until both drop in. Time a tester who thinks and one who just shakes the tray. If shaking is as fast, the level needs a trap that punishes it, like the wrong hole on the first tray.

In real games

Tilting the world instead of moving the hero is old and well loved. The wooden labyrinth toy, where two knobs tip a board to roll one ball past holes, is the physical ancestor. Super Monkey Ball tilts the stage under a ball, and phone games often use the accelerometer to tip a maze. Puzzles where one input moves many pieces at once are just as familiar from sliding tile games such as 2048, where every tile slides in the chosen direction.

Tilt Tray Twins borrows the tilt from the first group and the "one move, many pieces" from the second. What I have not seen at the centre of a game is the split by material: the same tilt is a different command to each marble. I cannot be sure no game has done it, so treat this as a direction to explore, not a gap I can promise.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with the tray edges as walls, two holes as circles, and the arrow keys setting tilt to 0.25 (or 0.8 with Shift). Use the first tray from the demo: one wall, both marbles in the middle, holes in opposite corners. Draw marbles and holes with love.graphics.circle.

Hand it to a friend and say only "each ball goes in its own hole". The one question to answer: do they discover, without being told, that a gentle tilt moves only one marble? If they find it within a minute and grin, the idea works. If they never find it, the materials need to look more different, or the first tray must make the gentle tilt the only way through.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: twelve trays with two marbles each, walls only, a time and slip count per tray, and the tilt ring always visible.
  • One-month version: a third material between steel and rubber, a fourth marble on late trays, soft patches of felt that raise grip where they lie, and a level editor.
  • Cut first: accelerometer control on phones. It feels great but makes the two thresholds hard to hit on purpose; press-and-hold works on every device.
  • The mechanic is the gap between two thresholds. Every feature should make that gap easier to see and use.
