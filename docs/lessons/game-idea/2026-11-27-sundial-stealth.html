Sundial Stealth: wind the sun, hide in what it casts

A stealth game where you turn the sun forward to move the shadows you hide in, and the day only runs one way. Playable prototype and Lua code.

You are a thief who stole the hand of the courtyard sundial, and now the sun goes where you push it. The guards only see you in sunlight, so every shadow is a hiding place you can move, but the day only turns forward and the gate locks at sunset.

The core loop: read the light, turn the hour, slip through

The courtyard is seen from above: stone pillars, two guards, and a gem on a pedestal. You enter at the bottom left, take the gem, and leave by the gate on the right.

Guards have a vision cone: a wedge in front of them, with a limited range, inside which they can notice you. Here they notice you only when you are standing in sunlight. Inside a shadow you are invisible, even in the middle of a cone.

You have one tool: the time of day. Winding it forward moves the sun across the sky, which swings every shadow around its pillar and stretches or shrinks it. Morning shadows are long and point west. At noon they are short stubs pointing north. In the evening they grow long again, pointing east.

Every ten seconds you are looking at the gap between where you are and where you want to be, and asking "is there an hour when a shadow covers that?" Every minute you make one crossing of the courtyard. Every session is a string of courtyards, each with its own pillars and its own budget of daylight.

The rules

  • Sunlight is visibility. A guard spots you only if you are in sunlight, inside their cone, and no pillar stands between you. Being seen fills an alarm meter in about half a second; a full meter sends you and the gem back to the start.
  • The sun is yours, but it turns one way. The day runs from 6:00 to 18:00. You can move it forward as fast or as slowly as you like, and never back. Getting caught does not refund the hours you spent.
  • Guards keep their own time. They patrol in real time. The sun decides where you can hide; the guards decide when you can move.
  • Goal. Carry the gem out of the right-hand gate before sunset. A good run uses fewer hours and raises no alarms.

How a pillar casts its shadow

Seen from above, a round pillar's shadow is a capsule: the pillar's circle dragged in a straight line, away from the sun. Two numbers describe it. The direction is opposite the sun's direction across the sky. The length is the pillar's height divided by the tangent of the sun's elevation, its angle above the horizon. A low sun has a small tangent, so the shadow is long; a high sun gives a short one.

What makes it different

Most stealth games treat light as part of the level, or give you a way to put lights out one at a time. Here the player owns the light source for the whole level, and the constraint is that it only moves forward. The player fantasy is the thief who bends the day to their route. The tension is that every hour you wind to cover one gap may uncover another, and the generous morning shadows never come back.

The hardest part to build is level layout, not code. A pillar's shadow sweeps a whole fan of ground through the day, so moving one pillar changes every hour of the level. You will want a debug view that draws the ground each pillar covers at every hour.

Try it

You start in the long shadow of the first pillar at dawn. Look at where the gem sits: no morning shadow reaches it. Wind the sun forward, watch the shadows swing, and find the hours when one covers the pedestal.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: where the sun is, whether a point is in shadow, and whether a guard can see it. One helper, the distance from a point to a segment, answers both "is this point in a shadow capsule?" and "does a pillar block the view?". Names and numbers match the demo; movement and drawing are left out.

local hour = 6                          -- the day runs from 6:00 to 18:00
local pillars = { { x = 3.0, y = 8.5, r = 0.55, h = 2.2 },
                  { x = 7.0, y = 2.8, r = 0.55, h = 2.4 } }  -- and five more
local function segDist(px, py, ax, ay, bx, by)  -- point to segment a-b
  local dx, dy = bx - ax, by - ay
  local len2 = dx * dx + dy * dy
  local t = len2 > 0 and ((px - ax) * dx + (py - ay) * dy) / len2 or 0
  t = math.max(0, math.min(1, t))
  local qx, qy = ax + dx * t - px, ay + dy * t - py
  return math.sqrt(qx * qx + qy * qy)
end
local function sun()
  local a = math.pi * (hour - 6) / 12                -- 0 at dawn, pi at dusk
  local elevation = math.rad(10 + 55 * math.sin(a))  -- 10 degrees up to 65
  return -math.cos(a), -math.sin(a), 1 / math.tan(elevation)
end
local function inShadow(x, y)
  local dx, dy, len = sun()               -- shadows point away from the sun
  for _, p in ipairs(pillars) do
    local L = p.h * len
    if segDist(x, y, p.x, p.y, p.x + dx * L, p.y + dy * L) <= p.r then return true end
  end
  return false
end
local function canSee(g, x, y)          -- g = { x, y, facing, range, half }
  if inShadow(x, y) then return false end
  local dx, dy = x - g.x, y - g.y
  if dx * dx + dy * dy > g.range * g.range then return false end
  local diff = (math.atan2(dy, dx) - g.facing + math.pi) % (2 * math.pi) - math.pi
  if math.abs(diff) > g.half then return false end
  for _, p in ipairs(pillars) do        -- a pillar in the way blocks the view
    if segDist(p.x, p.y, g.x, g.y, x, y) < p.r then return false end
  end
  return true
end
function love.update(dt)
  if love.keyboard.isDown("e") then
    hour = math.min(18, hour + 1.5 * dt)  -- forward only: no key turns it back
  end
end

The world uses screen directions: x grows to the right (east) and y grows downward (south). So at 6:00 the sun direction is east and the shadow vector (-cos a, -sin a) is (-1, 0), pointing west; at noon it is (0, -1), pointing north. The elevation never reaches zero, because 1 / tan(0) would make an endless shadow.

Pitfalls

  • Winding becomes free. If the day is long enough that hours never run short, players wind the sun to whatever suits them and the one-way rule stops mattering. Record how many hours good players have left at the exit; if it is more than a quarter of the day, shorten the day.
  • One perfect hour. If every pillar happens to cover the route at, say, 15:00, the level collapses into "wind to 15:00 and walk". Lay out pillars so that no single hour links the whole route.
  • Shadows you cannot read. Players must tell at a glance whether they are hidden. Draw the hero differently in shadow (the demo uses a dashed outline). Ask testers "are you hidden right now?" at random moments.
  • Caught by a shadow edge. A shadow sliding off you feels unfair if the alarm is instant. The half-second meter forgives grazes.

In real games

Light and shadow as the core of stealth is well explored. Thief: The Dark Project shows how visible you are with an on-screen light gem, and lets you douse torches with water arrows. Mark of the Ninja also hides you in darkness and lets you break lights. In both, light sources are objects in the level that you switch off one at a time.

Many open-world games have a day and night cycle that runs on its own clock. Sundial Stealth makes the sun the player's only verb, keeps one courtyard on screen, and treats daylight as a budget that cannot be refunded. I do not know of a game built on exactly that rule, but I have not seen every stealth game, so one may exist.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with circles for pillars, capsule shadows drawn as thick lines with round ends, one guard who walks back and forth, and a hero who changes look in shadow. Skip the gem: put a goal zone on the far side that no single hour fully shades.

Hand it to a friend and say only "get across without being seen". The one question: do they stop and wind the sun to make a shadow before they move, or do they just run and hope? If they plan with the sun, the idea works. If they run, the guards are too slow or the shadows too generous; tighten one and test again.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: one courtyard, round pillars only, two guards, a gem and a gate, with a score for hours used and alarms raised.
  • One-month version: ten courtyards, walls and archways that cast rectangular shadows, a second floor where rooftop shadows fall into the yard, and a courtyard where clouds briefly hide the sun.
  • Cut first: guard investigation behaviour, noise and alert states. Spotted or not spotted is enough while you learn whether winding the sun is fun.
  • Hardest part: level layout. Build the per-hour shadow coverage view before your second level.
