Metronome Duel: commit late, strike on the beat

A rhythm fencing duel where moves land only on the beat, and committing early lets your rival read you. Playable prototype and Lua code.

Two fencers face each other while a metronome ticks, and every move lands on the tick. You can choose attack, parry or feint whenever you like during the swing, but the moment you choose, your body gives it away, so the best fencer is the one who waits longest and still makes the beat.

The core loop: watch the swing, hold your nerve, commit

The duel happens on a piste, the narrow strip fencers fight on. A metronome swings above it. Each swing ends in a beat, and on the beat both fencers' moves are revealed and compared. Moves exist only on beats: there is no free movement, no health bar, no combo.

Every ten seconds you play about eight beats. On each one you watch the swing and the rival's stance, and decide when to commit, which means locking in your move for this beat. Every minute you win or lose a touch, a point scored by driving the other fencer off their end of the piste, while the tempo speeds up. Every session is a ladder of rivals, each with a different habit to learn.

The rules

  • Three moves in a triangle. Attack beats feint, because a feint is a slow fake that leaves you open. Feint beats parry, because it draws the parry and then lands. Parry beats attack, because a blocked attack opens the attacker to a counter. Equal moves clash and nothing happens.
  • Standing still is allowed, but risky. If you commit nothing, an attack hits you. A parry or feint against nobody does nothing.
  • The winner of a beat steps forward. The loser steps back one mark. Three net steps push someone off the end of the piste: that is a touch. First to three touches wins the bout.
  • One commit per beat, no take-backs. Right after a beat comes a short recovery when you cannot commit at all.
  • The swing has two zones. A commit in the first part of the swing is seen: the other fencer knows your move. A commit in the last 40 percent, the hidden zone, stays secret until the beat. This rule applies to both fencers.
  • The tempo climbs. Each beat is 4 percent shorter than the last, from 50 to about 86 beats per minute, and slows down again after a touch.

What makes it different

Plenty of games are rock, paper, scissors underneath. The twist here is that timing is information. The rhythm skill (hitting a window before the beat) and the mind game (picking the right move) are tied together by one rule: the earlier you act, the more you reveal. That gives the player two fantasies at once, the cool nerve of a fencer who waits, and the pounce on a rival who showed their hand too soon.

The rival plays by the same rule. It sometimes commits early, and you can see its move and counter it. It gets impatient when cornered near its own end of the piste, so pressing forward makes it easier to read. When it cannot see you, it falls back on a habit: half the time it assumes you will repeat your last move and picks the counter to it. A habit is a pattern a player can notice and exploit, which is what turns a random triangle into a duel.

The hardest part to build is the rivals. The code is small; making each rival feel like a person with a readable style, without being either random or a mind reader, takes many playtests.

Try it

Start the bout and watch the metronome arc. Try committing early once and see the rival counter you. Then wait for the dark part of the arc before you choose. When the rival's stance shows a letter, it committed in the open: counter it. When it shows a question mark, think about what you did last beat.

The code

The heart of the mechanic in LÖVE: a commit that remembers when it happened, and a rival that uses that time. phase runs from 0 to 1 across one swing, so the zones do not change as the tempo rises. Drawing, the touch counter and the bout ending are left out. The names and numbers are the demo's.

local MOVES   = { "attack", "parry", "feint" }
local BEATS   = { attack = "feint", feint = "parry", parry = "attack" } -- beats the one it names
local COUNTER = { attack = "parry", parry = "feint", feint = "attack" } -- what beats it
local KEYS    = { a = "attack", p = "parry", f = "feint" }
local SHOW, RECOVER, READ, HABIT = 0.6, 0.15, 0.75, 0.5
local beat, phase, piste, lastYours = 1.2, 0, 0, nil
local you, rival = {}, { at = 0.5 }

local function wins(a, b)
  return a ~= nil and (BEATS[a] == b or (a == "attack" and b == nil))
end
local function rivalTiming()
  if piste >= 2 then return 0.25 + love.math.random() * 0.5 end -- cornered: impatient
  return 0.4 + love.math.random() * 0.55
end
local function rivalChoose()
  if you.move and you.at < SHOW and love.math.random() < READ then
    return COUNTER[you.move]             -- it saw you commit early
  end
  if lastYours and love.math.random() < HABIT then
    return COUNTER[lastYours]            -- it expects you to repeat yourself
  end
  return MOVES[love.math.random(3)]
end
function love.keypressed(key)
  if KEYS[key] and not you.move and phase >= RECOVER then
    you.move, you.at = KEYS[key], phase  -- locked in, with a timestamp
  end
end
function love.update(dt)
  phase = phase + dt / beat
  if not rival.move and phase >= rival.at then rival.move = rivalChoose() end
  if phase >= 1 then                     -- the beat: both moves resolve at once
    if wins(you.move, rival.move) then piste = piste + 1
    elseif wins(rival.move, you.move) then piste = piste - 1 end
    lastYours, phase = you.move, 0
    beat = math.max(0.7, beat * 0.96)
    if math.abs(piste) >= 3 then piste, beat, lastYours = 0, 1.2, nil end -- a touch
    you, rival = {}, { at = rivalTiming() }
  end
end

The highlighted function is the whole personality of the rival. Change READ and HABIT and you have a different opponent: a high READ punishes early commits hard, a high HABIT makes a rival that is easy to bait once you notice it. A ladder of rivals can be little more than a table of these two numbers plus a timing range.

Pitfalls

  • It collapses into pure guessing. If the rival never shows a move and has no habit, every beat is a coin flip with three sides, and players feel they won or lost by luck. Test by playing twenty beats with your eyes closed, pressing a random key in the hidden zone. If your results look like your real play, the rival is not readable enough.
  • The rival reads you too well. A rival that counters every early commit makes the seen zone feel like a trap rather than a choice. The demo reads you three times out of four. Watch new players: if they stop ever committing early, lower READ or make early commits pay, for example with a stronger push.
  • The tempo outruns the reading. At high tempo the hidden zone is under a third of a second, and there is no time to think. The duel becomes a reflex test. Cap the tempo where a tester can still name the rival's habit out loud.
  • Unclear resolution. If players cannot tell why they lost a beat, they cannot learn. Always show both moves for a moment after the beat, in words or with clear poses. Ask testers after a bout which move beats which; if they hesitate, the feedback is too quiet.
  • Waiting has no cost. If committing at the last instant is always best, the seen zone is dead space. Give early commits something, such as a bonus step, or a rival that attacks the hesitant more often.

In real games

A triangle of moves where each beats one and loses to another is a long-standing structure in fighting games, where attacks, blocks and throws are often described this way. David Sirlin's card game Yomi is built on fighting-game choices revealed simultaneously, and its name is the Japanese word for reading the opponent's mind. Crypt of the NecroDancer showed that a game can require every action to fall on the beat of the music.

Metronome Duel sits between those. The moves are revealed together on the beat, but the moment you chose is part of the game: committing is a timing skill, and its timing leaks information. I do not know of a game built around that exact rule, though I have not checked every rhythm fighting game, so something close may already exist.

Your turn: the first playtest

In one sitting, build the Lua above with two rectangles for fencers, a line for the piste, a rotating line for the metronome and the moves printed as words over each fencer. Draw the hidden zone as a coloured arc. Leave out touches and tempo changes; just count beats won and lost.

Play it against a friend on one keyboard, then against the rival. The one question: after two minutes, can the player tell you the rival's habit? If they can, and they start baiting it on purpose, the duel works. If they only describe it as random, raise HABIT or make the rival commit early more often.

Takeaways

  • Weekend version: one rival, the three moves, the seen and hidden zones, the piste and touches, with clear move reveals on each beat.
  • One-month version: a ladder of five rivals with different READ, HABIT and timing values, a local two-player mode with split keys, and animated poses so moves read without text.
  • Cut first: extra moves, special abilities and stamina. Every new move weakens the triangle that the reading depends on.
  • Hardest part: rival design. Keep their behaviour in data, not code, so you can tune a rival between playtests without rewriting it.
