Pokémon Red and Blue: MissingNo. and one block of memory with two jobs

Why MissingNo. appears: the old man's tutorial parks your name in the wild Pokémon table, and one stretch of shore reads that leftover list.

In Pokémon Red and Blue, players found a strange routine: watch the old man in Viridian City demonstrate catching, fly to Cinnabar Island, and surf up and down its east shore. Sooner or later a scrambled creature called MissingNo. appears, often above level 100.

What you meet depends on your character's name. That is the clue: two parts of the game share the same bytes of memory, and one of them does not clean up.

The constraint: 8 KB of working memory

The Game Boy gives a game 8 KB of work RAM: party, bag, map, battle state, everything that changes while you play. Every variable of the game (Japan 1996, English versions 1998) had to fit.

One way to save room is an overlay: one block of memory with two owners that should never need it at the same time. The fan project pret has rebuilt the code as a disassembly, readable source that assembles back into the original game, and its memory layout marks 19 such blocks. One of them, 293 bytes long, holds the wild Pokémon table while you walk around. During a battle, the same bytes hold the opponent's team, and in a link cable battle the other player's name.

How a wild encounter table is stored

The table is 21 bytes. The first byte is the encounter rate: the chance, out of 256, that a step in tall grass starts a battle. Then come 10 slots, two bytes each: a level, then a species. The first two slots each come up 51 times in 256, the last only 3 times.

The species byte is an internal index, not the Pokédex number. Between 1 and 190, 39 values do not belong to a normal species: 36 are unused and 3 are kept for fossil and ghost pictures. When the game is asked to show one of those, you get MissingNo. ("missing number").

When you enter a new area, the game loads that area's grass rate. If the rate is not zero, it also copies the area's 10 slots. If the rate is zero, as in a town, it skips the copy and leaves the old slots where they are. That is stale data: values left over from an earlier job that nobody cleared.

The old man borrows the bytes

In the tutorial the battle text should say OLD MAN, so the game saves your name, writes "OLD MAN" in its place, and restores it afterwards. It saves the name in the link opponent's name, which, because of the overlay, starts at the grass rate byte.

A name is 11 bytes: up to 7 letters, an end marker, and spare bytes. So the copy covers the rate byte and the first five slots. When the battle ends, the town is reloaded. Its grass rate (zero) overwrites the first letter, and the copy of the slots is skipped. Letters 2, 4 and 6 of your name are now levels. Letters 3, 5 and 7 are species.

The shore that reads the wrong list

A town has no grass, so the stale list does nothing yet. The second half of the bug is the step check, which looks at two different tiles of the square you stand on. The bottom-right tile decides whether an encounter can happen and at what rate. The bottom-left tile decides which list to use: water if that tile is water, otherwise grass. On a left shore square, land on the left and sea on the right, those two answers disagree. You get the water encounter chance with the grass species list. A comment in the disassembly names the east coast of Cinnabar as an example.

The flight matters too. Reach the shore through any area that has grass, such as Route 21 south of Pallet Town, and its slots overwrite your name. Flying straight from Viridian City passes through no area with grass.

Fact and legend

  • Where exactly? Retellings say Cinnabar, and that is what players see. Reading the map data in the disassembly, this page found that those left shore squares are the first column of Route 20, the sea route east of the island. Cinnabar's own wild data has a rate of zero for grass and for water. Route 20 has no grass list, so it leaves yours alone, and its water rate is 5 in 256, about 2 percent a step. This is our reading of the source, not a claim from a published analysis.
  • "It ruins your save." Widely reported effects are that the sixth item in your bag gains 128 and that the Hall of Fame records come out garbled. This page did not check those against the code.

The idea in one line

Sharing memory is safe only if every new owner rewrites all of it, or clears it, before anyone reads it.

The lesson for today

Today the overlay is a union in C, a reused buffer, or an object pool, where a game reuses dead bullets or enemies instead of creating new ones, which still matters on phones and the web. MissingNo. is the classic pool bug: a reused enemy keeps last life's poison timer because the spawn code set only the fields it cared about.

Try it

Type a name, press Watch the old man's lesson, then Go to the shore and Surf until something appears. In the table, blue cells came from your name, orange cells are unpredictable leftovers, and grey cells are the last grassy area's data (here Route 1). G, H, J, M or S in positions 3, 5 or 7 land on unused indexes.

Then Reset, choose Walk through Route 21, and repeat. Same shore, but the grass list now belongs to Route 21.

The code

The mechanism in plain Lua (runs in LÖVE or any Lua 5.1). loadWildData skips the copy when an area has no grass, oldManLesson parks the name in the same bytes, and surfLeftShore rolls the water rate but reads the grass list. With TASHMIR, slots 1 and 2 become MissingNo. ($92 and $8C) at levels 128 and 135.

-- MissingNo. in miniature: one block of memory, two meanings.
local memory = {}                      -- [1] grass rate, then 10 slots of level, species
for i = 1, 21 do memory[i] = 0 end
local CHANCES = {51, 51, 39, 25, 25, 25, 13, 13, 11, 3}   -- out of 256

local function encode(ch)              -- Game Boy text code for one letter
  local b = string.byte(ch)
  if b >= 97 then return 0xA0 + b - 97 end                -- a to z
  return 0x80 + b - 65                                     -- A to Z
end

local function loadWildData(area)      -- runs every time an area loads
  memory[1] = area.grassRate
  if area.grassRate == 0 then return end                   -- no grass: old slots stay
  for i = 1, 20 do memory[i + 1] = area.grassMons[i] end
end

local function oldManLesson(name)      -- save the name in the same bytes
  for i = 1, #name do memory[i] = encode(name:sub(i, i)) end
  memory[#name + 1] = 0x50             -- end marker (the real copy also moves leftover bytes)
end

local function surfLeftShore(waterRate)
  if math.random(0, 255) >= waterRate then return nil end  -- no encounter this step
  local roll, total = math.random(0, 255), 0
  for slot = 1, 10 do
    total = total + CHANCES[slot]
    if roll < total then return memory[slot * 2], memory[slot * 2 + 1] end  -- the GRASS list
  end
end

local ROUTE_1 = { grassRate = 25, grassMons = { 3,0x24, 3,0xA5, 3,0xA5, 2,0xA5, 2,0x24,
                                                3,0x24, 3,0x24, 4,0xA5, 4,0x24, 5,0x24 } }
local NO_GRASS = { grassRate = 0 }

loadWildData(ROUTE_1)                  -- walked through Route 1 earlier
oldManLesson("TASHMIR")
loadWildData(NO_GRASS)                 -- battle over: Viridian City reloads
loadWildData(NO_GRASS)                 -- fly to Cinnabar Island
loadWildData(NO_GRASS)                 -- surf onto the shore (Route 20: water only)
local level, species
for step = 1, 2000 do
  level, species = surfLeftShore(5)
  if level then break end
end
if level then print(string.format("Wild Pokemon: index $%02X at level %d", species, level))
else print("No encounter in 2000 steps") end

About 40 percent of runs print $92 or $8C. Slots 6 to 10 still print Route 1's data, because nothing overwrote them.

Pitfalls

  • Stale data looks valid. Nothing marks the leftover slots as old. If a loader can skip writing, readers cannot trust what they find.
  • "Nothing to load" should still clear. Writing zeros or an "empty" flag for areas without grass would have closed the bug.
  • Two checks that should agree. Decide "grass or water" once and use that answer for both the rate and the list.
  • Lookup tables with holes. Check that an index points at a real entry before using it, especially when it comes from saved or shared data.
  • Pools need a full reset. When you reuse an object, reset every field, not only the ones the new owner sets.

In real games

The Red and Blue disassembly marks 19 shared blocks in work RAM. Most never cause trouble, because their owners really never meet.

Object pools are standard in modern engines: Unity ships an object pool class, and pooling bullets and particles is common advice for mobile and web games.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Take the Lua above and fix the bug in two different ways. First, change loadWildData so that an area with no grass writes zeros into all 20 slot bytes, and make surfLeftShore return nil when the species byte is 0. Second, undo that and instead make oldManLesson save the 11 original bytes and write them back when the lesson ends. You are done when, after either fix, every run prints Route 1's indexes $24 or $A5, or no encounter at all, and never $92 or $8C. Then decide which fix you would ship, and why.

Takeaways

  • Pokémon Red and Blue had 8 KB of work RAM and reused blocks of it for jobs that should never overlap.
  • The old man's tutorial saves your name in bytes shared with the wild encounter table, and nothing restores the table.
  • Areas without grass skip loading the list, so the name survives as levels (letters 2, 4, 6) and species (letters 3, 5, 7).
  • On left shore squares, the game rolls the water rate but reads the grass list. That is where your name turns into MissingNo.
  • Today: unions, buffers and object pools are still worth it. Reset everything when the owner changes.
