Sonic the Hedgehog: running round a loop with two sensors and one speed

How Sonic runs up walls and round loops: one speed along the ground, two sensors that read its angle, and a rule that drops him below 2.5 px per frame.

In Sonic the Hedgehog (Sega, 1991) the hero runs into a loop, up the wall, upside down across the top and out again. Arrive too slowly and he peels off and drops. No physics engine does this: a few short routines, run once a frame, do all of it.

The constraint: whole numbers, tables and tiles

The Mega Drive's Motorola 68000 processor has no hardware for decimal (floating-point) maths. Sonic's code uses fixed point: whole numbers that count 1/256 of a pixel. Every value below is per frame, at 60 frames a second on a North American or Japanese console.

An angle is one byte, so a full turn is 256 steps, written in hexadecimal with a $ sign: $40 (64) is a quarter turn, $80 a half. Sine and cosine come from a table. The solid ground is made of 16 by 16 pixel collision blocks, each with a height map (16 heights, one per pixel column) and one stored surface angle.

Part 1: one speed along the ground

On the ground Sonic does not keep a horizontal and a vertical speed. He keeps one number, his ground speed: how fast he moves along the surface. Each frame the game turns it into screen movement with the surface angle:

vx = groundSpeed * cos(angle)
vy = groundSpeed * sin(angle)

On flat ground all of the speed is horizontal; on a wall all of it is vertical. Running up a wall is the floor code with a different angle.

Slopes act on ground speed directly: before moving, the game adds 0.125 × sin(angle), so uphill slows Sonic and downhill speeds him up. This step is skipped within 45 degrees of upside down.

Part 2: two sensors read the ground

After moving, the game finds the surface again with two sensors: probes 9 pixels either side of Sonic's centre, at his feet 19 pixels below it. Each reads the height map of the block it lands in and returns the distance to the surface and that block's angle.

The nearer one wins. Sonic moves by that distance, so his feet touch the surface, and takes its angle as his own. If the surface is more than 14 pixels away, the ground has dropped away and he is in the air.

Sensors only look down, right, up or left. The mode, Sonic's angle rounded to the nearest quarter turn, decides which: down on the floor ($00), right on a wall rising to the right ($C0), up on the ceiling ($80), left on the wall coming down ($40).

Part 3: letting go of the wall

With sensors alone Sonic would creep across a ceiling at any speed, like a fly. So one more check runs: in any mode other than floor, if his ground speed is below 2.5 pixels per frame (150 a second), it is set to zero, he is put in the air, and left and right are ignored for 30 frames. Then ordinary falling takes over: 0.21875 pixels per frame added to his downward speed each frame.

The rule ignores the size of the loop. A real ball needs more speed on the ceiling of a bigger loop; Sonic needs 2.5 everywhere.

Part 4: a loop that crosses itself

A loop's way in and way out overlap: running in along the bottom, Sonic would hit the side he will later come down. Sonic 1 keeps a flag that changes as his angle passes the top of the loop, and while it is set the collision lookup reads a different version of that piece of level. One version is open on the way in, the other on the way out. The demo does the same with two paths.

Fact and legend

The numbers here come from the Sonic Retro disassembly of Sonic 1: the machine code turned back into readable form, labelled by fans, not Sega. Later Sonic games changed some details, so do not assume these values for them.

The idea in one line

Keep one speed along the surface, measure the surface every frame with short probes, turn the probes with the angle, and add a plain rule for when to let go.

The lesson for today

Modern character controllers start the same way: short rays at the feet keep the character pressed to the ground and report its angle. Most treat anything steeper than a set angle as a wall. To run up walls, do what Sonic does: turn the probes with the mode, and decide yourself when to let go.

Try it

The character enters the loop at the Start speed you choose. At 2.5, watch where it lets go, then raise the speed until it gets round. Set Controller to "Let go" to see how much more speed it needs with no button held. Turn off Fall off walls below 2.5 to watch a slow character crawl across the ceiling.

The code

The demo's ground and air steps in LÖVE, with the same names and numbers. The values are per frame, so love.update runs whole 1/60 second steps. findSurface stands in for your level; this one is a flat floor at y = 400.

-- Sonic-style ground movement. Units are pixels and frames, 60 frames a second.
local SLOPE, ACC, DEC, TOP = 0.125, 0.046875, 0.5, 6
local FALL_SPEED, SNAP, GRAVITY, W, H = 2.5, 14, 0.21875, 9, 19
local p = { x = 290, y = 381, gs = 3, vx = 0, vy = 0, angle = 0, air = false, lock = 0 }
local holdRight, timeLeft = true, 0

local function findSurface(x, y, dx, dy)    -- your level: distance, angle
  if dy > 0 then return 400 - y, 0 else return math.huge, 0 end
end

local function groundStep()
  -- slope pull, skipped within 45 degrees of upside down
  if math.cos(p.angle) > -0.7071 then p.gs = p.gs + SLOPE * math.sin(p.angle) end
  if not holdRight then p.gs = p.gs > 0 and math.max(0, p.gs - ACC) or math.min(0, p.gs + ACC)
  elseif p.lock > 0 then                        -- buttons ignored after letting go
  elseif p.gs < 0 then p.gs = p.gs + DEC
  elseif p.gs < TOP then p.gs = math.min(TOP, p.gs + ACC) end
  p.vx, p.vy = p.gs * math.cos(p.angle), p.gs * math.sin(p.angle)
  p.x, p.y = p.x + p.vx, p.y + p.vy
  local mode = math.floor(p.angle / (2 * math.pi) * 4 + 0.5) % 4
  local tx, ty = math.cos(mode * math.pi / 2), math.sin(mode * math.pi / 2)
  local dx, dy = -ty, tx                       -- sensors look this way
  local d1, a1 = findSurface(p.x - tx * W + dx * H, p.y - ty * W + dy * H, dx, dy)
  local d2, a2 = findSurface(p.x + tx * W + dx * H, p.y + ty * W + dy * H, dx, dy)
  if d2 < d1 then d1, a1 = d2, a2 end          -- the nearer sensor wins
  if d1 > SNAP then p.air = true return end
  p.x, p.y, p.angle = p.x + dx * d1, p.y + dy * d1, a1
  if p.lock > 0 then p.lock = p.lock - 1
  elseif mode ~= 0 and math.abs(p.gs) < FALL_SPEED then
    p.gs, p.air, p.lock = 0, true, 30           -- too slow: let go
  end
end

local function airStep()
  p.x, p.y, p.vy = p.x + p.vx, p.y + p.vy, p.vy + GRAVITY
  local d, a = findSurface(p.x, p.y + H, 0, 1)
  if p.vy >= 0 and d <= 0 then                 -- landed
    p.y, p.angle, p.air = p.y + d, a, false
    p.gs = p.vx * math.cos(a) + p.vy * math.sin(a)
  end
end

function love.update(dt)
  timeLeft = timeLeft + dt
  while timeLeft >= 1 / 60 do
    timeLeft = timeLeft - 1 / 60
    if p.air then airStep() else groundStep() end
  end
end

function love.draw()
  love.graphics.rectangle("fill", p.x - W, p.y - H, W * 2, H * 2)
end

Angles are radians in the game's direction: with y pointing down, a wall rising to the right is 3π/2 ($C0). Keep p.angle between 0 and 2π. The demo also pushes the character out of walls in the air and lands with two sensors; both are left out here.

Pitfalls

  • One sensor in the middle. It drops into small dips and misses the edge of a step. Two side probes, taking the nearer surface, ride over both.
  • Mode flicker at 45 degrees. An angle on a boundary can flip the sensors every frame. The original nudges angles near each boundary by one step before rounding, and the size of the nudge depends on the quarter.
  • Snap distance. With a 14 pixel snap, a fast character leaves the ground over a hilltop. Sometimes that launch is what you want; decide on purpose.
  • Per-frame numbers with a variable timestep. Scaling them by a changing dt moves the point where the character lets go. Run at a fixed 60 steps a second.
  • Landing on a slope. Air speed must become ground speed. The demo projects the velocity onto the surface; Sonic 1 has its own cases by angle, not covered here.

In real games

In Sonic 1 the loop path switch runs only in Green Hill Zone and Star Light Zone; every other zone skips it. While Sonic rolls, the same ground routine runs with different slope factors: 0.3125 downhill and 0.078125 uphill. The wall-release check runs either way.

Takeaways

  • On the ground Sonic has one speed along the surface; the angle turns it into horizontal and vertical movement.
  • Two sensors at the feet find the surface each frame; the nearer one sets his position and his angle.
  • The sensors look in one of four directions, chosen by rounding the angle to a quarter turn, which is how floor code runs walls and ceilings.
  • A plain rule, below 2.5 pixels per frame on a wall or ceiling, decides when he lets go.
  • A loop needs two collision paths, switched when the character passes the top.
