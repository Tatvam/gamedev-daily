Distributions: why do my damage rolls feel so swingy?

How adding random numbers pulls results towards the middle and how bending them with a power curve makes high or low results rare.

Your sword hits for a random 2 to 12, and players complain: one swing does 2, the next 12. Two six-sided dice give the same range, yet most hits land on 6, 7 or 8. Rare loot has the same problem in reverse. Both are about the shape of randomness, not its range.

The shape of a random result

Roll something many times and count how often each result comes up. Those counts are the distribution of the roll. Draw them as bars, one bar per result, and you get a histogram.

A single fair die, or love.math.random(2, 12), gives a uniform distribution: every result is equally likely, so the histogram is flat. Over 1,100 rolls, 2 to 12 each come up about 100 times. A 2 is exactly as common as a 7.

Adding dice: counting the ways

Now roll two dice and add them. The two dice can land in 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely pairs. Only one pair makes 2 (1 and 1), but six pairs make 7: 1+6, 2+5, 3+4, 4+3, 5+2 and 6+1.

So a 7 comes up 6 times in 36, which is 1 in 6, while a 2 comes up only 1 time in 36. The range did not change; the shape did. A third die makes the peak rounder: with three dice, 10 and 11 each have 27 of the 216 possible triples, and 3 has just 1. With more dice it becomes the smooth bell called the normal distribution.

The same trick with fractions

In code you usually start from a random fraction r from 0 up to, but never reaching, 1. Averaging several of them works like adding dice, but the result stays between 0 and 1, so you can stretch it to any range afterwards:

r = (random() + random()) / 2

How often is r below 0.25? With one random number, a quarter of the time. With the average of two, only 1 time in 8, because both numbers must be fairly low. With three, a result above 0.95 happens about once in 1,778 rolls, against once in 20 for one number.

Bending: making one end rare

Sometimes you want the results lopsided: loot quality that is usually poor and only now and then great. For that, push each random number through a curve. The simplest is a power:

value = r ^ k

Here r is the random number going in, value is what comes out, and k, the power, sets how much it bends. Both stay between 0 and 1, because 0 to any positive power is 0 and anything below 1 stays below 1.

Worked example with k = 2: r = 0.5 gives 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25. Every r below 0.5 gives a value below 0.25, so half of all rolls land in the bottom quarter. The average result drops from 0.5 to about 0.33.

A power below 1 bends the other way. With k = 0.5 (the square root), only 1 roll in 16 lands below 0.25, and most land high. A neat fact: the square root of one roll has exactly the same distribution as rolling twice and keeping the higher one.

Rule of thumb

Average several random numbers to pull results towards the middle. Raise one to a power to push them towards one end: k above 1 makes high results rare, k below 1 makes low results rare. You can do both: average first, then bend.

The code

Dice for whole numbers, an average for fractions, and the curve. rollDamage is what the demo does on each roll.

-- Sum of several dice, for example rollDice(2, 6) for 2 to 12
local function rollDice(count, sides)
  local total = 0
  for i = 1, count do
    total = total + love.math.random(1, sides)
  end
  return total
end

-- Average of n random fractions: still 0 to 1, but bunched in the middle
local function averageOf(n)
  local sum = 0
  for i = 1, n do
    sum = sum + love.math.random()
  end
  return sum / n
end

-- k above 1 pushes results low, k below 1 pushes them high. k must be above 0.
local function bend(r, k)
  return r ^ k
end

local function rollDamage(minDamage, maxDamage, dice, k)
  local value = bend(averageOf(dice), k)
  return minDamage + value * (maxDamage - minDamage)
end

function love.load()
  -- Count 10,000 rolls of r ^ 2 in four equal bins
  local counts = { 0, 0, 0, 0 }
  for i = 1, 10000 do
    local bin = math.min(4, math.floor(bend(averageOf(1), 2) * 4) + 1)
    counts[bin] = counts[bin] + 1
  end
  print(counts[1], counts[2], counts[3], counts[4]) -- about 5000 2071 1589 1340
end
var rng := RandomNumberGenerator.new()
var r := (rng.randf() + rng.randf()) / 2.0   # average of two
var value := pow(r, 2.0)                     # bent towards 0
var damage := 0.0 + value * 100.0
float r = (Random.value + Random.value) / 2f;  // average of two
float value = Mathf.Pow(r, 2f);                 // bent towards 0
float damage = 0f + value * 100f;

The counts in love.load match the demo with one die and k = 2. For a true bell curve with a chosen middle and spread, LÖVE has love.math.randomNormal(stddev, mean) and Godot has RandomNumberGenerator.randfn(mean, deviation). Their results are not limited to a range, so clamp them.

Pitfalls

  • Averaging hides the extremes almost completely. With three numbers averaged, the top 5% of damage happens about once in 1,778 hits. If a maximum hit should ever be seen, use fewer dice or add a separate critical-hit roll.
  • Adding dice changes the range. Two six-sided dice give 2 to 12, never 1. If you need 1 to 12, stretch an average to that range instead.
  • A power of 0 or below. r ^ 0 is always 1, so every hit is the maximum; Lua even returns 1 for 0 ^ 0. Negative powers give values above 1, and infinity for r = 0. Keep k above 0, as the demo does (0.25 to 4).
  • Feeding in numbers outside 0 to 1. The curve only behaves on 0 to 1. A negative input with a power like 0.5 gives nan (not a number), and an input above 1 grows instead of shrinking. Bend first, stretch to your range second.
  • Assuming the top end is excluded. Generators differ on whether exactly 1 can come out; Unity's Random.value, for one, is documented as including 1. If you turn a value into a bin or an array index with floor(value * n), clamp it to the last bin, as love.load does with math.min.
  • Bending changes the average. With one number and power k, the average is 1 / (k + 1): k = 2 lowers it to a third. Rebalance after you bend.

In real games

Board games lean on dice sums. In Catan the numbers 6 and 8 are printed in red because, after 7, they are the most likely sums of two dice. The tabletop role-playing system GURPS resolves most actions with three six-sided dice, so results cluster around 10 and 11.

Dungeons & Dragons fifth edition has advantage and disadvantage: roll two twenty-sided dice and keep the higher or the lower one. Keeping the higher is the dice version of the square root curve from this lesson.

In your own game: damage rolls, loot rarity, and small variations such as tree heights or enemy speeds around a typical value.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Pick one random number in your project, such as damage or a spawn delay. In love.load, roll it 10,000 times and print a text histogram of ten bins, one line of # per bin. Then try averageOf(2) and bend(r, 2). You are done when you can pick the shape that matches how it should feel, and say why.

Takeaways

  • A distribution is how often each result comes up. A histogram of many rolls shows its shape.
  • One random number is uniform: flat, with extremes as common as the middle.
  • Adding dice or averaging random numbers pulls results towards the middle, because middle results have more ways to happen.
  • r ^ k bends a fraction towards one end: k above 1 makes high results rare, k below 1 makes low results rare.
