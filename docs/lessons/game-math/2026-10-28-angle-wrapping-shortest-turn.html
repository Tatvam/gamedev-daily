Angle wrapping: why does my turret spin the long way round?

How wrapping the difference between two angles into −180 to 180 degrees gives the shortest turn, so a turret never spins the long way round.

Your turret now turns smoothly towards the mouse at a fixed speed. It looks great until the mouse crosses the left side of the turret. Then the barrel suddenly whirls almost a full circle the other way, even though the mouse barely moved.

Nothing is wrong with atan2. The turret is subtracting angles without remembering that a circle has no end. This lesson fixes that with one line.

One direction, many names

An angle measures a turn. Turn 360 degrees and you face the same way again, so every direction has many names: 170 degrees, −190 degrees and 530 degrees all point the same way. Adding or subtracting whole turns of 360 degrees never changes the direction.

atan2 picks one name for each direction, between −180 and 180 degrees. As in the earlier lessons, this lesson and its demo use y pointing down, as on a screen and in LÖVE, so positive angles turn clockwise: 0 points right, 90 degrees points down, and the seam where 180 meets −180 is on the left.

Subtracting two angles

Call the way the turret faces now a, the current angle, and the way it should face b, the target angle. The turn it needs is the angle difference, b − a. A positive difference means turn clockwise on screen, a negative one anticlockwise.

With a = 10° and b = 30°, b − a = 20°: turn 20 degrees clockwise. Correct.

Now let the target cross the seam. The turret faces a = 170°, just below the left side, and the mouse moves just above it, where atan2 says b = −170°. The difference is −170 − 170 = −340°: turn 340 degrees anticlockwise, almost a full circle. The real gap is only 20° clockwise. Both answers end up facing the same way, because −340 and 20 differ by exactly 360. Subtraction just picked the long name.

Wrapping the difference

To wrap an angle means to add or subtract whole turns until it lands in a chosen range. For a turn, the useful range is −180 to 180 degrees, because no direction is ever more than half a turn away. One formula does it, however far outside the range the number starts:

wrapped = (d + 180) % 360 - 180

Here d is the raw difference b − a, and % is the remainder (also called modulo): what is left after taking away as many whole 360s as fit. In Lua, the remainder by a positive number is never negative: -160 % 360 is 200, because −160 = −1 × 360 + 200. The formula shifts the range up by 180 so it runs from 0 to 360, takes the remainder, and shifts back.

Worked examples:

  • d = −340: (−340 + 180) % 360 − 180 = 200 − 180 = 20. Turn 20 degrees clockwise.
  • a = 10, b = 350, so d = 340: (520 % 360) − 180 = 160 − 180 = −20. Turn 20 degrees anticlockwise.
  • d = 20: (200 % 360) − 180 = 20. A difference that is already short stays the same.

In radians, as Lua's math.cos and math.atan2 use, replace 180 with π and 360 with 2π.

Turning at a limited speed

A turret that snaps straight to its target needs no difference at all. The difference matters when it turns at a turn speed, in degrees per second. Each frame it may turn at most speed × dt, where dt is the frame time in seconds:

  1. Find the wrapped difference.
  2. If it is smaller than this frame's step, set the angle to the target. This stops it overshooting and shaking.
  3. Otherwise turn by one step, clockwise if the difference is positive and anticlockwise if it is negative.

At 90 degrees per second and 60 frames per second, one step is 90 × 1/60 = 1.5 degrees. The 20 degree turn takes 14 frames, about a quarter of a second. The 340 degree spin would have taken 3.8 seconds, facing away from the target nearly the whole time.

Exactly behind

When the target is exactly 180 degrees away, both ways are equally short. The formula gives −180, so the turret turns anticlockwise. Either choice is fine; the formula always makes the same one.

The code

A turret that turns towards the mouse at 90 degrees per second, the demo's starting speed. angleDiff is the line that matters; the rest is the turning rule from above.

-- y grows downwards, as on LÖVE's screen, so a positive angle turns clockwise.
local TWO_PI = 2 * math.pi

-- Wrap any angle into the range -pi (included) to pi (excluded).
-- Lua's % never returns a negative number for a positive divisor.
local function wrapAngle(angle)
  return (angle + math.pi) % TWO_PI - math.pi
end

-- The shortest signed turn from angle a to angle b, in radians.
-- Positive: clockwise on screen. Negative: anticlockwise.
local function angleDiff(a, b)
  return wrapAngle(b - a)
end

-- Turn from current towards target by at most maxStep radians.
local function turnTowards(current, target, maxStep)
  local diff = angleDiff(current, target)
  if math.abs(diff) <= maxStep then
    return target                          -- arrive exactly, no shaking
  elseif diff > 0 then
    return wrapAngle(current + maxStep)    -- clockwise
  else
    return wrapAngle(current - maxStep)    -- anticlockwise
  end
end

local turret = { x = 400, y = 300, angle = math.rad(170) }
local TURN_SPEED = math.rad(90)            -- radians per second

-- Check: prints about 20, then about -20.
print(math.deg(angleDiff(math.rad(170), math.rad(-170))))
print(math.deg(angleDiff(math.rad(10), math.rad(350))))

function love.update(dt)
  local mouseX, mouseY = love.mouse.getPosition()
  local dx, dy = mouseX - turret.x, mouseY - turret.y
  if dx ~= 0 or dy ~= 0 then               -- no direction when on the turret
    local wanted = math.atan2(dy, dx)
    turret.angle = turnTowards(turret.angle, wanted, TURN_SPEED * dt)
  end
end

function love.draw()
  local tipX = turret.x + math.cos(turret.angle) * 30
  local tipY = turret.y + math.sin(turret.angle) * 30
  love.graphics.circle("line", turret.x, turret.y, 12)
  love.graphics.line(turret.x, turret.y, tipX, tipY)
  love.graphics.print(string.format("angle %.1f deg", math.deg(turret.angle)), 10, 10)
end

Built-in versions: Unity has Mathf.DeltaAngle(current, target) and Mathf.MoveTowardsAngle, both in degrees. Godot has lerp_angle, and recent Godot 4 versions add angle_difference(from, to), in radians.

Pitfalls

  • A remainder that keeps the sign. Lua's % gives 200 for -160 % 360. C#'s %, GDScript's fmod and C's fmod keep the sign of the left number and give −160, so the formula above returns −340 again. In those languages use the built-in function, or add 360 to the remainder when it is negative.
  • Wrapping with a single if. if d > 180 then d = d - 360 end fixes −340 and 340, but not 600. Angles that keep adding up, such as a wheel that has rolled for a minute, go far past one turn. The remainder formula handles any size.
  • Mixing degrees and radians. Wrapping radians with 360 or degrees with 2π gives nonsense that still looks like a number. Pick one unit per function and say which in a comment.
  • Overshooting. Without the "close enough, arrive" check, a turret whose last step is larger than the remaining gap jumps past, then back, and shakes. The check in turnTowards stops it.
  • Blending angles with lerp. Blending 170 towards −170 by plain arithmetic passes through 0, the far side of the circle. Blend with a + angleDiff(a, b) * t instead, so the blend goes the short way.
  • The zero-length direction. When the mouse sits on the turret, atan2(0, 0) gives 0 and the turret turns to face right for no reason. Skip the update, as love.update does.

In real games

Anything that rotates at a limited speed towards something needs the shortest turn: turrets and tanks tracking a target, top-down enemies turning to face the player, a ship steering towards a waypoint, a third-person camera swinging round behind the character. Engines ship ready-made versions, such as Unity's Mathf.DeltaAngle and Mathf.MoveTowardsAngle, because the bug is so common.

The same difference is also a cheap check for "am I facing it yet?": a guard that fires only when the absolute wrapped difference is below 5 degrees will never fire at a target behind it.

Takeaways

  • Every direction has many angle names that differ by whole turns, so b − a can be the long way round.
  • Wrap the difference into −180 to 180 degrees with (d + 180) % 360 - 180, or −π to π in radians.
  • The sign of the wrapped difference says which way to turn; with y down, positive is clockwise.
  • Turn by at most speed × dt each frame, and snap to the target when the gap is smaller than one step.
  • Check how your language's remainder treats negative numbers before trusting the formula.
