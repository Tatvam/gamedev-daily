Radians and the unit circle: why does my turret point the wrong way?

Why math.cos wants radians, how to convert from degrees, and how cos and sin give the point on a circle that aims a turret or places an orbiting shield.

You want your turret to point straight down the screen, so you write angle = 90 and aim with math.cos(angle) and math.sin(angle). The barrel points down and to the left instead, about 117 degrees round. Lua measured your 90 in a different unit.

This lesson explains that unit, the radian, and where cos and sin come from.

Two ways to measure a turn

An angle is an amount of turning. Degrees split one full turn into 360 equal parts. 360 is a human choice that divides evenly by many numbers; nothing in a circle demands it.

Radians measure the turn with the circle itself. Draw a circle, start at its rightmost point and walk along the edge. The angle in radians is how far you walked, measured in radiuses. Walk exactly one radius along the edge and you have turned 1 radian, a little under 60 degrees.

How many radiuses fit round the whole edge? The distance round a circle is 2 × π × radius, where π (the Greek letter pi) is the fixed number 3.14159... So a full turn is 2π radians, about 6.28, and half a turn is π radians, about 3.14.

Converting

Half a turn is 180 degrees and also π radians. That one fact gives both conversions:

radians = degrees × π / 180
degrees = radians × 180 / π

Worked example: 90 degrees × π / 180 = π / 2 ≈ 1.571 radians. Going back, 2 radians × 180 / π ≈ 114.6 degrees.

Now the turret makes sense. Lua's math.cos and math.sin take radians. 90 radians is about 14.3 full turns. Throw away the 14 whole turns and 0.32 of a turn is left: about 117 degrees. Lua has both conversions built in: math.rad(90) gives 1.571 and math.deg(2) gives 114.6.

The unit circle

The unit circle is a circle of radius 1 centred on the origin, the point (0, 0). Start at (1, 0) and turn by an angle a. You land on a point of the circle, and that point has two numbers:

  • its x is called the cosine of a, written cos a;
  • its y is called the sine of a, written sin a.

That is the whole definition. The point at angle a is (cos a, sin a).

Worked example at 60 degrees: cos 60° = 0.5 and sin 60° ≈ 0.866, so the point is (0.5, 0.866). Check its distance from the centre with Pythagoras: 0.5² + 0.866² = 0.25 + 0.75 = 1. Every point of the unit circle has length 1, so (cos a, sin a) is always a unit vector: a pure direction, ready to multiply by a speed or a distance.

Which way is positive?

The demo and the code use y pointing down, as on a screen and in LÖVE. There, sin 90° = 1 means one step down, so a growing angle turns clockwise on screen. In maths books and in Unity, y points up and the same growing angle turns anticlockwise. The numbers are identical; only the picture is mirrored.

From a circle to a game

Scale the unit circle up and move it anywhere. The point at angle a on a circle of radius r around a centre (cx, cy) is:

x = cx + cos(a) × r
y = cy + sin(a) × r

A turret at (400, 300) with a barrel 20 pixels long, aimed at 60 degrees, has its barrel tip at (400 + 0.5 × 20, 300 + 0.866 × 20) = (410, 317.3). A bullet fired at 300 pixels per second gets the velocity (0.5 × 300, 0.866 × 300) = (150, 259.8).

Rule of thumb

Store and compute angles in radians. Convert to degrees only at the edges: when a designer types a number into a settings file, or when you print one for a human to read.

The code

A turret you turn with the arrow keys at 45 degrees per second and fire with Space. It starts at 60 degrees, with the same numbers as the worked example.

-- y grows downwards, like LÖVE's screen, so a growing angle turns clockwise.
-- math.cos and math.sin take radians. math.rad and math.deg also do this.
local function toRadians(degrees) return degrees * math.pi / 180 end
local function toDegrees(radians) return radians * 180 / math.pi end

-- The point on the unit circle at this angle: always length 1.
local function direction(angle)
  return math.cos(angle), math.sin(angle)
end

local turret = { x = 400, y = 300, angle = toRadians(60), barrel = 20 }
local bullets = {}
local BULLET_SPEED = 300           -- pixels per second
local TURN_SPEED = toRadians(45)   -- 45 degrees per second, in radians

local function barrelTip(t)
  local dx, dy = direction(t.angle)
  return t.x + dx * t.barrel, t.y + dy * t.barrel   -- 60 deg: (410, 317.3)
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "space" then
    local x, y = barrelTip(turret)
    local dx, dy = direction(turret.angle)
    table.insert(bullets, { x = x, y = y,
      vx = dx * BULLET_SPEED, vy = dy * BULLET_SPEED })  -- (150, 259.8)
  end
end

function love.update(dt)
  if love.keyboard.isDown("right") then turret.angle = turret.angle + TURN_SPEED * dt end
  if love.keyboard.isDown("left") then turret.angle = turret.angle - TURN_SPEED * dt end
  -- Keep the angle between 0 and 2 pi. Lua's % gives a positive result
  -- even for negative angles: -1 % (2 * math.pi) is about 5.28.
  turret.angle = turret.angle % (2 * math.pi)
  for _, b in ipairs(bullets) do
    b.x = b.x + b.vx * dt
    b.y = b.y + b.vy * dt
  end
end

function love.draw()
  local tipX, tipY = barrelTip(turret)
  love.graphics.circle("line", turret.x, turret.y, 12)
  love.graphics.line(turret.x, turret.y, tipX, tipY)
  for _, b in ipairs(bullets) do
    love.graphics.circle("fill", b.x, b.y, 3)
  end
  love.graphics.print(string.format("%.0f deg = %.3f rad",
    toDegrees(turret.angle), turret.angle), 10, 10)
end

The demo lets its angle pass 360 to show the repeat; the code wraps it with %, which does not change the aim.

Built-in versions: Godot 4 has deg_to_rad, rad_to_deg and Vector2.from_angle. Unity has the constants Mathf.Deg2Rad and Mathf.Rad2Deg, and Mathf.Cos / Mathf.Sin take radians.

Pitfalls

  • Degrees into cos and sin. math.cos(90) is about −0.448, not 0. If an object points at a strange, almost random angle, look for a missing conversion.
  • Engines mix units. LÖVE's love.graphics.rotate takes radians. Godot's rotation is radians but rotation_degrees is degrees. Unity's transform.eulerAngles is degrees while Mathf.Sin wants radians.
  • Expecting exact zeros. π cannot be stored exactly, so math.cos(math.pi / 2) gives about 0.00000000000000006, not 0. Compare with a small tolerance, not ==.
  • Forgetting which way y points. With y down, 90 degrees is straight down and positive angles turn clockwise. With y up it is straight up and anticlockwise. Draw sprites facing right, where angle 0 points.
  • Angles past 360 or below 0. 420 and −300 degrees land on the same point as 60, so cos and sin do not care. Comparing angles does care: 350 and 10 degrees are 20 degrees apart, not 340. A later lesson in this track handles that.
  • No angle at the centre. A point exactly at the centre is a zero-length arrow and has no angle. When you turn a mouse position into an angle, keep the previous angle if the mouse is on the turret, as the demo does.

In real games

Turrets, twin-stick shooters and top-down characters store a facing angle and turn it into a direction with cos and sin. Orbiting shields and rotating hazards add to an angle every frame and place things on a circle. Radial bullet patterns place shots at equal angle steps round a circle; Track 2 builds those later with polar coordinates.

Editors usually show degrees for people to type, while the maths functions take radians, which is why Godot and Unity ship conversion helpers.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Make three shield orbs circle the player at a radius of 40 pixels. Give each orb the same angle plus a fixed offset: 0, 2π/3 and 4π/3 radians (0, 120 and 240 degrees). In love.update(dt) add 2 radians per second to the shared angle, and place each orb with x = cx + math.cos(a) * r, y = cy + math.sin(a) * r.

You are done when the three orbs stay evenly spaced, go round clockwise about once every 3.1 seconds (2π divided by 2), and swapping the angle step to −2 sends them the other way.

Takeaways

  • A radian is the angle whose arc is one radius long. A full turn is 2π radians, half a turn is π.
  • Convert with radians = degrees × π / 180, or math.rad and math.deg. cos and sin always want radians.
  • The point at angle a on the unit circle is (cos a, sin a), and it always has length 1.
  • Centre + (cos a, sin a) × r places anything on a circle: barrel tips, orbits, bullet directions.
  • With y down, positive angles turn clockwise on screen; with y up, anticlockwise.
