Rotating a vector: where is the tip of the sword?

How cos and sin rotate a point around the origin, and how "subtract, rotate, add back" turns a sword or any shape around its own pivot.

Your hero swings a sword. Drawing it is easy: you hand the sprite an angle and the engine turns it. But the hit test needs to know where the tip is after a 90 degree swing, and the engine will not tell you.

Or you rotate the points yourself and the sword flies off in a huge circle round the corner of the screen instead of turning in the hero's hand. This lesson fixes both problems with one small formula.

Rotating means turning every step you take

This lesson and its demo use y pointing down, as on a screen and in LÖVE. So a positive angle turns clockwise, and 90 degrees turns "right" into "down", just as in the atan2 lesson.

Start with the case you already know. The unit circle lesson showed that turning the arrow (1, 0) by an angle a lands on (cos a, sin a). Now turn the other basic arrow, (0, 1), which points straight down. It starts a quarter turn ahead, so it lands a quarter turn ahead too: on (−sin a, cos a). At a = 0 that is (0, 1) again, and at a = 90 degrees it is (−1, 0), pointing left. Down turned clockwise by a quarter is left, so that checks out.

Any point can be reached with those two arrows. The point p = (3, 1) means "3 steps of (1, 0), then 1 step of (0, 1)". A rotation turns the whole picture around the origin without stretching it, so the rotated point p′ (say "p prime") is reached by the same steps, taken along the turned arrows.

The formula

Add up the turned steps: x lots of (cos a, sin a) plus y lots of (−sin a, cos a). Collect the x parts and the y parts and you have the rotation formula:

x′ = x × cos a − y × sin a
y′ = x × sin a + y × cos a

Here x and y are the point before, x′ and y′ the point after, and a the angle in radians. Worked example: rotate (3, 1) by 90 degrees. cos 90° = 0 and sin 90° = 1, so x′ = 3 × 0 − 1 × 1 = −1 and y′ = 3 × 1 + 1 × 0 = 3. The answer is (−1, 3): down and a little left, a quarter turn clockwise from right and a little down. Its length is √(1 + 9) = √10, the same as before. Rotation never changes a length.

Big and negative angles need no special care. cos and sin repeat every full turn, so 450 degrees gives the same (−1, 3) as 90, and −90 degrees gives (1, −3), a quarter turn anticlockwise.

Rotating around a pivot

The formula always turns around the origin, (0, 0). A sword should turn around the hand that holds it. That point is the pivot. The trick is to make the pivot the origin for a moment:

  1. Subtract the pivot from the point. What is left is the offset: the point as seen from the pivot.
  2. Rotate the offset with the formula.
  3. Add the pivot back.

Worked example, the sword from the demo: the hand is at (2, 2) and the tip at (5, 3). Swing 90 degrees. The offset is (5 − 2, 3 − 2) = (3, 1), which rotates to (−1, 3) as before, and (2 + (−1), 2 + 3) = (1, 5). The tip moved round a circle centred on the hand, which is what a swing should do.

The flying sword

Skip steps 1 and 3 and you rotate (5, 3) around the origin instead: it lands on (−3, 5), far from the hand. If a shape swings in a wide circle around the corner of the world, you forgot the pivot.

Try it

Drag anywhere on the grid to swing the sword, or use the Angle slider. Drag the orange pivot to choose what the sword turns around. Keyboard: arrow keys move the pivot one square, Q and E turn by 15 degrees.

Watch the tip stay on the dashed circle around the pivot, and the readouts follow the three steps. Drag the pivot to the middle of the blade and it spins like a baton. Then switch on Forget the pivot and see the sword leave the hand.

The code

Two functions, then the sword from the demo swinging at 90 degrees per second. The tip starts at (5, 3) and is at (1, 5) after one second, as in the worked example.

-- y grows downwards, as on LÖVE's screen, so a positive angle turns clockwise.
-- Angles are in radians.
local function rotate(x, y, angle)
  local c, s = math.cos(angle), math.sin(angle)
  return x * c - y * s, x * s + y * c
end

local function rotateAround(x, y, pivotX, pivotY, angle)
  local rx, ry = rotate(x - pivotX, y - pivotY, angle)   -- 1. subtract, 2. rotate
  return pivotX + rx, pivotY + ry                          -- 3. add back
end

local UNIT = 40                           -- pixels per grid square
local ORIGIN_X, ORIGIN_Y = 400, 300       -- where (0, 0) is on screen
-- The sword at angle 0, in squares: pommel, edge, tip, edge.
local sword = { {1.4, 1.8}, {2.7, 1.9}, {5, 3}, {2.5, 2.5} }
local pivot = { x = 2, y = 2 }            -- the hand
local angle = 0
local SWING_SPEED = math.rad(90)          -- radians per second

function love.update(dt)
  angle = angle + SWING_SPEED * dt
end

function love.draw()
  local points = {}
  for _, p in ipairs(sword) do            -- always rotate the ORIGINAL points
    local x, y = rotateAround(p[1], p[2], pivot.x, pivot.y, angle)
    table.insert(points, ORIGIN_X + x * UNIT)
    table.insert(points, ORIGIN_Y + y * UNIT)
  end
  love.graphics.polygon("line", points)
  local tipX, tipY = rotateAround(5, 3, pivot.x, pivot.y, angle)
  love.graphics.print(string.format("tip (%.2f, %.2f)", tipX, tipY), 10, 10)
end

To check by hand: rotate(3, 1, math.pi / 2) returns about −1 and 3.

Built-in versions: Godot has Vector2.rotated(angle), in radians. Unity has no Vector2 rotate, but Quaternion.Euler(0, 0, degrees) * v does the job, in degrees. Both rotate around the origin, so you still subtract and add the pivot.

Pitfalls

  • Using the new x to work out the new y. Writing x = x * c - y * s and then y = x * s + y * c feeds the already-changed x into the second line. (3, 1) at 90 degrees becomes (−1, −1) instead of (−1, 3), and the shape shrinks. Compute both into new names first, as rotate does by returning two values.
  • Forgetting the pivot. The formula turns around (0, 0). Subtract the pivot first and add it back after, or the shape swings around the world origin.
  • Degrees instead of radians. math.cos(90) treats 90 as radians, about 14 full turns plus a bit. Convert with math.rad.
  • Rotating the rotated points again each frame. Turning last frame's result by a small step adds a tiny rounding error every time, and the errors pile up, worse with 32-bit floats. Keep the original points and one angle, and rotate the originals every frame, as the code does.
  • Expecting exact answers. cos(π / 2) is not exactly 0 in floating point, so you get −0.9999999999999998 instead of −1. Round for display and never compare rotated positions with ==.
  • Edge cases that are fine. A point exactly on the pivot has offset (0, 0) and stays put. Nothing is divided, so no zero-length check is needed. Angles past 180, past 360 or below 0 all work, because cos and sin repeat.
  • The y direction. With y up, as in maths books and Unity, the same formula turns anticlockwise for positive angles.

In real games

Drawing a rotated sprite is done for you: in LÖVE, love.graphics.draw takes a rotation and an origin offset, which is the pivot. You need the formula when you need to know where something is: the tip of a swinging weapon for a hit test, the corners of a turned box for collision, or a muzzle offset to the side of a gun, where the bullets appear.

A ship drawn with lines, in the style of classic asteroid-shooting games, can be stored as a list of points and rotated around the ship's centre every frame. Falling-block puzzle games turn a piece a quarter at a time around a pivot cell. At 90 degrees the formula becomes simply (x, y) → (−y, x), as the worked example showed.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Take the turret from the atan2 lesson and give its gun a muzzle that is not on the centre line: 24 pixels forward and 6 pixels to the side, the offset (24, 6) at angle 0. Each frame, aim at the mouse with math.atan2, then find the muzzle with rotateAround(turret.x + 24, turret.y + 6, turret.x, turret.y, angle) and spawn bullets there.

You are done when bullets come out of the same spot on the gun whichever way it points, including when aiming left and straight up, where a muzzle that was not rotated would visibly miss the barrel.

Takeaways

  • To rotate (x, y) by a around the origin: x′ = x cos a − y sin a, y′ = x sin a + y cos a.
  • It works because rotation turns the two basic arrows to (cos a, sin a) and (−sin a, cos a) and keeps the recipe of steps.
  • To rotate around a pivot: subtract the pivot, rotate, add the pivot back.
  • Rotation keeps lengths, accepts any angle, and needs radians. With y down, positive is clockwise.
  • Rotate the original points by the total angle each frame, never the previous frame's result.
