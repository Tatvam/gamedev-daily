Exponential smoothing: why does my camera lag more at 30 fps?

Why x = lerp(x, target, 0.1) every frame chases slower on slow machines, and how one power of dt makes the chase the same at any frame rate.

Your camera follows the player with one friendly line: every frame, move a tenth of the way to the player. On your 60 fps monitor it feels tight. A tester on a laptop running at 30 fps says the camera drags far behind, and a friend with a 144 Hz screen says it feels glued on. Same code, three different cameras.

Closing a share of the gap

Easing, from the last lesson, needs a start, an end and a duration. A camera has none of those: the player keeps moving. So instead we take a step from wherever we are now towards wherever the target is now:

x = lerp(x, target, k)       -- every frame

Here x is the follower's position, target is where it wants to be, and k is the blend: the share of the remaining distance closed in one frame. Call the distance still to go the gap.

Worked example with k = 0.1 and a gap of 100 pixels. Frame 1 closes 10 and leaves 90. Frame 2 closes 10 percent of 90, which is 9, and leaves 81. Then 72.9, then 65.6. Each frame the gap is multiplied by the same number, 1 − k = 0.9. We will call that number keep, the share of the gap that survives a frame. A quantity that is multiplied by the same factor again and again shrinks exponentially, which gives this technique its name: exponential smoothing. It starts fast and slows as it gets close: a soft arrival.

The hidden assumption: every frame is the same length

The line says "per frame", but a frame lasts 1/60 of a second on one machine and 1/30 on another. At 30 fps the follower gets half as many multiplications by 0.9 in the same time. Here is the gap left half a second after the target jumps 100 pixels, with k = 0.1:

Frame rateFrames in 0.5 sGap left (naive)Gap left (corrected)
20 fps1034.94.2
30 fps1520.64.2
60 fps304.24.2
144 fps720.054.2

The naive column is 0.9 multiplied by itself once per frame: 0.915 × 100 ≈ 20.6 at 30 fps. The 30 fps camera is five times further behind than the 60 fps one. The right-hand column is what we want: the same answer on every machine.

The fix: count in sixtieths of a second

You tuned k = 0.1 while playing at 60 fps, so what you really meant is "keep 0.9 of the gap every sixtieth of a second". A frame that lasts dt seconds (delta time, the length of the frame) contains dt × 60 sixtieths. Keeping 0.9 once for each of them means multiplying 0.9 by itself dt × 60 times, which is a power:

keep  = (1 - k) ^ (dt * 60)
blend = 1 - keep
x = lerp(x, target, blend)

Worked example at 30 fps, so dt = 1/30 and dt × 60 = 2. keep = 0.92 = 0.81, so blend = 0.19. A gap of 100 becomes 81 in one frame: exactly what two frames at 60 fps gave above (100, then 90, then 81). At 60 fps, dt × 60 = 1 and keep = 0.9, so nothing changes for you. The power does not have to be a whole number: at 144 fps it is 0.4167, and keep = 0.90.4167 ≈ 0.957.

The same formula in other clothes

You will meet this formula written two other ways. Both give the same keep; they just tune it with a different knob.

  • A rate per second: keep = e−rate × dt, written math.exp(-rate * dt). The number e is about 2.718. k = 0.1 at 60 fps matches rate = −60 × ln(0.9) ≈ 6.32 per second.
  • A half-life: keep = 2−dt / halfLife, where halfLife is the time it takes to close half of the gap. Worked example: halfLife = 0.25 s and dt = 0.5 s gives keep = 2−2 = 0.25, two halvings. k = 0.1 at 60 fps matches a half-life of about 0.11 s.

Multiplying k by dt is not the fix

A common half-fix is lerp(x, target, speed * dt). It is close at high frame rates, but not equal, and it breaks on a long frame. With speed = 6 and a 0.25 second hitch while a level loads, the blend is 1.5: the follower jumps past the target by half the gap. Above 2 it flies off further every frame. The power form never does this: keep stays between 0 and 1 for any dt, so the worst a long frame can do is arrive.

The code

One function that does the chase, with the same k and the same numbers as the demo, and a LÖVE program where a disc follows the mouse.

-- Move current towards target, closing share k of the gap
-- per sixtieth of a second, at any frame rate.
local function smooth(current, target, k, dt)
  k = math.max(0, math.min(1, k))      -- keep 1 - k from going negative
  local keep = (1 - k) ^ (dt * 60)     -- share of the gap left after dt
  return target + (current - target) * keep
end

print(smooth(0, 100, 0.1, 1 / 60))    -- 10   (one 60 fps frame)
print(smooth(0, 100, 0.1, 1 / 30))    -- 19   (same as two 60 fps frames)

-- Screen coordinates: x grows right, y grows down, as in LÖVE.
local K = 0.1
local follower = { x = 100, y = 100 }

function love.update(dt)
  local mx, my = love.mouse.getPosition()
  follower.x = smooth(follower.x, mx, K, dt)
  follower.y = smooth(follower.y, my, K, dt)
end

function love.draw()
  love.graphics.circle("fill", follower.x, follower.y, 12)
end

target + (current - target) * keep is the same as lerp(current, target, 1 - keep), written so that you can see the gap being multiplied by keep. Neither Godot nor Unity has this exact function built in, but both have the pieces: lerp and pow in Godot, Mathf.Lerp and Mathf.Pow in Unity.

Pitfalls

  • Using the frame count as the clock. Any "per frame" constant makes the game feel different on every machine. Switching to smooth keeps your old k: at 60 fps nothing changes.
  • k outside 0 to 1. With k = 1.2, 1 − k is −0.2, and a negative number raised to a fractional power has no real answer: Lua returns nan, and your object vanishes. The clamp in smooth prevents this. k = 0 never moves; k = 1 snaps straight to the target.
  • It never quite arrives. Multiplying by 0.9 never reaches zero. If game logic needs "arrived", test whether the gap is below a small distance, such as half a pixel, and then set the position to the target.
  • A dt of zero, or a huge one. dt = 0, for example while paused, gives keep = 1 and no movement, which is right. A very long frame gives keep close to 0, so the follower lands on the target without overshooting. Compare that with speed * dt in the warning above.
  • Smoothing an angle. Smoothing a rotation from 350 degrees towards 10 goes the long way round, through 180. Wrap the difference first, as in the angle wrapping lesson, and smooth that.

In real games

Exponential smoothing is one of the most common tricks in game code: cameras that follow a player, a pet that trails the hero, a health bar whose "damage trail" drains after the real value, a volume that fades towards a new level. Anywhere a value should drift towards a target that keeps changing, try this first.

Unity also offers Vector3.SmoothDamp, a different method that follows with a spring-like motion and keeps a velocity, so it starts gently too.

Takeaways

  • x = lerp(x, target, k) each frame multiplies the gap by keep = 1 − k each frame: exponential smoothing.
  • "Each frame" depends on the machine, so the naive line is slower at 30 fps and faster at 144 fps.
  • Raise keep to the power dt × 60: keep = (1 - k) ^ (dt * 60). At 30 fps that is 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81 per frame.
  • The rate form math.exp(-rate * dt) and the half-life form 2 ^ (-dt / halfLife) are the same idea with different knobs.
  • Clamp k to 0 to 1, snap when the gap is tiny, and never use k * dt as a blend.
