The 2D cross product: is the target to my left or my right?

How one subtraction, f.x × t.y − f.y × t.x, tells a ship which way to turn towards a target, and how it relates to perpendicular vectors.

Your homing missile can only turn so fast. Each frame it must decide: steer left or steer right? The dot product cannot answer, because a target 30 degrees to the left and one 30 degrees to the right give the same dot product.

You need a number that is positive on one side and negative on the other: the 2D cross product.

One line splits the world in two

Extend the ship's nose arrow forwards and backwards into a long line. Every point is on one side of it, or exactly on it. "Left or right?" means "which side of the line?"

We need two arrows, both starting at the ship:

  • f, the facing arrow: the way the nose points.
  • t, the arrow from the ship to the target: target position minus ship position, as in the subtraction lesson.

Positions are in grid squares and y grows downwards, as on a screen and in LÖVE. Here the ship faces f = (2, 1), right and a little down, and the target is at t = (1, 3).

The formula

The 2D cross product of two arrows a and b is one number:

cross(a, b)  =  a.x × b.y  −  a.y × b.x

Read it as "a's x times b's y, minus a's y times b's x". Each x pairs with the other arrow's y.

For the ship: cross(f, t) = 2 × 3 − 1 × 1 = 5. Positive. A second target at t₂ = (3, −2), up and to the right on screen, gives 2 × (−2) − 1 × 3 = −7. Negative. A target straight ahead at (4, 2) gives 2 × 2 − 1 × 4 = 0: it sits on the line.

With y pointing down, positive means the target is clockwise from the nose: on the ship's right. Negative means anticlockwise: on its left. Zero means on the line, straight ahead or straight behind.

The size of the number is the area of the parallelogram the two arrows make, the shaded shape above. For turning we only need the sign.

Where it comes from: a dot product with a side arrow

Turn f a quarter turn by swapping its parts and negating the new x:

perp(f)  =  (−f.y, f.x)        (2, 1)  becomes  (−1, 2)

Call this p, the perpendicular of f: an arrow at a right angle to it. Check with the dot product: (2, 1) · (−1, 2) = −2 + 2 = 0, and a dot product of zero means a right angle. With y down, this quarter turn is clockwise on screen, so p points out of the ship's right side.

Now ask the dot product question "does t point the same way as p?": p · t = −1 × 1 + 2 × 3 = 5. The same 5 as before. Write it out with letters and you get −f.y × t.x + f.x × t.y, which is the cross product formula rearranged. That is why it is also called the perp-dot product: the dot product of the perpendicular.

So the dot product with f says "in front or behind?" and the dot product with p, the cross product, says "right or left?"

The other quarter turn, (f.y, −f.x), points out of the other side. The last lesson used it for a wall's normal: (4, −3) became (−3, −4), then n = (−0.6, −0.8) after dividing by 5. Choosing between the two picks which face is the front.

Turning towards the target

Store the ship's heading as an angle. With y down, adding to it turns clockwise on screen. Each frame:

  1. Work out c = cross(f, t).
  2. If c is positive, add turn speed × dt to the angle. If negative, subtract it.
  3. If the sign of the cross product flipped after turning, the nose swung past the target. Point it straight at the target instead.

No angles are subtracted, so nothing goes wrong when an angle passes 180 degrees. The sign always picks the shorter turn.

Rule of thumb

cross(facing, toTarget) > 0 means "turn the way positive angles go". With y down that is clockwise, to the right. With y up, as in maths books and Unity, the same positive number means anticlockwise, to the left. The arithmetic never changes; only the picture is mirrored.

The code

A ship that turns at 90 degrees per second towards a fixed target. With 30 pixels per grid square, the target sits at (1, 3) squares from the ship and the ship starts facing (2, 1), as in the worked example and the demo.

-- Vectors are tables { x, y }. y grows downwards, like LÖVE's screen.
local function vec(x, y) return { x = x, y = y } end
local function dot(a, b) return a.x * b.x + a.y * b.y end
local function sub(a, b) return vec(a.x - b.x, a.y - b.y) end

-- 2D cross product. With y down: positive = b is clockwise from a
-- (to a's right), negative = anticlockwise (to its left), 0 = in line.
local function cross(a, b)
  return a.x * b.y - a.y * b.x
end

-- a turned a quarter turn clockwise on screen: (2, 1) becomes (-1, 2).
-- cross(a, b) is the same number as dot(perp(a), b).
local function perp(a) return vec(-a.y, a.x) end

local ship = { pos = vec(400, 300), angle = math.atan2(1, 2) } -- faces (2, 1)
local target = vec(430, 390)          -- (1, 3) squares away, 30 px each
local turnSpeed = math.rad(90)        -- 90 degrees per second

local function facing(s)
  return vec(math.cos(s.angle), math.sin(s.angle))
end

function love.update(dt)
  local to = sub(target, ship.pos)
  local c = cross(facing(ship), to)
  if c == 0 and dot(facing(ship), to) < 0 then
    c = 1                             -- directly behind: pick a side
  end
  if c > 0 then
    ship.angle = ship.angle + turnSpeed * dt  -- clockwise, right
  elseif c < 0 then
    ship.angle = ship.angle - turnSpeed * dt  -- anticlockwise, left
  end
  -- If the sign flipped, we turned past the target: face it exactly.
  local after = cross(facing(ship), to)
  if (c > 0 and after < 0) or (c < 0 and after > 0) then
    ship.angle = math.atan2(to.y, to.x)
  end
end

function love.draw()
  local f = facing(ship)
  local p = perp(f)
  local side = cross(f, sub(target, ship.pos))
  love.graphics.circle("fill", target.x, target.y, 6)
  love.graphics.line(ship.pos.x, ship.pos.y, ship.pos.x + f.x * 40, ship.pos.y + f.y * 40)
  love.graphics.line(ship.pos.x, ship.pos.y, ship.pos.x + p.x * 20, ship.pos.y + p.y * 20)
  love.graphics.print(string.format("cross: %.1f", side), 10, 10)
end

Here the facing has length 1 and the target is in pixels, so the printed number starts at 67.1, not 5. The sign is the same, and the sign is all the turning uses. math.cos, math.sin and math.atan2 convert between angles and arrows; Track 2 explains them.

Built-in versions: Godot has Vector2.cross, which returns this number. Unity has no 2D version; Vector3.Cross(a, b).z gives the same value for arrows with z = 0. Remember that Unity's y points up.

Pitfalls

  • Mixing up which way y points. The same positive number is "right" with y down and "left" with y up. If your ship turns away from the target, check this before anything else.
  • Swapping the order. cross(t, f) is minus cross(f, t): (1, 3) and (2, 1) give 1 × 1 − 3 × 2 = −5. Always put the facing first and the target second, and keep it that way everywhere.
  • Directly behind. A target exactly behind the ship gives 0, just like one dead ahead: with f = (2, 1), the target (−4, −2) gives 2 × (−2) − 1 × (−4) = 0. A ship that only turns on a non-zero sign will fly away from it forever. Use the dot product to tell the two apart (negative means behind) and pick a side.
  • Zero-length arrows. A target on the ship gives t = (0, 0), so both products are 0 and the code does nothing, which is right. A facing of (0, 0) would also give 0 for every target, so never let it shrink to nothing.
  • Wiggling on arrival. If the ship turns by a full turn speed × dt when it is almost lined up, it overshoots, then overshoots back, and the nose shakes. Snapping to the target when the sign flips stops it.
  • Reading the size as an angle. A far target gives a bigger number than a near one at the same angle, and even with arrows of length 1, targets 30 and 150 degrees off give the same value. Use the sign for the side and the dot product for front or back.

In real games

Anything that turns at a limited rate needs a left-or-right decision every frame: homing missiles, turrets, computer-driven cars. The cross product sign is a common way to make it, because it needs no angle wrapping.

The same "which side of this line?" test is a building block of geometry code: checking whether two segments cross and whether a polygon's corners run clockwise both use it, and both come later in this course. In 3D, the cross product of two arrows is a third arrow; the 2D number is that arrow's z part.

Paste the code into a LÖVE project. In love.update(dt), set the target to the mouse with target = vec(love.mouse.getPosition()). Draw the ship as a line or triangle along the facing arrow, and print "left", "right" or "ahead" from the sign of the cross product.

Takeaways

  • cross(a, b) = a.x × b.y − a.y × b.x is one number whose sign says which side of a the arrow b is on.
  • With y down, positive is clockwise (right); with y up, positive is anticlockwise (left).
  • It equals the dot product of perp(a) = (−a.y, a.x) with b, so it is a "left or right?" dot product.
  • Turn by the sign each frame, snap when the sign flips, and use the dot product to break the tie when the target is directly behind.
