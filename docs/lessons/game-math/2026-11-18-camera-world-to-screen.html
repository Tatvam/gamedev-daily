Cameras and world-to-screen: which tree did the player click?

How a 2D camera is an inverse transform that pans, zooms and turns the world, and how to turn a mouse click back into world coordinates.

Your map is 1200 pixels wide and the window is 800, so you add a camera that scrolls. Now clicking a tree selects the grass beside it, and zooming makes it worse. The mouse reports a screen position; your game thinks in the world. The camera sits between the two.

The screen is the camera's local space

As in the last four lessons, y points down, as on a screen and in LÖVE, so a positive angle turns clockwise. One world unit is one screen pixel when the camera is not zoomed.

A 2D camera is an object in the world with three settings:

  • position: the world point that appears in the centre of the screen,
  • zoom: how many screen pixels one world unit takes up (2 means everything looks twice as big),
  • angle: how far the camera is turned.

The screen shows the rectangle of world the camera sees. Below, the camera sits at (1000, 500) with zoom 2, so the 800 by 600 pixel screen shows only 400 by 300 world units.

From yesterday's lesson: the screen is the camera's local space. Drawing converts every world point into the camera's space, which is the inverse of the camera's transform. That is why a camera feels backwards: move it right and the world slides left, turn it clockwise and the world turns anticlockwise.

World to screen in four steps

Undo the camera's move, then its turn, apply the zoom, and shift the origin from the screen centre to the top-left corner.

Worked example, camera at (1000, 500), zoom 2, angle 0, screen 800 by 600, so its centre is (400, 300). For the world point P = (1100, 450):

1. minus camera:   (1100 − 1000, 450 − 500)  = (100, −50)
2. turn by −0°:    (100, −50)
3. times zoom 2:   (200, −100)
4. plus centre:    (200 + 400, −100 + 300)   = (600, 200)

Step 2 is the rotation from the rotating vectors lesson, by minus the camera's angle. Writing cos and sin for the cosine and sine of the camera's angle, an offset (dx, dy) becomes (dx × cos + dy × sin, −dx × sin + dy × cos). Turn the same camera 90 degrees clockwise (cos = 0, sin = 1) and the offset (100, −50) becomes (−50, −100). Times 2 is (−100, −200), plus the centre is (300, 100). P was to the camera's right; on screen it is now above the centre.

Screen to world: the same steps backwards

Undo each step, last one first. For a click at (300, 100) with the turned camera:

4. minus centre:   (300 − 400, 100 − 300)    = (−100, −200)
3. divide by 2:    (−50, −100)
2. turn by +90°:   (dx × cos − dy × sin, dx × sin + dy × cos) = (100, −50)
1. plus camera:    (100 + 1000, −50 + 500)   = (1100, 450)

Back at P. This is picking: finding which world point sits under the mouse.

In matrix terms, the camera's transform is Move(position) × Rotate(angle) × Scale(1 / zoom), and drawing uses its inverse with the centre shift in front: Move(centre) × Scale(zoom) × Rotate(−angle) × Move(−position).

Zooming towards the mouse

Changing only the zoom grows the world around the screen centre. To zoom around the cursor instead, note the world point under the mouse, change the zoom, see which world point is under it now, and move the camera by the difference.

Example: camera (1000, 500), zoom 1, mouse at screen (600, 200). Before, the mouse is over world (1200, 400). After zooming to 2 it is over (1100, 450). Move the camera by (1200 − 1100, 400 − 450) = (100, −50) to (1100, 450), and the mouse is over (1200, 400) again. Drag-panning uses the same idea: keep the grabbed world point under the pointer.

Rule of thumb

Input arrives in screen space, game logic lives in world space. Convert at the door: turn every mouse or touch position into world coordinates the moment it comes in, and draw the interface after the camera is switched off.

Try it

Drag to pan the map, scroll or use the zoom slider to zoom, and click or tap without dragging to plant a red flag. Keyboard: click the canvas, then arrow keys pan, + and − zoom, Q and E turn the camera, and Space plants a flag at the centre.

Plant a flag on a tree, then pan, zoom and turn: it stays on the tree because it is stored in world coordinates. The pointer's world position changes as you pan even when the mouse stands still. Then switch picking to raw screen position and click: the flag lands elsewhere, the bug from the opening.

The code

A camera with the two conversions, zoom towards the mouse, drag to pan with the right button, and left-click picking. The names match the demo; the demo starts zoomed out to fit your screen.

-- y grows downwards, so a positive angle turns clockwise.
local camera = { x = 400, y = 300, zoom = 1, angle = 0 }
local flag = nil

local function worldToScreen(cam, wx, wy)
  local cosA, sinA = math.cos(cam.angle), math.sin(cam.angle)
  local dx, dy = wx - cam.x, wy - cam.y                 -- 1. minus camera
  local rx, ry = dx * cosA + dy * sinA, -dx * sinA + dy * cosA  -- 2. turn back
  return rx * cam.zoom + love.graphics.getWidth() / 2,  -- 3. zoom, 4. centre
         ry * cam.zoom + love.graphics.getHeight() / 2
end

local function screenToWorld(cam, sx, sy)
  local cosA, sinA = math.cos(cam.angle), math.sin(cam.angle)
  local rx = (sx - love.graphics.getWidth() / 2) / cam.zoom
  local ry = (sy - love.graphics.getHeight() / 2) / cam.zoom
  return rx * cosA - ry * sinA + cam.x, rx * sinA + ry * cosA + cam.y
end

local function zoomAt(cam, sx, sy, factor)
  local beforeX, beforeY = screenToWorld(cam, sx, sy)
  cam.zoom = math.max(0.25, math.min(4, cam.zoom * factor))  -- never 0
  local afterX, afterY = screenToWorld(cam, sx, sy)
  cam.x, cam.y = cam.x + beforeX - afterX, cam.y + beforeY - afterY
end

function love.wheelmoved(x, y)
  local mx, my = love.mouse.getPosition()
  zoomAt(camera, mx, my, 1.1 ^ y)
end

function love.mousemoved(x, y, dx, dy)
  if love.mouse.isDown(2) then                -- right button drags the map
    local ax, ay = screenToWorld(camera, x - dx, y - dy)
    local bx, by = screenToWorld(camera, x, y)
    camera.x, camera.y = camera.x + ax - bx, camera.y + ay - by
  end
end

function love.mousepressed(x, y, button)
  if button == 1 then
    local wx, wy = screenToWorld(camera, x, y)
    flag = { x = wx, y = wy }
  end
end

function love.draw()
  love.graphics.push()
  -- Read these four lines bottom to top: that is the order they act on points.
  love.graphics.translate(love.graphics.getWidth() / 2, love.graphics.getHeight() / 2)
  love.graphics.scale(camera.zoom, camera.zoom)
  love.graphics.rotate(-camera.angle)
  love.graphics.translate(-camera.x, -camera.y)
  love.graphics.rectangle("line", 0, 0, 1200, 800)     -- the world
  if flag then love.graphics.circle("fill", flag.x, flag.y, 6) end
  love.graphics.pop()
  -- Interface: drawn after pop, in plain screen pixels.
  if flag then
    local sx, sy = worldToScreen(camera, flag.x, flag.y)
    love.graphics.print(string.format("flag on screen at (%.0f, %.0f)", sx, sy), 10, 10)
  end
end

With an 800 by 600 window, camera = { x = 1000, y = 500, zoom = 2, angle = math.rad(90) } and a click at (300, 100), the flag lands at world (1100, 450), as in the worked example. worldToScreen is for things you place over the world in screen space, such as a name tag above a unit.

Built-in versions: in Godot, a Camera2D node does the drawing side, and get_global_mouse_position() gives the mouse in world coordinates. In Unity, Camera.main.ScreenToWorldPoint(...) and WorldToScreenPoint(...).

Pitfalls

  • Picking with raw mouse coordinates. It breaks the moment the camera moves. Convert every pointer position with screenToWorld first.
  • Panning at the wrong speed. A 100 pixel drag is 50 world units at zoom 2 and 400 at zoom 0.25, and on a turned camera it points another way. Convert the drag through the camera, as in love.mousemoved.
  • Zoom of 0 or less. At zoom 0 screenToWorld divides by zero; a negative zoom flips the picture. Clamp it, and multiply by a factor rather than adding, so it never crosses 0.
  • Interface inside the camera. A health bar drawn between push and pop scrolls and zooms with the map. Draw it after pop.
  • Wrapping the camera angle. Not needed: 270° and −90° give the same sine and cosine, so the same picture and picks. Wrap only to display it or to turn smoothly towards a target.

In real games

Every scrolling 2D game does this, whether you write it or the engine does: Godot's Camera2D and Unity's Camera offer both conversions. In LÖVE you write it yourself or use a library; the hump library's camera module has worldCoords and cameraCoords.

Strategy games and level editors pick units and tiles with screen-to-world. Floating damage numbers and name tags use world-to-screen to sit over the world.

Takeaways

  • A 2D camera is a position, a zoom and an angle; the screen is the camera's local space.
  • World to screen: minus camera, turn by minus the angle, times zoom, plus the screen centre.
  • Screen to world: the same steps undone in reverse order. Use it for every click and touch.
  • To zoom or pan around the cursor, keep the world point under it fixed and move the camera by the difference.
  • Clamp the zoom above 0 and draw the interface outside the camera.
