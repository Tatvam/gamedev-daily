Coordinates, axes and units: where is (3, 2)?

Why screen pixels count y downwards while game worlds often count it upwards, and how two small functions convert between them with a unit you choose.

Your level file says there is a coin at (3, 2). You draw it, and it appears in the top-left corner, three pixels from the edge, far too small to see. You make the character jump by adding to its y, and it sinks into the floor instead.

The numbers are fine. What is missing is an agreement about what they mean.

A pair of numbers needs three agreements

A coordinate is a number that says how far along an axis something is. An axis is a line with a direction, like a ruler. In 2D we use two axes, x and y, so a position is a pair of numbers written (x, y).

The pair (3, 2) means nothing until three things are agreed:

  1. The origin. Where is (0, 0)?
  2. The directions. Which way does x grow, and which way does y grow?
  3. The unit. How long is a step of 1?

These three agreements together make a coordinate space. Games use at least two at once.

Screen space: pixels, y down

Screen space is how the window describes itself. The origin is the top-left corner, x grows to the right, y grows downwards, and the unit is one pixel. This matches the order in which screens and images store their rows: top row first. LÖVE, Godot's 2D nodes and web pages all work this way, and the mouse position you get from them is in screen space.

World space: your units, often y up

World space is how your game describes its level. You choose all three agreements. A common choice is the maths textbook one: y grows upwards, so jumping means adding to y. The unit is whatever is useful: one tile, one metre.

The number that links the two is pixels per unit: how many screen pixels one world unit covers when drawn. With 40 pixels per unit, a character 1.5 units tall is drawn 60 pixels tall.

Converting between them

Read the diagram. To reach the coin you walk 80 pixels to the world origin, then 3 units of 40 pixels. Vertically you walk 200 pixels down to the world origin, then come back up 2 units of 40 pixels. Up means a smaller screen y, so that part is subtracted.

Name the pieces. originX and originY are the screen pixel where the world origin is drawn. ppu is pixels per unit. wx, wy is a world position and sx, sy the screen position.

sx = originX + wx * ppu
sy = originY - wy * ppu     -- minus because world y points up

Worked example: sx = 80 + 3 * 40 = 200 and sy = 200 - 2 * 40 = 120. The coin is drawn at pixel (200, 120).

Going the other way, for example to find which world position the mouse is over, undo each step in reverse order: remove the origin offset, then divide by the unit.

wx = (sx - originX) / ppu
wy = (originY - sy) / ppu

Check it with the same numbers: (200 - 80) / 40 = 3 and (200 - 120) / 40 = 2. We are back at (3, 2).

If your world keeps y pointing down like the screen, the minus becomes a plus: sy = originY + wy * ppu. The coin at (3, 2) would then be drawn at pixel (200, 280), below the floor. Same numbers, different place.

Why bother with units at all?

Many small games simply use pixels as world units. Separate units pay off in three ways. Design numbers stay readable: "jump 3 tiles high" beats "jump 120 pixels". Zoom and resolution become one number, ppu, while stored positions stay the same. And physics libraries are tuned for objects of a certain size in their units, so pixel values can make them misbehave.

Rule of thumb

Store and simulate everything in world units. Convert to screen pixels only at the moment you draw, and convert the mouse to world units the moment you read it.

Try it

The demo uses the numbers from the diagram: world origin at screen pixel (80, 200), 40 pixels per unit, world y up. Move the pointer over the grid and compare its screen and world readings. Then change the pixels per unit and notice that the coin's world numbers do not change, only where it is drawn. Finally switch off "World y points up" and watch the whole scene flip upside down around the green x axis while every world number stays the same.

The code

Two functions and three settings, with the same names and numbers as the demo. LÖVE gives the mouse in screen pixels, so it goes through screenToWorld first.

-- The three agreements of world space
local originX, originY = 80, 200  -- screen pixel where world (0, 0) is drawn
local ppu = 40                    -- pixels per world unit
local yUp = true                  -- true: world y grows upwards

local function worldToScreen(wx, wy)
  local sx = originX + wx * ppu
  local sy
  if yUp then
    sy = originY - wy * ppu
  else
    sy = originY + wy * ppu
  end
  return sx, sy
end

local function screenToWorld(sx, sy)
  local wx = (sx - originX) / ppu
  local wy
  if yUp then
    wy = (originY - sy) / ppu
  else
    wy = (sy - originY) / ppu
  end
  return wx, wy
end

local coinX, coinY = 3, 2         -- stored in world units

function love.draw()
  local sx, sy = worldToScreen(coinX, coinY)   -- 200, 120
  love.graphics.circle("fill", sx, sy, 0.3 * ppu)

  local mx, my = love.mouse.getPosition()      -- screen pixels, y down
  local wx, wy = screenToWorld(mx, my)
  love.graphics.print(string.format("mouse in world: %.2f, %.2f", wx, wy), 10, 10)
end

Engines do this through the camera. In Godot 2D, get_global_mouse_position() returns the mouse in world coordinates. In Unity, Camera.main.ScreenToWorldPoint(Input.mousePosition) does the same job.

Pitfalls

  • Mixing the two y directions. With y down, gravity is a positive number and a jump velocity is negative. With y up it is the other way round. Copying a jump from a tutorial written for the other convention makes the character fall upwards. Decide once and write it at the top of your code.
  • Rotations turn the other way. An angle that turns anticlockwise in a y-up world turns clockwise on a y-down screen, because the flip mirrors everything. This comes back when we reach angles and atan2.
  • Pixels per unit of zero. screenToWorld divides by ppu. A zoom that reaches zero gives infinities. Clamp it to a small positive minimum.
  • Storing screen positions. A position saved in pixels is wrong as soon as the window resizes or the camera zooms.
  • Rounding too early. Round the result of worldToScreen for crisp pixel art, never the stored world position, or slow objects stop moving because each tiny step rounds back to where they were.
  • Not every screen space starts top-left. Unity's Input.mousePosition has its origin in the bottom-left corner, with y up.

In real games

Godot 2D uses y down, which is why its Vector2.UP is (0, -1). Its 3D space uses y up. Unity uses y up in both 2D and 3D, so Vector2.up is (0, 1), and a sprite's import settings have a Pixels Per Unit value that defaults to 100. LÖVE draws with the origin at the top-left and y down.

Physics libraries care about units. The Box2D manual recommends working in metres and keeping moving objects roughly between 0.1 and 10 metres in size, which is why LÖVE's physics module has love.physics.setMeter to say how many pixels make a metre (30 by default).

Your turn (15 minutes)

In a new LÖVE project (or your engine of choice), paste the two functions. Place a coin at world (3, 2) and print the mouse position in world units every frame.

Move the mouse over the coin: the printout should read close to (3, 2). Now change ppu to 64 and originX, originY to (100, 300), run again, and check that the coin moved on screen but the mouse still reads (3, 2) over it. You are done when you can predict where the coin appears with yUp false before running it.

Takeaways

  • A coordinate pair only has meaning with an origin, axis directions and a unit.
  • Screen space puts (0, 0) at the top-left, counts in pixels, and usually grows y downwards.
  • World space is your choice. Many games pick y up and a unit such as a tile or a metre.
  • World to screen: multiply by pixels per unit, then add the origin, flipping y if needed. Screen to world undoes those steps in reverse.
  • Keep game data in world units and convert only at the edges: drawing and reading the mouse.
