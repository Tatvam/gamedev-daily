Cameras and viewports: from world to screen

How a 2D camera turns world coordinates into screen pixels with position, rotation and zoom, how to undo it for mouse clicks, and how Godot and Unity do it.

You add a camera that follows the player, and suddenly clicking on an enemy selects empty grass. The health bar you drew over the boss drifts away as soon as the camera zooms. Nothing is wrong with the click or the bar. They are just measured in a different coordinate system from the things they point at.

Two coordinate systems

World space is where your game objects live. A tree at (560, 280) stays at (560, 280) whatever the camera does. The units are yours: pixels in LÖVE and Godot 2D, metres-ish "units" in Unity.

Screen space is the grid of pixels you actually draw into. Its origin is a corner of the window: top left with y pointing down in LÖVE and Godot, bottom left with y pointing up in Unity.

The rectangle of pixels a camera draws into is its viewport. Usually that is the whole window, but split screen gives each player a viewport of their own. In this lesson the viewport is 320 by 180 pixels.

A camera is the description of which part of the world lands in that viewport. In 2D it has three numbers: a position (the world point at the centre of the view), a rotation, and a zoom.

The camera is a transform, run backwards

A camera never moves anything on screen by itself. To show the camera moving right, you draw the whole world moved left. Every world point goes through four steps on its way to the screen:

  1. Subtract the camera position. Now the point is measured from the camera.
  2. Rotate by minus the camera angle. Turning the camera clockwise turns the world anticlockwise.
  3. Multiply by the zoom. At zoom 2 every distance doubles, so things look twice as big.
  4. Add half the viewport size. The camera's position ends up in the middle of the viewport, not in its corner.

Try it with small numbers. The camera is at (500, 300), zoom 2, no rotation. A flag at (560, 280) becomes (60, −20) after step 1, stays (60, −20) in step 2, becomes (120, −40) after step 3 and (280, 50) after step 4. It is drawn 280 pixels from the left and 50 from the top.

Going the other way is how mouse picking works. The mouse reports a screen position, and you need the world position to know what was clicked. Undo the same four steps in reverse order: subtract half the viewport, divide by the zoom, rotate by plus the angle, add the camera position. Screen (280, 50) gives (120, −40), then (60, −20), then (60, −20), then (560, 280). The round trip lands exactly on the flag.

One consequence is worth remembering: the part of the world you can see is the viewport divided by the zoom. A 320 by 180 viewport at zoom 2 shows 160 by 90 world units. At zoom 0.5 it shows 640 by 360.

Who owns the transform

In LÖVE you own it. You push the four steps onto the graphics transform before drawing the world, and pop them before drawing the interface, which should stay put in screen space.

In Godot 4 a Camera2D node owns it. The current camera of a viewport writes the viewport's canvas transform, and every 2D node in that canvas is drawn through it. A CanvasLayer has its own transform, which is why a HUD placed in one does not move with the camera. Make a Camera2D a child of the player and the transform hierarchy makes it follow for free.

In Unity the Camera component on a GameObject owns it. The GameObject's Transform gives position and rotation; for 2D you use an orthographic camera, whose zoom is expressed as orthographicSize: half the visible height in world units. Cameras render after LateUpdate, which is why follow scripts usually move the camera there, after every object has finished moving.

Rule of thumb

Before you add, compare or store a position, ask which space it is in. Mouse positions and UI are screen space; entities are world space. Convert at the boundary, once, with the engine's function.

The right panel is the 320 by 180 viewport: what the camera sees. You can drag that too, like grabbing the map.

The code

The Lua is a LÖVE 11 program with the demo's numbers: a 320 by 180 window, a 960 by 640 world with a grid every 80 units, the flag at (560, 280), panning at 240 units per second and rotating at 90 degrees per second. Notice the four graphics calls in love.draw: read bottom to top, they are worldToScreen, applied to everything drawn between push and pop.

-- main.lua for LÖVE 11. Arrow keys pan, Q and E rotate, the mouse wheel zooms.
local VIEW_W, VIEW_H = 320, 180       -- the viewport, in screen pixels
local WORLD_W, WORLD_H = 960, 640     -- the world, in world units
local PAN_SPEED = 240                 -- world units per second
local FLAG = { x = 560, y = 280 }
local cam = { x = 480, y = 320, zoom = 1, angle = 0 }   -- angle in radians

local function worldToScreen(wx, wy)
  local dx, dy = wx - cam.x, wy - cam.y                 -- 1. relative to the camera
  local c, s = math.cos(cam.angle), math.sin(cam.angle)
  local rx, ry = dx * c + dy * s, -dx * s + dy * c      -- 2. rotate by -angle
  return rx * cam.zoom + VIEW_W / 2,                    -- 3. zoom
         ry * cam.zoom + VIEW_H / 2                     -- 4. centre of the viewport
end

local function screenToWorld(sx, sy)  -- the same steps, undone in reverse order
  local vx = (sx - VIEW_W / 2) / cam.zoom
  local vy = (sy - VIEW_H / 2) / cam.zoom
  local c, s = math.cos(cam.angle), math.sin(cam.angle)
  return vx * c - vy * s + cam.x, vx * s + vy * c + cam.y
end

function love.load()
  love.window.setMode(VIEW_W, VIEW_H)
end

function love.update(dt)
  local down = love.keyboard.isDown
  if down("left") then cam.x = cam.x - PAN_SPEED * dt end
  if down("right") then cam.x = cam.x + PAN_SPEED * dt end
  if down("up") then cam.y = cam.y - PAN_SPEED * dt end
  if down("down") then cam.y = cam.y + PAN_SPEED * dt end
  if down("q") then cam.angle = cam.angle - math.rad(90) * dt end
  if down("e") then cam.angle = cam.angle + math.rad(90) * dt end
  cam.x = math.max(0, math.min(WORLD_W, cam.x))
  cam.y = math.max(0, math.min(WORLD_H, cam.y))
end

function love.wheelmoved(x, y)
  if y > 0 then cam.zoom = math.min(3, cam.zoom * 1.1) end
  if y < 0 then cam.zoom = math.max(0.5, cam.zoom / 1.1) end
end

function love.draw()
  love.graphics.push()                                  -- world space from here
  love.graphics.translate(VIEW_W / 2, VIEW_H / 2)       -- 4.
  love.graphics.scale(cam.zoom)                         -- 3.
  love.graphics.rotate(-cam.angle)                      -- 2.
  love.graphics.translate(-cam.x, -cam.y)               -- 1.
  for i = 0, WORLD_W, 80 do love.graphics.line(i, 0, i, WORLD_H) end
  for j = 0, WORLD_H, 80 do love.graphics.line(0, j, WORLD_W, j) end
  love.graphics.circle("fill", FLAG.x, FLAG.y, 6)
  love.graphics.pop()                                   -- back to screen space

  local fx, fy = worldToScreen(FLAG.x, FLAG.y)          -- a label that never scales
  love.graphics.print("flag", fx + 8, fy - 8)
  local wx, wy = screenToWorld(love.mouse.getPosition())
  love.graphics.print("mouse in world: " .. math.floor(wx) .. ", " .. math.floor(wy), 4, 4)
end
# camera_demo.gd for Godot 4. Attach to a Node2D in a new scene and run.
# Arrow keys pan, Q and E rotate, the mouse wheel zooms, a left click prints both conversions.
# Set the window to 320 x 180 (Project Settings, Display > Window) to match the demo's numbers.
extends Node2D

const WORLD := Vector2(960, 640)
const FLAG := Vector2(560, 280)
const PAN_SPEED := 240.0
var cam := Camera2D.new()

func _ready() -> void:
    cam.position = Vector2(480, 320)
    cam.ignore_rotation = false   # otherwise Camera2D ignores its own rotation
    add_child(cam)
    cam.make_current()

func _process(delta: float) -> void:
    var move := Vector2(Input.get_axis("ui_left", "ui_right"), Input.get_axis("ui_up", "ui_down"))
    cam.position = (cam.position + move * PAN_SPEED * delta).clamp(Vector2.ZERO, WORLD)
    if Input.is_key_pressed(KEY_Q):
        cam.rotation -= deg_to_rad(90.0) * delta
    if Input.is_key_pressed(KEY_E):
        cam.rotation += deg_to_rad(90.0) * delta

func _unhandled_input(event: InputEvent) -> void:
    if event is InputEventMouseButton and event.pressed:
        if event.button_index == MOUSE_BUTTON_WHEEL_UP:
            cam.zoom = (cam.zoom * 1.1).clamp(Vector2(0.5, 0.5), Vector2(3, 3))
        elif event.button_index == MOUSE_BUTTON_WHEEL_DOWN:
            cam.zoom = (cam.zoom / 1.1).clamp(Vector2(0.5, 0.5), Vector2(3, 3))
        elif event.button_index == MOUSE_BUTTON_LEFT:
            var world := get_global_mouse_position()                     # screen to world
            var screen := get_viewport().get_canvas_transform() * FLAG     # world to screen
            print("clicked world ", world, ", flag on screen ", screen)

func _draw() -> void:   # drawn in world space, through the camera
    for i in range(0, 961, 80):
        draw_line(Vector2(i, 0), Vector2(i, WORLD.y), Color(1, 1, 1, 0.25))
    for j in range(0, 641, 80):
        draw_line(Vector2(0, j), Vector2(WORLD.x, j), Color(1, 1, 1, 0.25))
    draw_circle(FLAG, 6.0, Color.RED)
// CameraDemo.cs. Unity 2022 LTS or Unity 6. Add it to the Main Camera of a 2D scene,
// drag any sprite into Flag, then play. Distances are in Unity units, not pixels.
// Uses the Input Manager. If Active Input Handling (Player settings) is set to the
// Input System package only, switch it to Both.
using UnityEngine;

public class CameraDemo : MonoBehaviour
{
    public Transform flag;
    const float PanSpeed = 5f;      // units per second
    Camera cam;
    float sizeAtZoom1;

    void Start()
    {
        cam = GetComponent<Camera>();
        cam.orthographic = true;
        sizeAtZoom1 = cam.orthographicSize;   // half the visible height, in units
    }

    void Update()
    {
        var move = new Vector3(Input.GetAxisRaw("Horizontal"), Input.GetAxisRaw("Vertical"), 0f);
        transform.position += move * PanSpeed * Time.deltaTime;   // world y points up
        if (Input.GetKey(KeyCode.Q)) transform.Rotate(0f, 0f, 90f * Time.deltaTime);
        if (Input.GetKey(KeyCode.E)) transform.Rotate(0f, 0f, -90f * Time.deltaTime);

        float zoom = sizeAtZoom1 / cam.orthographicSize;
        if (Input.mouseScrollDelta.y > 0f) zoom = Mathf.Min(3f, zoom * 1.1f);
        if (Input.mouseScrollDelta.y < 0f) zoom = Mathf.Max(0.5f, zoom / 1.1f);
        cam.orthographicSize = sizeAtZoom1 / zoom;   // zooming in means a smaller size

        if (Input.GetMouseButtonDown(0))
        {
            Vector3 world = cam.ScreenToWorldPoint(Input.mousePosition);
            Vector3 screen = cam.WorldToScreenPoint(flag.position);
            Debug.Log($"clicked world {world}, flag on screen {screen}");
        }
    }
}

Unity's screen positions start at the bottom left, so the flag's screen y grows as it moves up. For an orthographic camera ScreenToWorldPoint gets x and y right whatever z you pass; with a perspective camera z must be the distance from the camera.

Pitfalls

  • Comparing a mouse position with an entity position. It works until the camera moves, which is why it often survives the first test. Convert the mouse to world space first.
  • Doing the steps in the wrong order. Rotating before subtracting the camera position spins the world around the world origin instead of around the camera. Zooming before rotating is harmless; moving the centre step anywhere but last is not.
  • Zoom pointing the other way. In Godot 4 a larger zoom is closer; Godot 3 was the opposite, so older tutorials will mislead you. In Unity a larger orthographicSize shows more of the world, so it is further away.
  • A Camera2D that will not rotate. In Godot 4, ignore_rotation is true by default, so setting the camera's rotation changes nothing until you turn it off.
  • HUD drawn in world space. A health bar drawn as a child of the world zooms and spins with it. Put the HUD on a CanvasLayer in Godot or a Screen Space canvas in Unity, and position it with world to screen.
  • Pixel art at odd zooms. At a zoom such as 1.5, some art pixels cover one screen pixel and some cover two, so sprites shimmer as the camera moves. Prefer whole-number zooms for pixel art.

In real games

Split-screen multiplayer is several cameras, each with its own viewport inside one window. Unity sets this with a camera's viewport rectangle; Godot uses a SubViewport per player. A minimap is the same idea: a second camera, zoomed far out, drawing into a small viewport in the corner.

Screen shake is a small random offset added to the camera position for a few frames, so the world moves while the HUD does not. Follow cameras with smoothing and dead zones are common enough that both engines help: Godot's Camera2D has built-in smoothing and limits, and Unity offers the Cinemachine package.

Takeaways

  • World space is where objects live; screen space is the pixels of a viewport. The camera maps one onto the other.
  • World to screen: subtract the camera position, rotate by minus the angle, multiply by zoom, add half the viewport.
  • Screen to world undoes the same steps in reverse order, and is how every mouse click finds its target.
  • The visible part of the world is the viewport size divided by the zoom.
  • Godot's Camera2D writes the viewport's canvas transform; Unity's orthographic Camera uses its Transform plus orthographicSize. Both give you functions for the two conversions.
