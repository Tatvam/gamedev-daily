Skeletal animation: bones, forward and inverse kinematics

How a bone hierarchy turns local rotations into world positions, how two-bone IK works backwards from a target, and how Godot and Unity skin and solve.

Your character's walk cycle looks fine on flat ground. On a slope, one foot floats in the air and the other sinks into the hill. The clip was authored for flat ground. The fix is not a new clip: let the engine bend the knee so the foot lands where the ground really is.

A skeleton is a transform hierarchy with a mesh on top

A bone is a transform: a position, rotation and scale relative to its parent bone. A skeleton is a tree of bones, usually rooted at the hips. It is the same parent-child hierarchy as a scene tree, so moving the upper arm carries the forearm and hand with it.

The bones are never drawn. The character's mesh, or its cut-out sprite pieces in 2D, follows them through skinning. Every vertex stores one or more bone weights, numbers that say how strongly each bone pulls it. A vertex near the elbow might be 60% upper arm and 40% forearm.

Forward kinematics: angles in, positions out

Forward kinematics (FK) means you set each bone's local rotation and the hierarchy works out where everything ends up. Animation clips work this way: they store local rotations per bone, per keyframe. Working out the result is a walk down the chain:

world = parentWorld + localAngle
joint = parentJoint + length * direction(world)

In 2D the angles simply add. In 3D the walk multiplies rotations as quaternions or matrices, but the idea is identical. FK is cheap and predictable.

Inverse kinematics: position in, angles out

Often you know where the hand or foot must be, not the angles that put it there. Inverse kinematics (IK) solves that backwards problem: given a target position, find the local rotations.

For two bones, such as upper arm and forearm or thigh and shin, there is an exact answer. The shoulder, the elbow and the target form a triangle. You know all three sides: the two bone lengths L1 and L2, and the distance d from shoulder to target. The law of cosines gives the angles.

With B and A known, the two local rotations are:

shoulder = angleToTarget - bend * A
elbow    = bend * (180° - B)

bend is 1 or -1. Every reachable target has two solutions, elbow up or elbow down, and you must pick one. Real rigs use a pole target (Unity calls it a hint), a point the knee or elbow should point towards.

Two cases need care. If the target is further than L1 + L2, no triangle exists: clamp d and the arm points straight at the target. If it is closer than |L1 - L2|, the arm folds as far as it can. Longer chains, like a tail or a tentacle, have no exact formula. Engines use iterative solvers such as CCD (cyclic coordinate descent, rotating one joint at a time towards the target) and FABRIK (dragging joint positions forwards and backwards along the chain).

What happens, in what order, and who owns the data

The skeleton owns the current local pose of every bone and its rest pose (Unity calls the stored version the bind pose), the pose the mesh was modelled in. The mesh owns the weights. Clips are read-only data. Gameplay owns the IK target, for example "the ground under the left foot", usually found with a raycast. IK must run after the animation writes its pose, or the animation overwrites it.

Godot and Unity

Godot 4, 2D: a Skeleton2D node holds Bone2D children. A Bone2D is a Node2D, so bones are ordinary scene nodes with a rest pose. A Polygon2D is skinned to the skeleton through its skeleton property and weights painted in its UV editor. For IK, Godot 4 has modification resources such as SkeletonModification2DTwoBoneIK, SkeletonModification2DCCDIK and SkeletonModification2DFABRIK, collected in a SkeletonModificationStack2D on the skeleton. As far as we know these are still marked experimental, so check your version's class reference.

Godot 4, 3D: a Skeleton3D stores its bones as indexed data inside one node, not as child nodes. You read and write them by index, for example find_bone() and set_bone_pose_rotation(), and a BoneAttachment3D lets a node, such as a sword, follow a bone. Godot 4.3 added SkeletonModifier3D, a base class for nodes that adjust the pose after animation. Which IK modifiers ship on top of it has changed between 4.x releases.

Unity (2022 LTS and Unity 6): bones are ordinary Transforms in the GameObject hierarchy. A SkinnedMeshRenderer lists them and the mesh stores the weights and bind poses. Humanoid rigs get built-in IK: enable IK Pass on the Animator layer, then call SetIKPosition and SetIKPositionWeight inside OnAnimatorIK. The Animation Rigging package adds constraints such as Two Bone IK Constraint for any rig. In 2D, the 2D Animation package adds the Sprite Skin component and an IK Manager 2D with Limb, CCD and FABRIK solvers.

The trade-off: Unity's bones-as-Transforms are easy to inspect and parent things to, but each is a full GameObject. Godot's Skeleton3D keeps bones as compact data, at the cost of addressing them by index.

Rule of thumb

Animate with FK, correct with IK. Clips give the motion its character; IK only fixes the few joints that must touch the world, such as feet on the ground and hands on a ledge or a weapon.

Try it

In FK mode, drag any bone or use the sliders: only that bone's local angle changes, but the world angle of everything below it changes too. Switch to IK and drag the target ring. The shoulder and elbow sliders now move by themselves, because IK writes the same local rotations FK reads. Drag the target outside the outer circle to see the arm straighten. Press Flip elbow for the other solution. Set Skin to rigid weights, bend the elbow hard, and watch the skin tear; smooth weights fix it. On the focused canvas, the arrow keys move the target in IK mode.

The code

The same arm as the demo in LÖVE 11: lengths 120, 100 and 40, a starting pose of 30, 40 and 10 degrees, and the same two-bone solve. Only the shoulder and elbow are solved; the hand keeps its FK rotation. The Godot and Unity tabs solve the same triangle on real nodes and let the engine compose the hierarchy.

-- main.lua for LÖVE 11. Tab switches FK and IK, F flips the elbow.
-- FK: Q/A turn the shoulder, W/S the elbow, E/D the hand. IK: the wrist follows the mouse.
local L1, L2, L3 = 120, 100, 40                  -- upper arm, forearm, hand
local shoulderX, shoulderY = 300, 300
local angles = { math.rad(30), math.rad(40), math.rad(10) }   -- local rotations
local bend = 1                                   -- 1 or -1: which way the elbow points
local mode = "fk"

local function clamp(v, lo, hi) return math.max(lo, math.min(hi, v)) end

-- Forward kinematics: each world angle is the parent's plus the local one.
local function forward()
  local lengths = { L1, L2, L3 }
  local x, y, world = shoulderX, shoulderY, 0
  local joints = { { x, y } }
  for i = 1, 3 do
    world = world + angles[i]
    x = x + lengths[i] * math.cos(world)
    y = y + lengths[i] * math.sin(world)
    joints[i + 1] = { x, y }
  end
  return joints
end

-- Inverse kinematics: the two local rotations that put the wrist on the target.
local function solveTwoBone(tx, ty)
  local dx, dy = tx - shoulderX, ty - shoulderY
  local d = math.sqrt(dx * dx + dy * dy)
  d = clamp(d, math.abs(L1 - L2) + 0.001, L1 + L2 - 0.001)   -- out of reach: clamp
  local B = math.acos(clamp((L1 * L1 + L2 * L2 - d * d) / (2 * L1 * L2), -1, 1))
  local A = math.acos(clamp((L1 * L1 + d * d - L2 * L2) / (2 * L1 * d), -1, 1))
  angles[1] = math.atan2(dy, dx) - bend * A
  angles[2] = bend * (math.pi - B)
  -- angles[3], the hand, is left alone: it stays FK
end

function love.update(dt)
  local turn = math.rad(90) * dt
  if love.keyboard.isDown("e") then angles[3] = angles[3] - turn end
  if love.keyboard.isDown("d") then angles[3] = angles[3] + turn end
  if mode == "ik" then
    solveTwoBone(love.mouse.getPosition())
  else
    if love.keyboard.isDown("q") then angles[1] = angles[1] - turn end
    if love.keyboard.isDown("a") then angles[1] = angles[1] + turn end
    if love.keyboard.isDown("w") then angles[2] = angles[2] - turn end
    if love.keyboard.isDown("s") then angles[2] = angles[2] + turn end
  end
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "tab" then
    if mode == "fk" then mode = "ik" else mode = "fk" end
  elseif key == "f" then
    bend = -bend
  end
end

function love.draw()
  local j = forward()
  for i = 1, 3 do
    love.graphics.line(j[i][1], j[i][2], j[i + 1][1], j[i + 1][2])
    love.graphics.circle("line", j[i][1], j[i][2], 6)
  end
  love.graphics.circle("line", shoulderX, shoulderY, L1 + L2)   -- the reach
  love.graphics.print(string.format("%s  shoulder %.0f  elbow %.0f  hand %.0f", mode,
    math.deg(angles[1]), math.deg(angles[2]), math.deg(angles[3])), 10, 10)
end
# two_bone_ik.gd for Godot 4. Scene tree:
#   Arm (Node2D, this script)
#     Upper (Node2D) at the shoulder
#       Lower (Node2D) at position (120, 0)
#         Hand (Node2D) at position (100, 0)
# Add a Sprite2D or Line2D under each bone to see it. Bones point along local +X.
# Bone2D nodes under a Skeleton2D work the same way, since Bone2D extends Node2D.
extends Node2D

@export var ik_enabled := true
@export var bend := 1.0   # 1 or -1; Enter (ui_accept) flips it

@onready var upper: Node2D = $Upper
@onready var lower: Node2D = $Upper/Lower
@onready var hand: Node2D = $Upper/Lower/Hand

func _process(_delta: float) -> void:
    if Input.is_action_just_pressed("ui_accept"):
        bend = -bend
    if not ik_enabled:
        return   # FK: the bones keep whatever rotations they have
    var l1 := lower.position.length()
    var l2 := hand.position.length()
    # target in Arm's space, measured from the shoulder
    var t := to_local(get_global_mouse_position()) - upper.position
    var d := clampf(t.length(), absf(l1 - l2) + 0.001, l1 + l2 - 0.001)
    var b := acos(clampf((l1 * l1 + l2 * l2 - d * d) / (2.0 * l1 * l2), -1.0, 1.0))
    var a := acos(clampf((l1 * l1 + d * d - l2 * l2) / (2.0 * l1 * d), -1.0, 1.0))
    upper.rotation = t.angle() - bend * a
    lower.rotation = bend * (PI - b)
    # Godot composes the hierarchy: hand.global_position is now on the target,
    # or as close as the arm can reach.
// TwoBoneIK2D.cs for Unity 2022 LTS or Unity 6. Hierarchy:
//   Arm (this script)
//     Upper        at the shoulder
//       Lower      local position (1.2, 0, 0)
//         Hand     local position (1.0, 0, 0)
//   Target         any GameObject; drag it in the Scene view while playing
// Give each bone a child sprite to see it. Bones point along local +X, scale 1.
using UnityEngine;

public class TwoBoneIK2D : MonoBehaviour
{
    public Transform upper, lower, hand, target;
    public bool ikEnabled = true;
    public float bend = 1f;   // 1 or -1: which way the elbow points

    // LateUpdate runs after the Animator has written this frame's pose,
    // so the IK corrects the animation instead of being overwritten by it.
    void LateUpdate()
    {
        if (!ikEnabled || target == null) return;
        float l1 = lower.localPosition.magnitude;
        float l2 = hand.localPosition.magnitude;
        // target in Arm's space, measured from the shoulder
        Vector2 t = transform.InverseTransformPoint(target.position) - upper.localPosition;
        float d = Mathf.Clamp(t.magnitude, Mathf.Abs(l1 - l2) + 0.001f, l1 + l2 - 0.001f);
        float b = Mathf.Acos(Mathf.Clamp((l1 * l1 + l2 * l2 - d * d) / (2f * l1 * l2), -1f, 1f));
        float a = Mathf.Acos(Mathf.Clamp((l1 * l1 + d * d - l2 * l2) / (2f * l1 * d), -1f, 1f));
        float shoulder = Mathf.Atan2(t.y, t.x) - bend * a;
        float elbow = bend * (Mathf.PI - b);
        upper.localRotation = Quaternion.Euler(0f, 0f, shoulder * Mathf.Rad2Deg);
        lower.localRotation = Quaternion.Euler(0f, 0f, elbow * Mathf.Rad2Deg);
    }
}

Unity's Y axis points up and Godot's points down, so the same bend value bends the elbow the opposite way on screen. Flip it if your elbow points the wrong way.

Pitfalls

  • IK that runs too early. If your solver runs before the animation system writes the pose, the clip overwrites it and nothing seems to happen, or the arm flickers. In Unity, solve in LateUpdate or OnAnimatorIK. In Godot, use the engine's modification or modifier system, or keep the bones you solve off the animation tracks.
  • The elbow pops to the other side. Near a fully straight arm, the two solutions meet, and a tiny change can flip a bone. Fix the bend with a pole target, and keep targets slightly inside full reach.
  • Mixing up local and world. Bone rotations are relative to the parent. Measuring the target in world space gives an arm that is right only while the character is unrotated. Convert the target into the chain's parent space first, as both engine tabs do.
  • Scaled bones. Non-uniform scale on a parent bone distorts the lengths the solver reads. Keep bone scale at 1 and scale the mesh or the root instead.
  • Rigid weights at joints. One bone per vertex makes the skin tear at the elbow, as in the demo. Blend weights across the joint; rigs add helper bones where even blended skin loses volume.
  • Performance. Skinning usually runs on the GPU, but every bone still costs a transform update per frame. Use fewer bones on distant characters.

In real games

Almost every animated 3D character in a modern game is a mesh skinned to a skeleton, and 2D games use the same idea through tools such as Esoteric Software's Spine or the engines' own 2D skeletons. Foot placement on uneven ground and hands gripping weapons or ledges are the classic uses of two-bone IK. Unreal Engine has a Two Bone IK node in its animation graphs, alongside the Godot and Unity features above.

Takeaways

  • A skeleton is a transform hierarchy; skinning makes the mesh follow it through per-vertex bone weights.
  • Forward kinematics turns local rotations into world positions by walking down the chain. Animation clips are FK data.
  • Inverse kinematics works backwards from a target. Two bones have an exact law-of-cosines solution with two mirror answers; longer chains use CCD or FABRIK.
  • IK writes ordinary local rotations, and it must run after the animation has posed the skeleton.
  • Godot 2D and Unity treat bones as scene objects; Godot's Skeleton3D stores them as indexed data in one node.
