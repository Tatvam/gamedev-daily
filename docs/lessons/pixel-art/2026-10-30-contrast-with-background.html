Contrast with the background: readable everywhere

Why a sprite that reads on one background vanishes on another, and how a dark outline with a light fill keeps an edge on light, dark and busy ground.

Your character looks great on the sunny beach of level one. In the cave of level two the player sees two eyes floating in the dark. In the forest of level three the coins you placed on a leafy wall are found only by players who already know where they are.

The sprite did not change. What was behind it did. Contrast belongs to the pair of sprite and background, and in a game that pair changes every time something moves.

Contrast happens at the edge

Contrast is how different two neighbouring colours are. For finding a sprite, what matters is contrast at its edge, where sprite pixels touch background pixels. Where those pairs differ strongly, the eye traces the silhouette. Where they are close, that stretch dissolves into the scene.

Value comes first put a character's main colour outside the greys of one background. In a game the same sprite crosses many backgrounds, usually of three kinds:

  • Light, such as sand, snow or sky.
  • Dark, such as caves, night or deep water.
  • Busy: many values mixed in small clusters, such as leaves or rubble, covering a wide band of greys at once.

No single colour sits outside all three. A light sprite merges with sand, a dark one with the cave, and a mid-grey one lands inside the busy band. Changing brightness only moves the problem to another level.

Give the sprite both ends

The usual fix is a dark outline around a fairly light fill. The sprite now carries both ends of the grey scale, and each background only has to disagree with one of them:

  • On light ground, the dark outline makes the edge.
  • On dark ground, the outline merges, but the light fill just inside it makes the edge instead.
  • On busy ground, the outline is darker than the darkest leaf, so the edge holds.

Measuring it: the edge score

The demo looks at every pair of a sprite pixel and the background pixel beside it. A pair is a clear edge when their greys differ by 45 or more; for an outline pixel, the fill pixels touching it from inside may make the edge instead. The edge score is the share of pairs that are clear.

The 45 is the rough guide from the value lesson, my own figure rather than a standard. Use the score to find the weak spot, then judge that spot by eye at 1x.

Saturation is the second channel

Saturation, how strong a colour is, also pulls the eye: a strong orange on a greyish background gets noticed even when the greys are close. Games usually keep backgrounds more muted than characters and pickups, so the most saturated things on screen are the ones you can touch.

But saturation draws no edge in greyscale, and it fails for players who see some colour pairs poorly. It helps on top of value contrast; it does not replace it.

Rule of thumb

Test a sprite on the lightest, the darkest and the busiest background in your game, not just the first level. If one value cannot clear all three, give the sprite a dark outline and a lighter fill, and keep the backgrounds a little less saturated than the things the player must track.

These are conventions, not laws. A soft style can skip outlines and keep its backgrounds in a narrow, calm band of values instead. What breaks is an outline-free sprite in the middle of a wide, busy background.

The code

This is the demo's edge score in Lua, as a checking tool: run it from a debug key where the player stands, or over a whole level in a test script, and list the low spots. The sprite is a list of strings where . is empty and 1 is outline. background(x, y) is your own function returning the level's colour at a pixel, or nil outside it.

local CLEAR = 45   -- grey steps that count as a clear edge
local DIRS = { {1, 0}, {-1, 0}, {0, 1}, {0, -1} }

local function luma(c)
  return 0.299 * c[1] + 0.587 * c[2] + 0.114 * c[3]
end

local function keyAt(sprite, x, y)   -- nil when empty or outside
  local row = sprite[y]
  if row == nil or x < 1 or x > #row then return nil end
  local key = row:sub(x, x)
  if key == "." then return nil end
  return key
end

-- The sprite's top-left pixel sits on level pixel (px + 1, py + 1).
local function edgeScore(sprite, palette, background, px, py)
  local clear, total = 0, 0
  for y = 1, #sprite do
    for x = 1, #sprite[y] do
      local key = keyAt(sprite, x, y)
      for _, d in ipairs(DIRS) do
        local back = background(px + x + d[1], py + y + d[2])
        if key and back and not keyAt(sprite, x + d[1], y + d[2]) then
          local b = luma(back)
          local best = math.abs(luma(palette[key]) - b)
          if key == "1" then   -- outline: the fill just inside may make the edge
            for oy = -1, 1 do
              for ox = -1, 1 do
                local inner = keyAt(sprite, x + ox, y + oy)
                if inner and inner ~= "1" then
                  best = math.max(best, math.abs(luma(palette[inner]) - b))
                end
              end
            end
          end
          total = total + 1
          if best >= CLEAR then clear = clear + 1 end
        end
      end
    end
  end
  if total == 0 then return 1 end
  return clear / total
end

The demo's After palette is { ["1"] = {27, 20, 41}, ["2"] = {179, 83, 36}, ["3"] = {219, 132, 55}, ["4"] = {255, 199, 102}, ["5"] = {27, 20, 41} }: outline, shade, fill, light and eyes. To score the version without an outline, the demo swaps every 1 in the sprite for 2, so the outer ring takes the shade colour and the outline rule no longer applies.

Pitfalls

  • Testing on the first level only. Keep a test sheet with a strip of every background in the game and drop each new sprite onto all of them.
  • Outlining everything. If scenery gets the same dark outline as characters, the outline stops meaning "you can touch this". A common convention is outlines on characters, enemies and pickups, and softer or none on scenery.
  • Brightening past the top. Raising a ramp pushes its lightest step into white, where it stops changing: in the demo, +20 and +30 give the same highlight. Leave room above the fill.
  • Fighting a busy background with the sprite. When a background spans most of the grey scale, it is often cheaper to narrow its value range so the sprite has room above or below it.
  • Tiny pickups. A 6 by 6 coin has few edge pairs and is seen for a moment while running past. Pickups often get the most saturated colour in the scene, a dark outline, and a blink so motion helps too.

In real games

The NES kept separate palettes for background tiles and for sprites: four of each, with three colours apiece plus a shared backdrop or transparency. That let artists pick character colours independently of the scenery behind them.

Outside games, the web accessibility guidelines (WCAG 2.1) ask for a contrast ratio of at least 3 to 1 between important interface graphics and the colours next to them: a different formula, but the same idea of measuring at the boundary.

To check the convention yourself, take a greyscale screenshot of a busy scene in any 2D game and compare the background's range of greys with the player character's.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Cut a strip of the lightest, darkest and busiest backgrounds in your project into one image. Paste your main character on each at 1x and look at it in greyscale.

Where the silhouette breaks, first move the fill brightness, then add a dark outline if one value cannot work everywhere, then calm the background if it is still busy. You are done when the whole silhouette can be traced on all three strips at 1x.

Takeaways

  • Contrast is measured at the edge, between sprite pixels and the background pixels touching them, and it changes as the sprite moves.
  • Light, dark and busy backgrounds cannot all be cleared by one value.
  • A dark outline with a lighter fill gives the sprite both ends of the grey scale, so every background disagrees with one of them.
  • Saturation draws the eye but makes no edge in greyscale. Use it on top of value contrast: muted backgrounds, stronger characters and pickups.
  • Test on your worst backgrounds, at 1x.
