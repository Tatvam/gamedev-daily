Seamless tiling: hide the edges of a repeating tile

Why a repeated tile shows a grid of seams, and how offsetting it by half moves the edges into the middle where you can paint them away.

You draw a lovely patch of stony ground, 16 by 16 pixels. On its own it looks finished. Then you fill a level with it and the floor turns into a checkerboard: every tile has a light corner, a dark corner and half a stone sliced off at the edge.

The tile was drawn as a picture. A tile that repeats has to be drawn as a loop.

The edges of a tile are its neighbours

Large areas such as ground, water or a brick wall usually repeat one tile many times. Where copies meet, the last column of the tile sits beside its own first column, and the bottom row sits above the top row.

A seam is a join between two copies that the eye can see: a straight line, or a grid of them, that belongs to the tile and not to the thing it shows. A seamless tile (also called a tileable tile) is one whose right edge carries on into its left edge and whose bottom carries on into its top, so its copies join without a visible border.

Three ways a tile grows seams

  • Cut-off shapes. A stone that runs off the right edge has to come back on the left edge, at the same height. If it does not, every copy shows half a stone with a flat side.
  • Lighting across the whole tile. You have learned to pick a light source and shade forms. Shading the tile as if it were one form, light at the top left and dark at the bottom right, makes each copy a little pillow. Light the stones inside the tile, but keep the ground under them level in value from edge to edge.
  • A border. An outline or a darker row along the edge draws the grid on purpose. That can be right for a deliberately blocky wall, but it is wrong for ground that should feel continuous.

The fix: move the edges to where you can see them

The trouble with edges is that the two pixels that touch in the game are 16 pixels apart in your editor. The classic trick is to offset the tile by half its size with wrap-around: slide every pixel 8 to the right and 8 down, and whatever falls off one side comes back on the other.

After the offset the four quarters have swapped corners. The old edges now meet in a cross through the middle of the tile, side by side, exactly as they will meet in the game. The new edges are the old centre lines, which were already continuous because you drew them as one picture.

The full routine:

  1. Look at the tile repeated at least 3 by 3. A single tile hides every seam.
  2. Offset by half. Paint over the cross: let stones and clusters run across it, and even out any jump in brightness.
  3. Offset back (or not; a seamless tile works from any starting point). Look at the 3 by 3 view again.

Rule of thumb

Judge a tile only when it is surrounded by its own copies. If you can point at where one copy ends, it is not seamless yet.

Try it

The editor holds one 16 by 16 ground tile. Beside it (below it on a phone) the tile is repeated 3 by 3 at a large size, and again at its real size.

  • Start with the before tile. Find the grid in the 3 by 3 view, then turn on Show tile borders to check where you thought the edges were.
  • Turn on Offset by half. The editor now shows the seams as a cross in the middle. Red ticks mark pairs of touching pixels that are two or more shades apart.
  • Paint over the cross with the five shades until the 3 by 3 view stops showing a grid. Then press Load after to compare with the fixed version of the same tile.

The readouts compare the seams with the tile's centre lines: texture always has some sharp steps, so a seam is fine when it has about as many as the middle. With a keyboard, focus the canvas, move with the arrow keys, press Space to paint and 1 to 5 to pick a shade.

The code

Two pieces carry over into a game. First, the offset and the seam check work on a tile stored as rows of shade numbers, 1 for the darkest and 5 for the lightest, just like the demo. Second, LÖVE can fill a large area with one seamless tile by setting the image to repeat and drawing it through a Quad bigger than the image.

-- tile[y][x] holds a shade from 1 (dark) to 5 (light)
local function offset(tile, dx, dy)
  local n = #tile
  local out = {}
  for y = 1, n do
    out[y] = {}
    for x = 1, n do
      local sx = (x - 1 - dx) % n + 1   -- wrap around: what leaves one side
      local sy = (y - 1 - dy) % n + 1   -- comes back on the other
      out[y][x] = tile[sy][sx]
    end
  end
  return out
end

-- count touching pairs that are 2 or more shades apart across the seams
local function harshPairs(tile)
  local n = #tile
  local count = 0
  for i = 1, n do
    if math.abs(tile[i][n] - tile[i][1]) >= 2 then count = count + 1 end
    if math.abs(tile[n][i] - tile[1][i]) >= 2 then count = count + 1 end
  end
  return count
end

-- in the game: fill the screen with one seamless 16 x 16 image
local ground, quad

function love.load()
  ground = love.graphics.newImage("ground.png")
  ground:setFilter("nearest", "nearest")
  ground:setWrap("repeat", "repeat")
  quad = love.graphics.newQuad(0, 0, 320, 180, ground:getWidth(), ground:getHeight())
end

function love.draw()
  love.graphics.scale(4)
  love.graphics.draw(ground, quad, 0, 0)
end

offset(tile, 8, 8) gives the demo's offset view. Lua's % returns 0 to n - 1 even for negative numbers, so the wrap works both ways. The 320 by 180 quad is filled with copies of the 16 by 16 image.

Pitfalls

  • Fixing the edges but keeping the gradient. Stones can wrap perfectly and the tile still shows a grid, because one corner is lighter than the other. Even out the ground itself.
  • Painting a line along the cross. A straight run of one shade, or a neat row of stones, along the old edge is a new seam drawn by hand. Let shapes cross the joins at different places.
  • Seamless but obviously repeating. One standout feature, such as a single bright pebble, still forms a visible pattern. That is covered next.
  • Seams that are not in the art. Thin lines in the game but not in your preview usually come from smooth filtering or a camera at a fractional pixel position, not from the tile.

In real games

Older 2D games usually filled ground, water and walls by repeating one tile or a small group of tiles, which only looks continuous when the edges match. 3D games repeat floor and wall textures the same way.

The offset trick is common enough that image editors ship it: Photoshop has an Offset filter and GIMP an Offset tool, both with a wrap-around option. Aseprite has a tiled mode that draws copies around the canvas while you paint. In engines, Godot's TextureRect has a tile stretch mode and Unity's SpriteRenderer a tiled draw mode.

Your turn (15 minutes)

  1. Draw a 16 by 16 ground tile with four or five shades: grass, sand, stone or dirt. Draw it the way you normally would, without thinking about the edges.
  2. Make a 48 by 48 test image and paste the tile into it 3 by 3 (or switch on your editor's tiled mode). Note every place where you can see a join.
  3. Offset the tile by 8 pixels in both directions with wrap-around. If your editor has no offset command, cut the four quarters and swap them diagonally.
  4. Paint over the cross, then update the 3 by 3 test image.

You are done when someone who has not seen the tile cannot point at where one copy ends in the 3 by 3 view. For a bonus, save it as ground.png and run the LÖVE code above to fill a whole screen.

Takeaways

  • A repeated tile's right edge touches its own left edge, and its bottom touches its own top.
  • Seams usually come from shapes cut off at the edge, from lighting the whole tile like one object, or from a border.
  • Offsetting by half with wrap-around moves the joins into a cross in the middle, where you can paint across them.
  • Always judge a tile surrounded by its own copies, at least 3 by 3.
