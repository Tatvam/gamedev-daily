Side view and three-quarter view: where the camera stands

How the camera angle decides which faces of walls, floors and objects you draw, and why three-quarter view stacks every top above its front.

You draw a nice wooden table, drop it into your RPG room, and it looks like a cardboard cut-out leaning against the floor. Or you draw a rug for your platformer and it hangs in the air like a painting.

Neither drawing is bad; each was drawn for a different camera. Decide where the camera stands before you draw a wall, because that decides which faces exist.

Three directions, two screen axes

A room has three directions: across (left to right), depth (from the back wall towards you) and height. The screen only has two. Across always stays across. The question is what "up the screen" means, and each answer is a view convention: an agreement, kept for the whole game, about which directions survive.

  • Side view. Up the screen means higher. Depth is thrown away. You see the front of everything and nothing else.
  • Straight top-down. Up the screen means further back. Height is thrown away. You see the top of everything and nothing else.
  • Three-quarter view. Up the screen means further back or higher. One pixel deeper and one pixel taller both move a point one pixel up. You see tops and fronts, stacked.

Notice that the front face is the same drawing in side view and in three-quarter view. Three-quarter view only adds a lid on top. Mathematically this is a kind of oblique projection: two faces kept at full size and squeezed into one picture, with nothing shrinking in the distance.

The three-quarter rules of thumb

Three-quarter view is common in RPGs and adventure games because it shows the floor where the player walks and still gives objects a readable front. It comes with a few habits:

  • Tops are lighter than fronts. With the light from above, as in the light source lesson, every lid and wall top is the light colour of its ramp and every front is a step darker. That contrast is what tells the eye "this is a top".
  • The near wall goes. A full-height wall at the bottom of the room would hide as many rows of floor as it is tall. Games usually cut it down to a ledge, fade it out, or skip it.
  • Characters cheat. The floor is seen from above, but people are drawn almost straight from the front, standing up. Seen truly from above they would be a head and two shoulders.
  • A position means the feet. Since higher and deeper both move up the screen, a sprite's place on the floor is where its feet touch it. Anything whose feet are further down the screen is nearer the camera and is drawn later.

A convention, not a law

Many games draw tops shorter than their true depth, for example half as tall, so rooms do not look like they are tipping forward. Any ratio works. What breaks the picture is mixing: an object drawn for one camera placed in a room drawn for another always looks pasted on.

  • Turn on Mark top and front faces and compare the three views.
  • Drag the hero, or focus the demo and use the arrow keys. In side view, up and down move the hero in depth, but only the overlap with the table changes.
  • In straight top-down, the floor reads well but the table and chest are just rectangles.

The code

The whole difference between the two views is one line in project; the drawing order is shared. Tab switches views, up and down move the hero in depth. The numbers match the demo.

local SCALE = 4
local W, D, H = 56, 32, 16      -- room width, depth and wall height in art pixels
local OX, OY = 8, 24            -- screen position of the back-left corner of the floor
local view = "side"             -- or "threequarter"

local COLORS = {
  wall = { 0.37, 0.33, 0.46 }, floor = { 0.77, 0.70, 0.57 }, hero = { 0.25, 0.43, 0.60 },
  top = { 0.79, 0.60, 0.37 },     -- lit tops
  front = { 0.42, 0.29, 0.21 },   -- shaded fronts
}

local hero = { x = 30, y = 24, d = 0 }        -- x, y is the point between the feet
local things = {
  { x = 36, y = 0,  w = 12, d = 4, h = 14 },   -- shelf
  { x = 8,  y = 12, w = 16, d = 8, h = 7 },    -- table
  { x = 38, y = 20, w = 10, d = 6, h = 6 },    -- chest
  hero,
}

-- x across, y depth (0 at the back wall), z height
local function project(x, y, z)
  if view == "threequarter" then
    return OX + x, OY + y - z     -- further back or higher: both go up the screen
  end
  return OX + x, OY - z           -- side view: depth is thrown away
end

local function face(kind, x, y, w, h)
  love.graphics.setColor(COLORS[kind])
  love.graphics.rectangle("fill", x, y, w, h)
end

local function drawBox(b)
  local sx, sy = project(b.x, b.y, b.h)     -- back-left corner of the lid
  if view == "threequarter" then
    face("top", sx, sy, b.w, b.d)           -- the lid is b.d pixels tall
    sy = sy + b.d
  end
  face("front", sx, sy, b.w, b.h)           -- the same front in both views
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "tab" then view = (view == "side") and "threequarter" or "side" end
  if key == "up" then hero.y = hero.y - 2 end
  if key == "down" then hero.y = hero.y + 2 end
end

function love.draw()
  love.graphics.scale(SCALE)
  local x, y = project(0, 0, H)
  face("wall", x, y, W, H)                  -- the back wall does not move
  x, y = project(0, 0, 0)
  face("floor", x, y, W, view == "threequarter" and D or 6)
  table.sort(things, function(a, b) return a.y + a.d < b.y + b.d end)
  for _, t in ipairs(things) do             -- far to near, by front edge
    if t == hero then
      local hx, hy = project(hero.x, hero.y, 0)
      face("hero", hx - 4, hy - 12, 8, 12)  -- feet on the floor
    else
      drawBox(t)
    end
  end
end

To stay short, this uses plain rectangles and lets the hero walk through furniture. In a real game, face draws the matching top or front image from your sprite sheet.

Pitfalls

  • Mixing cameras in one tile set. A side-view barrel in a three-quarter room has no lid, so it looks like a cut-out. Check every new object against a finished one.
  • Lids that are too deep. A top drawn deeper than it is wide looks tipped towards the camera. Draw tops shorter and keep that ratio everywhere.
  • Tops and fronts in the same colour. Without a value step, the two faces merge into one flat shape and the volume is gone.
  • Sorting by the top of the sprite. Sort by where things touch the floor, or tall things jump in front of short things.
  • Forgetting that side view hides the floor. Rugs, puddles and paths vanish. If they matter for play, move them onto the back wall or the ground strip.

In real games

Side view is the camera of most platformers: Super Mario Bros., Celeste and Terraria all show the world as a slice, with the ground as a band seen edge on. Terraria makes the backdrop explicit: players place background walls that sit behind the layer they walk on.

The Legend of Zelda: A Link to the Past and Stardew Valley use three-quarter view. Indoors, the back wall of a room shows its face, the side walls show as strips of wall top, and characters are drawn nearly from the front. The Game Boy Pokémon games draw their houses the same way: the roof seen from above, the front wall with its door below it.

Your turn (15 minutes)

  1. Pick three colours from one ramp in your palette.
  2. Draw a crate in side view: a front face 12 pixels wide and 10 tall, outlined, with planks in the middle colour.
  3. Copy it. On the copy, add a lid 6 pixels tall above the front, in the lightest colour, sharing the front's left and right outline.
  4. Place each crate on a strip of floor that matches its view: a ground line for the first, a patch of floor tiles for the second. Check both at 1x.

You are done when the front of the three-quarter crate matches the side-view crate pixel for pixel, and the lid reads as a top at real size.

Takeaways

  • A view convention decides what "up the screen" means: higher, further back, or both.
  • Side view shows fronts only; the floor becomes a strip and the back wall a backdrop.
  • Three-quarter view stacks each top above its front, so the floor and the objects both read.
  • In three-quarter rooms, the back wall shows its face, side walls show their tops, and the near wall is usually cut away.
  • Pick one convention and one top-to-front ratio, and use them for every tile and sprite in the game.
