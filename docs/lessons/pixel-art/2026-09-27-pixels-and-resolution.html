Pixels and resolution: every pixel is a decision

How the size of a sprite limits the detail it can hold, and why small sprites must be placed pixel by pixel instead of shrunk.

You draw a character you are happy with, shrink it to 16 by 16 pixels for your game, and the face is gone. The eyes have become white smudges, the mouth has vanished, and the little leaf on its head floats in the air because the stem that held it disappeared.

The drawing was fine. The canvas simply had no room for all of it, and nobody decided what to keep. This lesson is about making that decision yourself.

A pixel holds exactly one colour

In pixel art, a pixel is the smallest square of the image, and it holds exactly one colour. It cannot be half eye and half skin. The resolution of a sprite is how many pixels wide and tall it is, written as width by height, such as 16 × 16.

Resolution is a budget. Every feature you want the player to see has to be paid for in pixels, and the budget grows faster than you might expect:

Sprite sizePixels to fillEnough room for
8 × 864a silhouette and one or two marks
16 × 16256a readable face and simple shading
32 × 321,024clothing, hands, clear expressions
64 × 644,096texture, fine shading, small props

Doubling the width multiplies the number of pixels by four. That means four times the room for detail, but also four times the pixels to place, in every frame of every animation.

What happens when a drawing is shrunk

When a smooth drawing is turned into pixels, each pixel has to pick one colour. The simplest rule, and the one the demos on this page use, is point sampling: look at the exact centre of each pixel and take whatever colour the drawing has there.

In code, the rule is one loop. A shape drawn later wins, just like painting over something:

-- The colour of pixel (x, y) on a sprite that is `size` pixels wide.
-- x and y count from 1, so the centre of pixel 1 is at 0.5.
local function pixelColour(x, y, size)
  local u = (x - 0.5) / size
  local v = (y - 0.5) / size
  local colour = "."                -- "." means transparent
  for _, shape in ipairs(SHAPES) do
    if inside(shape, u, v) then colour = shape.colour end
  end
  return colour
end

Many image editors average the colours under each pixel instead. Thin lines then survive as faint smears, but new in-between colours appear that are not in your palette. Neither rule knows what matters in your drawing.

The detail limit

Two things follow from "one colour per pixel".

Thin features are down to luck. A round blob about one and a half pixels across always covers at least one pixel centre, wherever it lands. Anything thinner than a pixel may land on a centre or fall between two, so as the size changes it pops in and out. In the demo below, the stem on the creature's head is about one thirty-third of the sprite wide, so it only appears on some sizes above 33 pixels.

Fixed-width lines eat small sprites. An outline is usually one pixel wide whatever the sprite size. On a 64 pixel sprite that is a thin border. On the demo's 8 by 8 version, 14 of the 22 filled pixels are outline, leaving 8 for everything else.

Why every pixel is a decision

At high resolutions you can think in shapes and let the pixels follow. At low resolutions each pixel becomes a choice between features: this square can be part of the eye or part of the cheek, not both.

Pixel artists usually solve this with exaggeration: they give the features the player must read more pixels than their real proportions would allow, and take those pixels from parts nobody will miss. A 16 pixel character's eyes are often far too big for its head, and that is why it reads as a face at all.

Rule of thumb

Before placing a pixel, name the two or three features the player has to recognise. Give those pixels first, even if it breaks the proportions. Spend whatever is left on shading and decoration.

The code

In a game you usually store a small sprite exactly as you decided it: a grid of palette entries. Here the hand-placed 16 by 16 creature from the demo is a list of strings, one letter per pixel, and each letter names a colour in the palette. The function draws it twice: zoomed to inspect, and at real size to judge.

-- One letter per colour. LÖVE colours use 0 to 1 per channel.
local PALETTE = {
  K = {0.14, 0.13, 0.23},  -- ink
  s = {0.18, 0.49, 0.33},  -- shade
  g = {0.35, 0.72, 0.42},  -- body
  L = {0.71, 0.89, 0.54},  -- light
  w = {0.96, 0.95, 0.91},  -- white
  m = {0.78, 0.31, 0.35},  -- mouth
}

-- The hand-placed sprite. "." is transparent.
local SPRITE = {
  "................",
  "........KKK.....",
  ".......KLLLK....",
  ".......KKKK.....",
  ".......K........",
  ".....KKKKKK.....",
  "....KggggggK....",
  "...KgLLgggggK...",
  "..KgLgggggggsK..",
  "..KggwKggwKgsK..",
  "..KggwKggwKgsK..",
  "..KggggmmgggsK..",
  "...KggggggssK...",
  "....KKssssKK....",
  "......KKKK......",
  "................",
}

local SCALE = 12  -- zoom, always a whole number

local function drawSprite(sprite, left, top, scale)
  for y = 1, #sprite do
    local row = sprite[y]
    for x = 1, #row do
      local c = row:sub(x, x)
      if c ~= "." then
        love.graphics.setColor(PALETTE[c])
        love.graphics.rectangle("fill", left + (x - 1) * scale, top + (y - 1) * scale, scale, scale)
      end
    end
  end
end

function love.draw()
  love.graphics.clear(0.85, 0.87, 0.89)
  drawSprite(SPRITE, 20, 20, SCALE)                   -- zoomed, to inspect
  drawSprite(SPRITE, 20 + 16 * SCALE + 20, 20, 1)     -- real size, to judge
  love.graphics.setColor(1, 1, 1)                     -- reset for anything drawn later
end

Each art pixel becomes one filled rectangle of scale by scale screen pixels. Because SCALE is a whole number, every art pixel ends up exactly the same size. Real games usually load the sprite as an image instead of drawing rectangles; how to enlarge an image without blurring it is the subject of a later lesson.

Pitfalls

  • Drawing big and shrinking. Whether the editor samples centres or averages colours, a rule is choosing your pixels, not you. You get lost features or muddy extra colours. A shrunk copy is fine as a sketch to redraw over.
  • Judging only at zoom. Pixel art is seen at its real size, or at a small whole-number multiple of it. A detail that looks clear at 12 times can be an unreadable speck in the game. Keep a real-size preview open while you work; most pixel editors have one.
  • More detail than the size can carry. Single pixels that do not join into a readable shape look like noise. If a feature needs one pixel to exist, ask whether it is one of the features that matter. If not, leave it out.
  • Mixing pixel sizes. The usual convention is that every art pixel on screen is the same size. A detailed sprite drawn at twice the resolution of the tiles around it looks like it comes from another game. There are games that break this on purpose, but it should be a choice.

In real games

Old hardware set the budget for artists. The NES drew its moving objects from hardware sprites of 8 by 8 or 8 by 16 pixels, so a character was usually assembled from several of them. The original Game Boy screen was 160 by 144 pixels in total, which is why its characters are so small and their faces so simple.

Modern pixel art games have no such hardware limit, so they choose one. Many draw the whole game to a small internal image, such as 320 by 180 pixels, and then scale it up to fill the screen. 320 by 180 multiplied by 6 is exactly 1920 by 1080, so every art pixel stays the same size on a full HD display.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Take a character you have drawn at 32 by 32 or larger, or sketch a quick one. Make a 16 by 16 version in two ways. First, resize it with your editor's image scaling and save the result. Second, start from an empty 16 by 16 canvas, write down the three features that must survive, place those first, and then fill in the rest.

Put both versions side by side at real size. You are done when someone who has not seen the original can name all three features in your hand-placed version.

Takeaways

  • A pixel holds exactly one colour, so resolution is a fixed budget for detail. Doubling the width gives four times the pixels to fill.
  • A feature thinner than about one pixel survives shrinking only by luck, and fixed-width outlines take a bigger share of smaller sprites.
  • Automatic shrinking lets a rule decide what to keep. At small sizes, place the pixels yourself.
  • Exaggerate the features the player has to read and cut the rest.
  • Always judge pixel art at its real size.
