Shiny materials: making a ball read as metal or glass

How high contrast, a dark reflected horizon and a tiny sharp highlight turn the same grey ball from matte clay into polished metal or glass.

You draw a sword or a coin, shade it carefully with a grey ramp, and it comes out looking like stone. Nothing in the pixels says "polished".

The shading is not wrong. It is shading for the wrong material. Shiny things follow different rules, and you can switch between the rules with the same palette.

Matte surfaces show the light, shiny surfaces show the world

A matte surface, such as clay, chalk or cloth, scatters the light that hits it in every direction. How bright a spot looks depends almost only on how directly it faces the light. That rule gives one smooth hill of values, mostly mid-tones.

A shiny surface, such as polished metal or glass, works more like a mirror. Each spot shows you whatever lies in the direction it reflects towards. The top of a chrome ball faces the sky, so it shows the sky. The middle faces the horizon. The bottom faces the floor. The light source itself shows up as one small, very bright spot.

So a polished sphere is not shaded by the light so much as painted with a squashed picture of its surroundings. Three changes follow from that, and together they are what makes the eye say "metal":

  1. More contrast. The darkest and lightest steps of the ramp sit right next to each other. Matte shading mostly uses the middle steps; shiny shading uses the ends.
  2. Sharp edges between bands. The reflected sky and the reflected ground meet at a hard line, usually with a very dark band where the horizon and the dark ground near it are mirrored. That band bends with the form.
  3. A small, hard highlight. The shinier the surface, the smaller and brighter the highlight, and the more abrupt its edge. On clay it is a wide soft area or nothing; on chrome it is two or three pure white pixels.

Remapping the shade bands

You do not need a new palette for metal. Take the same six-step grey ramp you would use for clay and change which band goes where: push the top towards the light steps, put the darkest step in a thin band just below the middle, bring the bottom back up to a mid-light step for the reflected floor, and save the lightest step for a tiny highlight. Matte clay usually never touches the lightest or darkest step; chrome uses both, side by side.

Plastic and satin metal sit in between. Shininess is a dial, not a switch.

Glass is the same idea, plus seeing through

Glass reflects like metal, so it keeps the hard highlight. But most of what you see is the scene behind it, bent by the curved surface. A solid glass ball acts like a lens and shows distant things upside down, so the ground appears in its top half and the sky in its bottom half. Pixel artists usually add a dark rim, because glass reflects more at grazing angles and the edge is otherwise lost, and often a small light spot on the shadow side where light passing through the ball is gathered. Treat that spot as a convention: it reads as glass even when it is not physically exact.

Rule of thumb

If it should look polished, put your darkest and lightest values next to each other, make the band edges hard, and shrink the highlight. If it should look soft, keep the values close together and the highlight broad.

The code

This LÖVE program draws the metal ball from the demo with the same numbers: a ball of radius 11 centred at (16, 16.5), light from the top left, and a shininess from 0 to 1 that blends the matte value with the reflected one. Press up and down to change it.

local ZOOM, CX, CY, R = 8, 16, 16.5, 11
local RAMP = { {28,27,38}, {63,61,76}, {106,104,119}, {154,152,163}, {203,202,208}, {251,251,246} }
local L = { -0.501, -0.602, 0.622 }   -- light from top left and in front, unit length
local shininess = 0.7

local function reflected(ny, nz)      -- what the mirror sees: sky, dark horizon, ground
  local u = ny + 0.3 * (1 - nz)       -- bend the horizon band around the form
  if u < 0.12 then return 0.55 + 0.35 * (0.12 - u) / 1.12 end
  if u < 0.32 then return 0.05 end
  return 0.66 - 0.3 * (u - 0.32) / 0.68
end

local function shadeIndex(nx, ny, nz, s)
  local d = math.max(0, nx * L[1] + ny * L[2] + nz * L[3])
  local matte = 0.2 + 0.68 * d
  local shiny = reflected(ny, nz) + 0.2 * (d - 0.5)
  local v = (1 - s) * matte + s * shiny
  v = 0.5 + (v - 0.5) * (1 + 0.5 * s)  -- shinier means more contrast
  local i = math.max(1, math.min(5, math.floor(v * 5) + 1))
  local rv = 2 * d * nz - L[3]          -- how straight the light bounces at the eye
  if s >= 0.15 and rv > 0.93 + 0.06 * s then i = 6 end   -- smaller highlight when shinier
  return i
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "up" then shininess = math.min(shininess + 0.05, 1) end
  if key == "down" then shininess = math.max(shininess - 0.05, 0) end
end

function love.draw()
  for y = 0, 31 do
    for x = 0, 31 do
      local nx, ny = (x + 0.5 - CX) / R, (y + 0.5 - CY) / R
      local q = nx * nx + ny * ny
      if q <= 1 then
        local c = RAMP[shadeIndex(nx, ny, math.sqrt(1 - q), shininess)]
        love.graphics.setColor(c[1] / 255, c[2] / 255, c[3] / 255)
        love.graphics.rectangle("fill", x * ZOOM, y * ZOOM, ZOOM, ZOOM)
      end
    end
  end
end

The highlighted lines are the whole idea. The first blends "how much does this spot face the light" with "what does this spot reflect" and then stretches the values apart. The second shrinks the white highlight as shininess grows. The numbers 0.12, 0.32, 0.3 and 0.93 are my choices for this ball, not standard values.

Pitfalls

  • Adding a highlight to matte shading. A white dot on a smooth mid-tone ball reads as a wet clay ball, not metal. The contrast of the whole surface has to change, not just one spot.
  • Too many steps. Polish comes from jumps. A ten-step smooth gradient from black to white looks like satin or plastic. Chrome usually uses fewer, harder bands.
  • Noise instead of reflections. Random light and dark pixels do not read as shine. Each band should follow the curve of the form.
  • A highlight on the wrong side. The highlight must agree with your light source. On a polished object it is the most visible pixel, so a mismatch is obvious.
  • Every metal object as chrome. Full mirror contrast is loud and steals attention. Dull iron and brass sit lower on the dial.

In real games

3D games fake shiny reflections with a technique called environment mapping: they store a picture of the surroundings, often as a cube map, and look up the reflected direction in it. The hand-drawn sky, dark band and floor on a pixel art sword are the same idea, done by eye.

Visual effects teams photograph a mirrored ball on set to record the light around a scene, because a polished sphere shows nearly all of its surroundings at once.

For pixel art, the best evidence is in the games you already play: look closely at swords, armour, coins and gems, and you will usually find a near-white pixel or two right next to one of the darkest colours in the sprite.

Your turn (15 minutes)

  1. Draw a 16 pixel wide ball or a short sword blade, shaded as matte with four grey steps and no highlight.
  2. Duplicate it. On the copy, add a darker and a lighter step to the ramp.
  3. Put a one or two pixel thick dark band just below the middle, following the curve. Lighten everything above it, and make the area below it a mid-light step.
  4. Add a highlight of two to four pixels in the lightest step, on the side facing your light.
  5. Put both at 1x next to each other.

You are done when someone who has not seen your process can point to the metal one without being told.

Takeaways

  • Matte surfaces are shaded by how they face the light. Shiny surfaces show their surroundings.
  • Polish reads from contrast: the darkest and lightest steps placed side by side.
  • The shinier the surface, the harder the band edges and the smaller the highlight.
  • Shininess is a remap of the same ramp, not a new palette.
  • Glass shows the scene behind it, often upside down, held together by a rim and a hard highlight.
