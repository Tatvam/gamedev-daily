Colour temperature and mood: one scene, four times of day

How tinting a whole palette towards one light colour moves the same scene from dawn to night, and why changing only the sky looks pasted on.

You want your village level to happen at night, so you paint the sky dark blue. The result looks wrong: the grass is still bright green and the walls are still sunny cream, as if someone cut the daytime village out and pasted it in front of a night photo.

Night is not a sky colour. It is a light that touches every pixel on screen. This lesson shows how to move a whole palette with it.

Warm, cool, and the light that falls on everything

Colour temperature is how warm or cool a colour feels. Reds, oranges and yellows are called warm, because we meet them in sunlight, fire and lamps. Blues, cyans and blue-violets are called cool, because we meet them in shade, water, ice and the night sky. Greens and purples sit in between and lean either way.

These are associations, not physics, but games, films and paintings lean on them all the time:

  • A mostly warm palette usually reads as sunny, cosy, late in the day, or dangerous when it gets very red.
  • A mostly cool palette usually reads as calm, cold, lonely or night-time.

The word that matters is mostly. Mood comes from the whole palette, not from one colour. A blue sky above a field at noon does not feel cold, because the field, the walls and the roofs are lit by warm sunlight.

The sky is the light

The sky is a background, but it also tells the player what light is shining. When the sky says "dusk" and the ground is lit like noon, the eye reads that as a mistake: the pasted look from the hook. So when the time of day changes, every colour moves towards the colour of the light:

  1. Pick a light colour for the mood: almost white for midday, orange-pink for dusk, a dim grey-blue for night.
  2. Tint every colour by it. Multiply each red, green and blue value by the light's value divided by 255. Under orange light, blue drops the most, so everything leans warm. Under dim blue light, red and green drop and everything gets darker and cooler.
  3. Mute it if the mood needs it. At night we see colour poorly: in low light the eye's colour-sensing cells give way to cells that see no colour, so a night palette is usually less saturated as well as darker. Mix each tinted colour part of the way towards its own grey.

Step 2 is what a multiply layer filled with the light colour does in most image editors. Step 3 is a saturation adjustment. Then tidy the results into a proper palette.

Remap by index, not by redrawing

If your art stores palette indexes (numbers that say "colour 3" instead of an actual colour), you never redraw the scene. You build one palette per time of day and look the same indexes up in a different table.

Keep one thing warm

Look at the window in the night row. A lamp makes its own light, so it does not take the night's tint. One warm spot in a cool scene is the strongest point of contrast on screen, because it is the only thing that disagrees in temperature. That is why a lit window, a campfire or a torch pulls the eye so hard at night.

Rule of thumb

Decide the mood, pick one light colour for it, and move every colour towards that light, not just the sky. Then keep one or two things that make their own light outside the tint, so the eye has somewhere to go.

These are conventions. Moonlight is reflected sunlight and is not really blue; we draw night blue partly because of how our eyes see in the dark and partly because film and painting taught us to. A stylised game can make night purple or green and still read as night, as long as the whole palette moves together and gets darker. What breaks is a scene where the sky and the ground disagree about the light.

Try it

Set Remap to Sky only (before) and drag Time of day to dusk, then night. The sky changes but the village stays at noon, and it looks cut out. Now switch to Whole palette (after) and drag again: the same indexes, looked up in a tinted palette, make the scene belong to its sky.

At night, turn Window stays warm off and on. Watch the readouts too: the count of warm land colours falls from 10 at dusk to 3 at night, and only one of those three is bright, the window. With the window off, the land's values span about 60 steps at night against about 140 at midday, which is why night scenes need extra care with contrast.

The code

This is the demo's remap in Lua for LÖVE, cut down to five colours. The map holds indexes; every frame the palette is rebuilt for the current time, and the map is drawn through it. t runs from 0 (dawn) through 100 (midday) and 200 (dusk) to 300 (night), the same scale as the demo's slider.

-- Land colours as drawn at midday. The map stores only their indexes.
local base = {
  {70, 150, 60},    -- 1 grass
  {220, 200, 160},  -- 2 wall
  {170, 60, 50},    -- 3 roof
  {70, 130, 180},   -- 4 water
  {120, 150, 170},  -- 5 window
}
local WINDOW, GLOW = 5, {255, 205, 100}

-- Dawn, midday, dusk, night: a light colour and a saturation factor.
local keys = {
  { light = {240, 200, 200}, sat = 0.9 },
  { light = {255, 255, 250}, sat = 1.0 },
  { light = {255, 160, 130}, sat = 1.0 },
  { light = {90, 105, 160},  sat = 0.6 },
}

local function lerp(a, b, u) return a + (b - a) * u end

local function remap(t)
  local seg = math.min(math.floor(t / 100), 2)
  local u = (t - seg * 100) / 100
  local a, b = keys[seg + 1], keys[seg + 2]
  local sat = lerp(a.sat, b.sat, u)
  local glow = math.max(0, math.min(1, (t - 150) / 50))
  local pal = {}
  for i, c in ipairs(base) do
    local m = {}
    for j = 1, 3 do
      m[j] = c[j] * lerp(a.light[j], b.light[j], u) / 255   -- tint by the light
    end
    local grey = 0.299 * m[1] + 0.587 * m[2] + 0.114 * m[3]
    for j = 1, 3 do
      m[j] = grey + (m[j] - grey) * sat                      -- then mute
      if i == WINDOW then m[j] = lerp(m[j], GLOW[j], glow) end
    end
    pal[i] = m
  end
  return pal
end

local map = { {3, 3, 3, 3}, {2, 5, 2, 2}, {1, 1, 4, 4} }  -- your level
local time, Z = 200, 8

function love.update(dt)
  time = math.min(300, time + 10 * dt)   -- dusk to night in 10 seconds
end

function love.draw()
  local pal = remap(time)
  for y, row in ipairs(map) do
    for x, index in ipairs(row) do
      local c = pal[index]
      love.graphics.setColor(c[1] / 255, c[2] / 255, c[3] / 255)
      love.graphics.rectangle("fill", (x - 1) * Z, (y - 1) * Z, Z, Z)
    end
  end
end

The sky is not remapped: its colours are keyframes of their own, picked by hand, because the sky is the light. The demo uses the same four keys on 13 land colours. A real game would draw tiles rather than rectangles and do the index lookup in a shader, which the later lesson on palette swapping covers.

Pitfalls

  • A night too dark to play. Tinting by a dim light compresses every value into the bottom of the scale. In the demo, with the window off, the land's values span about 140 steps at midday and about 60 at night. Check the character in greyscale against the night palette, and if needed lift the light colour or brighten the character more than the scenery.
  • Saturated night. Multiplying by a strong blue makes colours more saturated, not less, so night looks like neon. That is why the night key mutes to 60%. Usually night is darker and greyer.
  • Mud between keys. Blending straight from orange dusk to blue night passes through dull grey-browns. That is fine for a short transition; if a mid-point matters, give it its own keyframe.
  • Trusting the multiply blindly. The tint is a starting point. It can give two neighbouring colours the same value, or turn water an odd grey. Fix those entries by hand in the palette table.

In real games

Engines have the multiply step built in. In LÖVE, love.graphics.setColor tints everything drawn after it, so setting it to the light colour before drawing the ground layer gives the multiply for free. Godot's CanvasModulate node tints a whole 2D scene the same way, and Unity's SpriteRenderer.color tints one sprite.

Hardware with indexed colour, such as the NES, stored the screen as palette indexes, so changing a few palette entries recoloured everything that used them at once without touching the tiles.

Animation studios plan mood the same way across a whole film: a colour script is a strip of small paintings, one per scene, that sets the palette and temperature of each moment before production. Pixar's colour scripts are published in book form (see below).

Your turn (15 minutes)

Take one small scene or tile set from your project. Duplicate it and put a multiply layer over the copy, filled with an orange-pink such as 255, 160, 130. Make a second copy with 90, 105, 160, then lower its saturation by about 40%.

Pick colours from the results to build a dusk and a night palette with as many entries as the original. Repaint each sky by hand, keep lamps at their own colour, and check the night version in greyscale. You are done when the three versions sit side by side at 1x and each reads as a clear time of day.

Takeaways

  • Warm palettes usually feel sunny, cosy or late in the day; cool palettes usually feel calm, cold or night-time. Mood comes from the whole palette.
  • The sky tells the player what light is shining, so when it changes, every colour must move towards the same light colour.
  • Tint by multiplying with the light colour, then mute: night is usually darker and less saturated.
  • Store art as palette indexes and build one palette per time of day, then fix the odd entries by hand.
  • Keep light sources outside the tint. One warm spot in a cool scene is where the eye goes.
