Choosing a light source: one sun for every sprite

Why every sprite in a game should be shaded as if lit from the same direction, how to pick that direction, and what breaks when sprites disagree.

Each sprite in your scene looks fine on its own: a shaded ball, a barrel, a rock. Put them side by side and something feels off. Nobody can say what, but the scene looks like a collage of stickers. The usual cause is that each sprite was shaded on a different day with a different idea of where the light comes from.

Pick the light once, before you shade anything

A light source in pixel art is an imagined lamp or sun that you shade every sprite against. Nothing in the game has to compute it. It is a decision you write down and then obey: "light comes from the top left, a little in front of the screen".

Shading tells the eye which way each part of a surface faces. A pixel on the side that faces the light gets a light step of the ramp; a pixel facing away gets a dark step. So the light direction is not decoration. It is the rule that turns a flat shape into a ball or a cylinder.

Two numbers describe the light

  • Direction. Where the light comes from on the screen, as an angle: 0° is from the right, 90° from straight above, 135° from the top left.
  • Height. How far the light sits in front of the screen, towards the viewer. A low light rakes across the sprite and leaves a wide shadow side. A high light sits almost behind the viewer's head, lights nearly everything and leaves only a thin shadow at the far edge.

Why the usual choice is the top left

Most pixel art guides suggest a light from above and slightly to one side, and the top left is the most common pick. It is a convention, not a law. There are good reasons for "above". People tend to read a shaded shape as if the light comes from overhead, which is why a shaded circle can flip between a bump and a dent when you turn it upside down. Light from directly below therefore looks strange or spooky, which is useful for a campfire scene and wrong for everything else.

Why left rather than right is less certain. Some perception studies report a slight preference for light from the above-left, and the habit is also old in user interfaces, where buttons often have a light top-left edge and a dark bottom-right edge. Either way, the side matters far less than sticking to it. A game lit from the top right looks fine as long as every sprite agrees.

Directions in between usually work best. Straight above gives symmetric shading, which can look flat. Straight from the side splits each form into a light half and a dark half, which reads well for drama and poorly for a whole game.

What breaks when sprites disagree

  • The scene stops being one place. The eye compares shadows across the whole screen. When they disagree, sprites read as pasted on.
  • Forms flip. A rock lit from below can read as a hollow, and a button shaded the wrong way looks pressed in.
  • Tiles show seams. Two tiles shaded with different lights do not join, because the shading jumps at the border.

Rule of thumb

Write the light down before drawing, for example "from the top left at 135°, height 45°", and keep a small shaded ball in your palette file as a reference. Every new sprite is checked against that ball.

The code

Hand-drawn sprites carry the light baked into their pixels, so in a game the light is mostly a note in your style guide. When you generate shaded art in code, or check your own shading against a reference, the light becomes two numbers that every shading function reads. This LÖVE program shades the demo's 16 by 16 ball with the demo's numbers and draws it at 10x.

-- One light for the whole game. Angle: 0 = from the right, 90 = from above.
local LIGHT_ANGLE = 135   -- from the top left
local LIGHT_HEIGHT = 45   -- degrees in front of the screen

local function lightVector(angle, height)
  local a, e = math.rad(angle), math.rad(height)
  return math.cos(e) * math.cos(a), -math.cos(e) * math.sin(a), math.sin(e)
end                       -- y is negated because screen y points down
local LX, LY, LZ = lightVector(LIGHT_ANGLE, LIGHT_HEIGHT)

local function rgb(r, g, b) return { r / 255, g / 255, b / 255 } end
local RAMP = { rgb(59, 29, 58), rgb(122, 46, 62), rgb(196, 71, 63), rgb(242, 146, 90) }

-- How directly a surface faces the light, turned into a ramp step 1..4.
local function band(d)
  if d < 0.2 then return 1 elseif d < 0.6 then return 2
  elseif d < 0.9 then return 3 else return 4 end
end

local SIZE, RADIUS = 16, 7.7
local ball

function love.load()
  ball = love.graphics.newCanvas(SIZE, SIZE)
  ball:setFilter("nearest", "nearest")
  love.graphics.setCanvas(ball)
  for y = 0, SIZE - 1 do
    for x = 0, SIZE - 1 do
      local nx = (x + 0.5 - SIZE / 2) / RADIUS
      local ny = (y + 0.5 - SIZE / 2) / RADIUS
      local d2 = nx * nx + ny * ny
      if d2 <= 1 then
        local nz = math.sqrt(1 - d2)           -- the ball bulges towards us
        love.graphics.setColor(RAMP[band(nx * LX + ny * LY + nz * LZ)])
        love.graphics.rectangle("fill", x, y, 1, 1)
      end
    end
  end
  love.graphics.setCanvas()
  love.graphics.setColor(1, 1, 1)
end

function love.draw()
  love.graphics.draw(ball, 40, 40, 0, 10, 10)    -- whole-number zoom
end

The highlighted lines are the whole idea. lightVector turns the two numbers into a direction, and each pixel's step is the dot product of that direction with the way the surface faces. If a barrel or a rock generator reads the same LX, LY, LZ, it cannot disagree with the ball. The band limits 0.2, 0.6 and 0.9 are my choice for this demo, not a standard.

Pitfalls

  • Flipping a sprite flips its light. Games often draw a character facing left by mirroring the right-facing sprite, for example with a negative x scale. A light from the top left becomes a light from the top right. On a small moving character this is usually accepted, and a light from straight above avoids it entirely. Large static props are where it shows.
  • Rotating a sprite rotates its light. A shaded rock spun 180° is now lit from below. Shade rotating objects lightly, or keep separate drawings for each angle.
  • Pillow shading. Darkening every edge and lightening the middle is shading without a light source. It gets its own lesson, but the cure starts here: pick a direction first.
  • Each artist with their own sun. On a team, write the light into the style guide and share a reference sprite. Otherwise the art drifts slowly.
  • Story lights versus the main light. A torch or a lava pit can add a local light on top. Treat it as a second, weaker light and keep the main one unchanged, or the scene loses its anchor.

In real games

Look at almost any 2D game with shaded tiles and you can find its light by checking which side of each block or rock is lighter. The convention extends to interfaces: the classic Windows 95 style buttons have a light top and left edge and a dark bottom and right edge, which is the same light from the top left.

When the light must move during play, baked shading is not enough. Engines such as Godot and Unity can light 2D sprites at runtime using a normal map, an extra image that stores the direction each pixel faces, which is the per-pixel version of the dot product in the code above.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Open three sprites from your game or from your own sketches. For each one, draw a small arrow beside it showing where its light seems to come from. If the arrows disagree, pick one direction for the whole game and write it at the top of your palette file.

Then shade a 16 by 16 reference ball with that light and reshade the sprite that disagrees most, moving only light and dark pixels, not the outline. You are done when, at 1x, all three sprites have their lit sides facing the same way.

Takeaways

  • A light source is a decision you make once and apply to every sprite, not a feature of the engine.
  • Describe it with two numbers: the direction on the screen and the height in front of it.
  • Light from above and to one side, usually the top left, is the common convention; consistency matters more than the side.
  • Sprites that disagree make a scene look pasted together, flip bumps into dents and show seams between tiles.
  • Mirroring or rotating a sprite moves its baked light too.
