Cast shadows: grounding a sprite and showing its height

How a small shadow on the floor pins a sprite to the ground, shows how high it jumps, and which of the blob and projected styles to choose.

In a top-down or three-quarter view game, your character jumps. On screen it simply moves up, which looks exactly like walking a few steps north. Props placed on the floor look as if they hover a little above it.

The sprites are fine. What is missing is a few dark pixels on the floor.

Two kinds of shadow

The last lessons shaded the object itself. The dark side of a ball is its form shadow: the part of the surface that faces away from the light. A cast shadow is different. It is the dark shape the object throws onto something else, usually the ground, by blocking the light.

A cast shadow answers two questions the sprite cannot answer alone:

  • Where is the floor under this thing? The shadow lies on the ground, so it marks the spot the object stands on, or hovers over.
  • How high is it? When the object touches the ground, the shadow touches the object. When it leaves the ground, a gap opens between them, and the size of that gap is the height.

That second point is the key decision. The shadow stays on the floor. It may slide sideways or shrink, but it never rises with the sprite.

The blob shadow

The simplest cast shadow is a blob shadow: a small flat oval under the feet, roughly as wide as the sprite's footprint. It does not try to match the sprite's shape. For a 16 pixel character it is often three rows tall: a full-width middle row and two shorter rows above and below. The sprite covers the top row, leaving a dark rim at the feet.

A blob is cheap and works with any light, because it ignores the light. That is also its weakness: next to art with a strong sun from the top left, a plain oval can look flat.

The projected shadow

A projected shadow is the sprite's own silhouette, flattened and slanted away from the light so that it lies on the floor. In the demo the light comes from the top left, so the silhouette is squashed to about a third of its height and leans to the right. It starts at the feet, which is what makes it look attached.

It matches your light source and looks richer, but it is larger and can fall across other sprites in confusing ways. Most games pick one style for every object, for the same reason they keep one light direction.

Changing the shadow with height

Many games make the shadow smaller and lighter as the object rises. In real sunlight a shadow does not shrink with height; its edge gets softer. Games shrink it anyway because the size change is easy to read at a glance. Treat it as a convention you can choose.

In pixel art "lighter" usually does not mean transparent. A half-transparent black adds colours that are not in your palette. A common alternative is to switch the shadow to a checker dither, so half its pixels show the floor through.

Rule of thumb

Draw the shadow in a dark colour from the floor's own ramp, cooled a little, and draw it after the floor but before any sprite. On the ground it touches the feet. In the air it stays on the floor row and, usually, shrinks or dithers.

The code

This LÖVE program draws the blob shadow from the demo with the same numbers: ground row 31, a 10 pixel wide blob that loses 2 pixels every 4 pixels of height, and a checker dither from height 7. It expects a 12 by 14 sprite called sprout.png with its feet on the bottom row. Press up and down to change the height.

local ZOOM, GY, W0 = 8, 31, 10       -- ground row and blob width, in art pixels
local FLOOR_SHADOW = { 122 / 255, 106 / 255, 120 / 255 }   -- dark step of the floor ramp
local player = { x = 6, height = 0 }  -- height in whole art pixels
local sprite

function love.load()
  love.graphics.setDefaultFilter("nearest", "nearest")
  sprite = love.graphics.newImage("sprout.png")
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "up" then player.height = math.min(player.height + 1, 14) end
  if key == "down" then player.height = math.max(player.height - 1, 0) end
end

local function blobShadow(cx, h)
  local w = math.max(W0 - 2 * math.floor(h / 4), 4)   -- shrink with height
  local fade = h >= 7                                  -- high up: checker dither
  love.graphics.setColor(FLOOR_SHADOW)
  for row = -1, 1 do
    local rw = (row == 0) and w or w - 4
    for x = cx - rw / 2, cx + rw / 2 - 1 do
      local y = GY + row                               -- always the floor, never the sprite
      if not fade or (x + y) % 2 == 0 then
        love.graphics.rectangle("fill", x * ZOOM, y * ZOOM, ZOOM, ZOOM)
      end
    end
  end
end

function love.draw()
  -- draw the floor tiles first, then every shadow, then every sprite
  blobShadow(player.x + 6, player.height)
  love.graphics.setColor(1, 1, 1)
  love.graphics.draw(sprite, player.x * ZOOM, (GY - 13 - player.height) * ZOOM, 0, ZOOM, ZOOM)
end

The two highlighted lines are the whole idea. The shadow's row comes from GY alone. Only the sprite's row subtracts player.height. Keep height as its own number, separate from y: y is where the character stands on the floor, height is how far above it. The widths 10 and 4 and the threshold 7 are my choices for this sprite, not standard values.

Pitfalls

  • Moving the shadow with the sprite. If the shadow is part of the sprite image, it rises when the character jumps and the height cue is gone. Keep the shadow a separate drawing tied to the floor position.
  • Semi-transparent black. It creates colours outside your palette, and overlapping shadows get darker still. One solid palette colour simply merges.
  • A gap at height 0. If the blob sits a row below the feet, the sprite looks like it floats even when standing. On the ground, the shadow should touch or tuck under the feet.
  • Mixing styles and directions. A projected shadow leaning right next to one leaning left, or blobs next to projections, makes the scene look like it has two suns.
  • Shadows drawn over other sprites. Draw all shadows in one pass after the floor and before any sprite.

In real games

Belt-scrolling beat 'em ups, where fighters walk up and down a floor with depth, usually draw a shadow under each fighter, because without one a jump and a step towards the back look the same.

Super Mario 64 draws a round shadow under Mario that stays on the ground while he is in the air, which helps players judge where they will land.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Take a character and one prop from your own project, or draw a 12 by 14 test sprite.

  1. Pick the shadow colour: one dark step of your floor ramp, shifted slightly towards blue or purple.
  2. Draw a blob three rows tall, about as wide as the footprint, tucked under the feet.
  3. In your game, store a separate height for the character and draw the shadow at the floor position only.
  4. Jump, and decide whether the shadow should shrink, dither, both or neither.

You are done when a friend can tell at 1x, from a single screenshot, whether the character is jumping or standing.

Takeaways

  • A cast shadow lies on the ground and shows where the floor is under the object.
  • On the ground the shadow touches the feet. In the air it stays on the floor, and the gap shows the height.
  • A blob shadow is cheap and ignores the light. A projected shadow is the flattened silhouette leaning away from the light.
  • Shrinking or dithering the shadow with height is a readable convention, not physics.
  • Use one solid colour from the floor's ramp, drawn before every sprite.
