Rough materials: texture from clusters, not noise

How small shaped clusters of pixels suggest wood, stone and cloth on a shaded form, and why scattered single pixels turn into noise instead.

You shade a block nicely, then try to make it look like stone by sprinkling darker pixels over it. At 1x it looks like dirt, and adding more pixels makes it worse.

The problem is the size of each mark. Texture in pixel art is built from small clusters with a shape, and the shape tells the eye "wood", "stone" or "cloth".

A mark needs a shape before it can mean anything

Texture is the small change in value across a surface that tells you what the surface is made of. At sprite sizes you cannot draw every wood fibre or grain of sand.

A lone pixel is a texture pixel with no other texture pixel directly above, below, left or right of it. It has no shape, so the eye reads a scatter of them as dust or static, whatever the material was supposed to be.

A cluster is a group of two to about six texture pixels that touch. A cluster has a length, a direction and a silhouette, so it can describe something. A thin streak along the surface says "grain". A chunky lump lit on one side says "pebble". A row of identical dashes repeating at a fixed spacing says "woven".

Three decisions for every rough surface

1. Keep the marks close in value. Texture marks usually use the ramp step right next to the face's own colour: one step darker for pits and grain, one step lighter for bumps catching the light. The face keeps its overall value, so the form you shaded still reads first. Jump two or three steps and the texture becomes louder than the light, and a cube stops looking like a cube.

2. Give the clusters the material's shape. This is where the material is decided:

  • Wood: long, thin streaks that all run the same way, along the board. Some step one row up or down, so the grain flows. A cut end shows short curved arcs of growth rings.
  • Stone: irregular chunks of two to four pixels, lit on the side that faces your light and dark on the other side. Each pebble is a tiny form under the same light as the block.
  • Cloth: a regular pattern, because cloth is woven. The demo uses a twill, the diagonal weave you see on denim, drawn as two-pixel dashes on a 2:1 slope. Natural materials are irregular; made materials repeat.

3. Leave plain space. Texture is most convincing when it is sparse. In the demo, roughly 10 to 30 percent of a face covered by marks reads well; that range is my choice for this cube, not a standard. Past it, clusters merge into a busy pattern or average out into a new flat tone. Artists also usually thin texture out in the brightest highlight and deepest shadow and keep most of it in the mid-tones.

Rule of thumb

Before you place a texture pixel, ask what shape it is part of. If you cannot name the shape, it is noise. Delete it or join it to a neighbour.

The code

You will usually draw texture by hand, but code makes the rules exact. This LÖVE program builds the front face of the wood cube from the demo: a 20 by 20 face on ramp step 3, grain streaks of 3 to 7 pixels, a quarter of them light, 40 percent of them stepping one row up or down, and 0.15 streaks per pixel at full density. It outlines lone pixels in red. Press up and down to change the density.

local ZOOM, W, H, BASE, SEED = 12, 20, 20, 3, 1017
local RAMP = { {43,25,19}, {91,51,34}, {140,86,49}, {187,130,72}, {227,187,124} }  -- wood, dark to light
local density = 0.3
local marks = {}   -- one value per pixel: -1 darker, 0 plain, 1 lighter

local function add(u, v, sign)
  if u >= 1 and u <= W and v >= 1 and v <= H then marks[(v - 1) * W + u] = sign end
end

local function mark(u, v)
  if u < 1 or u > W or v < 1 or v > H then return 0 end
  return marks[(v - 1) * W + u]
end

local function makeGrain()
  for i = 1, W * H do marks[i] = 0 end
  local rng = love.math.newRandomGenerator(SEED)
  local n = math.floor(density * W * H * 0.15 + 0.5)
  for i = 1, n do
    local u, v = rng:random(1, W), rng:random(1, H)
    local len = rng:random(3, 7)                  -- a streak, never a dot
    local sign = (rng:random() < 0.25) and 1 or -1
    for j = 0, len - 1 do add(u + j, v, sign) end
    if rng:random() < 0.4 then                    -- the grain steps one row and flows on
      local dv = (rng:random() < 0.5) and -1 or 1
      for j = 0, rng:random(2, 3) - 1 do add(u + len + j, v + dv, sign) end
    end
  end
end

local function isLone(u, v)
  return mark(u, v) ~= 0 and mark(u - 1, v) == 0 and mark(u + 1, v) == 0
     and mark(u, v - 1) == 0 and mark(u, v + 1) == 0
end

function love.load() makeGrain() end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "up" then density = math.min(density + 0.1, 1) end
  if key == "down" then density = math.max(density - 0.1, 0) end
  makeGrain()
end

function love.draw()
  local lone = 0
  for v = 1, H do
    for u = 1, W do
      local c = RAMP[BASE + mark(u, v)]               -- one step from the face colour
      love.graphics.setColor(c[1] / 255, c[2] / 255, c[3] / 255)
      love.graphics.rectangle("fill", (u - 1) * ZOOM, (v - 1) * ZOOM, ZOOM, ZOOM)
      if isLone(u, v) then
        lone = lone + 1
        love.graphics.setColor(1, 0, 0)
        love.graphics.rectangle("line", (u - 1) * ZOOM + 1, (v - 1) * ZOOM + 1, ZOOM - 2, ZOOM - 2)
      end
    end
  end
  love.graphics.setColor(1, 1, 1)
  love.graphics.print(string.format("density %.1f, lone pixels %d", density, lone), 4, H * ZOOM + 8)
end

The two highlighted lines carry the lesson: every mark is at least three pixels long, and every mark is exactly one ramp step away from the face. The streaks land in different places from the demo because LÖVE's random generator gives a different sequence, but the counts and shapes follow the same rules. The only lone pixels left are streak ends cut off by the edge of the face.

Pitfalls

  • Sprinkling single pixels. The most common mistake. Join each dot to a neighbour or delete it.
  • Using a noise filter. The "add noise" filter in a general image editor produces exactly that scatter of lone pixels, often in colours outside your palette. If you start from generated noise, expect to clean up most of it by hand.
  • Too much contrast. Texture two or more steps away from the face colour fights the light. The form flattens.
  • The same density everywhere. An even spread looks like wallpaper. Let marks gather near edges, cracks and the mid-tones, and leave calm areas between them.
  • Texture that ignores the form. On a curved surface, grain and weave should bend with the curve. Straight lines across a round barrel make it look flat.
  • Texture on things that move a lot. Fine marks flicker between animation frames, so characters usually get less texture than walls and floors.

In real games

3D engines face the same problem from the other side. When a detailed texture is drawn so small that its detail is finer than a screen pixel, it shimmers and sparkles as the camera moves. Mipmapping fixes this by switching to smaller, pre-blurred copies of the texture at a distance, which throws away detail too fine to read. A pixel artist removing lone pixels is making the same trade by hand: detail that cannot hold a shape is better left out.

For pixel art itself, zoom in on a stone wall or a crate in a 2D game you like and count the marks. You will usually find a handful of shaped clusters on plain areas, not an even scatter.

Your turn (15 minutes)

  1. Draw a 16 by 16 block, three faces visible like the demo cube, with a five-step ramp. Shade it with flat faces and no texture.
  2. Make three copies: wood, stone and cloth.
  3. Texture each copy using only the ramp step next to each face's colour. Use streaks for wood, lit chunks for stone and a repeating dash pattern for cloth.
  4. Hunt down every lone pixel and either join it to a neighbour or delete it.
  5. Look at all three at 1x.

You are done when someone else can name all three materials at 1x without being told, and the cube still reads as a cube in all three.

Takeaways

  • Texture in pixel art is built from small clusters, not single pixels. A lone pixel has no shape and reads as noise.
  • The shape of the clusters names the material: streaks for wood, lit chunks for stone, a regular repeat for cloth.
  • Texture marks usually stay one ramp step from the face colour, so the light and form still read first.
  • Leave plain space. Past a certain density marks merge into a busy pattern or a new flat tone.
