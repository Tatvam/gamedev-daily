Value comes first: check your sprite in greyscale

Why a character must stand apart from the background in greyscale before colour helps, and how to fix it by moving value instead of hue.

You give your hero a bright red cloak and put them in a green meadow. Red and green are opposites, so the hero should pop. Then you watch someone play on a phone in daylight, and they keep losing track of where the character is. The colours were different. Their lightness was not.

Your eye reads light and dark before colour

In this lesson, value means how light or dark a colour looks, from black to white. The V in HSV from the last lesson is a rough version of it, and the last lesson ended on its flaw: pure yellow and pure blue have the same V but look nothing alike in lightness.

A better estimate is luma, a weighted sum of red, green and blue. Green counts most because our eyes are most sensitive to it, and blue counts least:

luma = 0.299 * r + 0.587 * g + 0.114 * b   -- 0 is black, 255 is white

Turn every pixel into a grey of its own luma and you have a greyscale view. It shows the picture as the eye first sorts it: shapes are found from edges, and the strongest edges are where light meets dark. Where two neighbouring areas have nearly the same value, the edge between them is weak, however different their hues are.

That is why value usually decides readability. Hue still helps on a large, sharp screen with good colour vision, but it gets weaker on small screens, in sunlight, at 1x scale, and for the many players with some form of colour vision deficiency, most often a difficulty telling red from green. Value survives all of those.

The greyscale test

Put a greyscale layer over your scene, or switch your editor's preview to greyscale, and look at it at real size. Can you find the character in under a second? If not, the colours were doing work that value should have done.

Here is the meadow from the hook. The cloak and the grass are a red and a green, far apart on the hue wheel. In grey they are 94 and 99, nearly the same.

Fix it with value, not hue

When the test fails, the tempting fix is a louder hue, such as a more saturated red. That changes the colour picture and leaves the grey picture as it was. The reliable fix is to move value, and to move it in groups:

  • Find the background's range. List the greys of everything the character stands in front of. In the meadow that is hills, two bush greens and two grass greens: 66 to 99.
  • Put the character's main mass outside it. Usually the character goes lighter than a darker, calmer background, or darker than a light one. Either works. What breaks is landing in the middle of the band, because then some part of the background will match.
  • Move whole groups. Shift all the character's fill colours together and all the background colours together, so the shading inside each group keeps its order.

Rule of thumb

The biggest area of the character should sit outside the grey range of whatever it stands in front of. In the demo below, under 20 grey steps apart reads as merged and 45 or more as clear. Those numbers are a rough guide of mine, not a standard; trust your eye at 1x.

This is a convention, not a law. A game may hide a secret door or a camouflaged enemy on purpose by matching values. The point is that matching should be a choice.

Try it

Switch on Greyscale first and look at the 1x previews on the right: in the Before scene the cloak, hills and grass are nearly one grey. Now drag Character value up and Background value down until the readout says the cloak separates clearly, or press After. Hue and saturation never change; only value moves.

Then try the opposite fix: character down, background up. It works too, as long as the cloak's marker leaves the shaded band on the value bar.

The code

Two tools carry this into a game. A greyscale shader on a debug key lets you play any level in grey and spot where the hero vanishes. A valueGap function does the demo's check with the demo's numbers, so you can test a palette before drawing with it.

-- Luma: a common estimate of how light a colour looks, 0 to 255.
local function luma(r, g, b)
  return 0.299 * r + 0.587 * g + 0.114 * b
end

-- How far a colour sits outside the greys of a background. 0 = inside, so it merges.
local function valueGap(colour, background)
  local lo, hi = 255, 0
  for i = 1, #background do
    local y = luma(unpack(background[i]))
    lo = math.min(lo, y)
    hi = math.max(hi, y)
  end
  local y = luma(unpack(colour))
  if y > hi then return y - hi end
  if y < lo then return lo - y end
  return 0
end

-- The demo's cloak and its hills, bushes and grass, before and after.
local CLOAK_BEFORE = { 189, 53, 53 }
local MEADOW_BEFORE = { {69, 115, 99}, {35, 87, 39}, {71, 112, 50}, {78, 120, 48}, {48, 97, 39} }
local CLOAK_AFTER = { 240, 67, 67 }
local MEADOW_AFTER = { {46, 77, 66}, {19, 48, 22}, {47, 74, 33}, {53, 82, 33}, {29, 59, 23} }
print(math.floor(valueGap(CLOAK_BEFORE, MEADOW_BEFORE) + 0.5))   -- 0
print(math.floor(valueGap(CLOAK_AFTER, MEADOW_AFTER) + 0.5))     -- 51

-- Press G to play the whole game in greyscale.
local greyShader
local greyscale = false

function love.load()
  greyShader = love.graphics.newShader([[
    vec4 effect(vec4 colour, Image tex, vec2 uv, vec2 screen) {
      vec4 p = Texel(tex, uv) * colour;
      float y = dot(p.rgb, vec3(0.299, 0.587, 0.114));
      return vec4(y, y, y, p.a);
    }
  ]])
end

local function drawGame()
  -- your own drawing code: level, sprites, interface
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "g" then greyscale = not greyscale end
end

function love.draw()
  if greyscale then love.graphics.setShader(greyShader) end
  drawGame()
  love.graphics.setShader()    -- back to normal for anything drawn after
end

The shader works on colour values from 0 to 1, so its luma is also 0 to 1; the weights are the same. unpack spreads a table into separate arguments, so luma(unpack({189, 53, 53})) is luma(189, 53, 53).

Pitfalls

  • Trusting the outline. A dark outline separates the hero when zoomed in. At 1x it is a thin line, and in front of a dark bush it disappears. The demo's Before scene is readable when zoomed mainly because of its outline; look at the 1x preview instead.
  • Saturated reds and blues are darker than they look. Pure red has a luma of about 76 and pure blue about 29. A strong red cloak can never be a light grey; past a point, lightening it means moving it towards pink or orange. Plan the background to be darker instead, as the demo does.
  • The wrong greyscale. Some desaturate commands average the three channels or use HSL lightness, and HSL lightness gives pure yellow and pure blue the same grey. Use a luminosity or luma based greyscale, or the check lies to you.
  • Checking one spot only. Value contrast depends on what is behind the character. A hero who reads well on grass may vanish in a cave. Test against every kind of background the game has.
  • A background that uses every value. If the background runs from near black to near white, no grey is left over for the character. Backgrounds usually keep a narrower, calmer range than the things the player must follow. In the meadow the band is only about 33 grey steps wide, which leaves room both above and below it.

In real games

The original Game Boy screen shows four shades of a single greenish tint, so every game made for it had to separate characters from backgrounds by value alone.

Checking a piece in greyscale is a long-standing habit among painters and illustrators. Digital artists often keep a greyscale adjustment layer at the top of the file to toggle while they work.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Take a screenshot of your game, or paste one of your sprites onto a background you plan to use. Make a greyscale copy with a luminosity based greyscale and look at it at 1x. Write down the grey of the character's biggest colour and the lightest and darkest greys of what it stands in front of.

If the character's grey falls inside that range, move the value of the character's colours as a group, or the background's, without touching hue. You are done when someone who has not seen the scene can point to the character in the greyscale copy within a second.

Takeaways

  • Value is how light or dark a colour looks; luma is a simple way to measure it from RGB.
  • Edges are seen where values differ. Different hues at the same value make a weak edge.
  • Test in greyscale at 1x. If the character disappears, colour was hiding the problem.
  • Put the character's main mass outside the grey range of what is behind it, by moving whole groups of colours.
  • Matching values should be a deliberate choice, for example to hide something.
