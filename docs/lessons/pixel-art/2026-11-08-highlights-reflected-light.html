Highlights and reflected light: making a form look solid

How a small highlight, a dark core shadow and a faint band of reflected light turn a two-tone ball into a solid object, and how to keep each one in its place.

You shaded a ball with one light direction: a lit side and a shadow side, split cleanly. It is correct, and it still looks like a flat disc cut in half. The shading says where the light comes from, but not that the thing is round.

Three small additions fix that, and together they repaint fewer than a third of the pixels. This lesson adds them one at a time.

Two tones are a start, not a form

So far a pixel has been either lit or in shadow. The line where one turns into the other is called the terminator. A sphere with only a lit side and a shadow side has a terminator but nothing else, so the eye reads two flat shapes glued together.

Real light on a round object makes a few more zones, each telling the eye something different. Three of them matter at sprite size.

The highlight

A highlight is the small spot where the surface reflects the light source straight towards the viewer. It is the lightest colour on the object. On a sphere lit from the top left, it sits inside the lit side, a little way in from the edge, not on the edge itself. That is because it appears where the surface faces halfway between the light and your eye.

Keep it small. On a sprite of 16 to 32 pixels a highlight is usually a cluster of 2 to 10 pixels; the demo uses 10. A big one turns into a second lit zone and makes the ball look flatter, not rounder. Its size also hints at the material, which is a later lesson.

The core shadow

The core shadow is the darkest band on the shadow side, just past the terminator. It is dark because this part of the surface faces away from the light and also faces away from anything that could bounce light back onto it. Further into the shadow, the surface starts to face the ground and the room again and picks up a little light.

Without it, lit and shadow meet like two pieces of paper. With it, the shadow seems to wrap round the back.

Reflected light

Reflected light, also called bounce light, is light that hits a nearby surface, usually the ground, and bounces back into the shadow side. It shows up as a faint band along the edge of the shadow side that faces that surface. On a ball resting on the ground it runs along the bottom and the lower right.

Reflected light is easy to make too bright. It is still in shadow, so it is usually kept darker than the darkest pixel of the lit side. If it climbs to the lit colour, the eye reads a second light source behind the ball, and the form flattens again.

Rule of thumb

Order the values: core shadow, shadow, reflected light, lit side, highlight. Usually each zone stays darker than everything to its right. Usually that means a five-colour ramp for a sphere, and reflected light is the step that most often gets cut when colours are short.

The code

In a game you would bake this into the sprite once, but each layer reads well as a single test. This LÖVE program draws the demo's ball at 8x with the same light, the same thresholds and the same five colours. Press 1, 2 and 3 to toggle the layers.

local CX, CY, R, ZOOM = 12, 12, 10.5, 8
local function unit(x, y, z)
  local m = math.sqrt(x * x + y * y + z * z)
  return x / m, y / m, z / m
end
local a, e = math.rad(135), math.rad(5)                -- top left, low
local LX, LY, LZ = math.cos(e) * math.cos(a), -math.cos(e) * math.sin(a), math.sin(e)
local HX, HY, HZ = unit(LX, LY, LZ + 1)               -- halfway to the eye
local BX, BY, BZ = unit(0.5, 1, 0)                    -- bounce, up from the ground

local function rgb(r, g, b) return { r / 255, g / 255, b / 255 } end
local COLOURS = { core = rgb(42, 24, 56), shadow = rgb(90, 42, 74),
  reflect = rgb(138, 74, 82), lit = rgb(200, 96, 60), highlight = rgb(246, 216, 160) }
local layers = { core = true, reflect = true, highlight = true }

local function zone(x, y)
  local u, v = (x + 0.5 - CX) / R, (y + 0.5 - CY) / R
  if u * u + v * v > 1 then return nil end
  local w = math.sqrt(1 - u * u - v * v)                -- facing towards the viewer
  local d = u * LX + v * LY + w * LZ                    -- facing towards the light
  local name = (d >= 0) and "lit" or "shadow"
  if layers.core and d < 0 and d > -0.22 then name = "core" end
  if layers.reflect and d <= -0.22 and u * BX + v * BY + w * BZ > 0.3 and w < 0.6 then
    name = "reflect"
  end
  if layers.highlight and u * HX + v * HY + w * HZ > 0.98 then name = "highlight" end
  return name
end

function love.keypressed(key)
  if key == "1" then layers.core = not layers.core end
  if key == "2" then layers.reflect = not layers.reflect end
  if key == "3" then layers.highlight = not layers.highlight end
end

function love.draw()
  for y = 0, 23 do
    for x = 0, 23 do
      local name = zone(x, y)
      if name then
        love.graphics.setColor(COLOURS[name])
        love.graphics.rectangle("fill", 40 + x * ZOOM, 40 + y * ZOOM, ZOOM, ZOOM)
      end
    end
  end
end

The highlighted lines are the three layers. The core shadow is a narrow range of facing just below zero. Reflected light needs a pixel past the core shadow, facing the ground, near the rim (w < 0.6). The highlight tests the halfway direction, not the light, so it lands inside the lit side. The thresholds 0.22, 0.3, 0.6 and 0.98 are my choices for a 24 pixel ball, not standard values.

Pitfalls

  • Reflected light as bright as the lit side. The ball looks lit from two sides, or like a ring. Keep the band at least one ramp step below the lit colour.
  • Reflected light everywhere around the shadow edge. It comes from a surface, so it faces that surface. A band that follows the whole outline is pillow shading by another name.
  • A highlight that is too big or touches the edge. A large spot becomes a flat second lit zone. On the very edge it looks like a chip of paint, because the edge faces sideways, not at your eye.
  • A highlight on every part. Usually only the key round shapes get one, such as an eye or a gem. Highlights everywhere make a sprite glitter.
  • A core shadow as wide as the shadow side. Then it is just a darker shadow. Keep it two or three pixels wide, and stagger its edge on large sprites so it does not band.
  • Mixing up reflected light and rim light. A rim light is a second, real light behind the object, and it may be bright. Reflected light has no source of its own and stays dim.

In real games

The zones come from traditional drawing, where shading a lit sphere and naming these parts is a classic early exercise. Pixel artists carry them over, scaled down to a handful of colours.

Look at the round objects in sprite-based games: orbs, bombs, coins, gems and eyes. A small highlight on the side facing the light is very common. Reflected light is rarer, because it costs an extra colour, and with tight palettes it is often the first zone to go.

Engines can also light sprites at runtime: Godot's CanvasTexture and Unity's 2D lights use a normal map, and Godot's also takes a specular map for highlights.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Take a round object of 16 to 32 pixels that you shaded with only a lit and a shadow side, such as a fruit or a head. Work on a copy.

  1. Add a core shadow: one darker colour, two or three pixels wide, just past the terminator.
  2. Add reflected light: a lighter shadow colour along the edge that faces the ground. Check in greyscale that it is still darker than every lit pixel.
  3. Add a highlight of 2 to 6 pixels inside the lit side, nearer the light.

You are done when the new version looks rounder than the old one at 1x, and nothing in the shadow is as light as the lit side.

Takeaways

  • Lit and shadow alone show the light direction. The extra zones show that the form is round.
  • The highlight is small and sits inside the lit side, where the surface faces halfway between the light and the eye.
  • The core shadow is a narrow dark band just past the terminator, and it makes the shadow wrap round.
  • Reflected light is a faint band on the shadow edge that faces the ground, and it always stays darker than the lit side.
  • Keep the value order: core shadow, shadow, reflected light, lit side, highlight.
