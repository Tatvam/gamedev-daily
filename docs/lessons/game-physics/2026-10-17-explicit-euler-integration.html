Explicit Euler integration

How the simplest integrator turns velocity into motion, and why moving in straight lines every step slowly pumps energy into orbits, springs and jumps.

You put a moon in orbit around a planet. The numbers are right: the speed is exactly what a circular orbit needs. For a few seconds it looks perfect. Then the circle opens into a spiral, the moon drifts outward, and a minute later it has left the screen.

Nothing pushed it. The extra energy came from the two lines of code that move it.

The simplest step there is

A game cannot follow motion continuously. It jumps forward one step at a time, and something has to decide where a body ends up after each step. That rule is called an integrator.

The simplest integrator is explicit Euler, named after the mathematician Leonhard Euler. Its whole idea is: look at the body at the start of the step, and assume nothing changes until the step is over.

position = position + velocity * dt

In words: move in a straight line, at the velocity you had at the start, for the whole step.

velocity = velocity + acceleration * dt

In words: change the velocity by the acceleration you felt at the start, for the whole step.

"Explicit" means the new state is computed directly from the old one, with no looking ahead.

Look at the order

The earlier lessons updated velocity first. Here position moves first, with the old velocity. Swapping two lines sounds harmless; this lesson shows what it costs, and the next one shows why the other order works better.

Why it gains energy

In a circular orbit the moon's velocity always points along the circle, at a right angle to the line from the planet. Explicit Euler moves the moon along that velocity in a straight line. A straight line that touches a circle leaves it on both sides, so every step lands slightly outside the orbit.

The velocity has the same problem. Gravity pulls toward the planet, at a right angle to the velocity. Adding something at a right angle to an arrow always makes the arrow longer, so every step also makes the moon slightly faster.

One step by hand

These are the demo's numbers. The planet's pull is GM / r², where GM is a single number for the planet's strength, here 1,000,000 px³/s². The moon starts 100 px to the right of the planet, moving at 100 px/s at a right angle. At that distance the pull is 1,000,000 / 100² = 100 px/s², exactly what a circle of radius 100 at that speed needs.

Position moved by velocity × dt = 100 × 1/60 = 1.667 px straight down the screen. Velocity changed by acceleration × dt = −100 × 1/60 = −1.667 px/s toward the planet. Both lengths grew by the same 0.0139.

To measure the damage we use the orbit's energy, per unit of mass: E = ½ · speed² − GM / r. In words: one part grows with speed, the other grows (toward zero) with distance from the planet. A perfect orbit keeps E constant. At the start E is 5000 − 10000 = −5000. After one step it is −4997.2. Energy has to reach 0 for the moon to escape completely, so we will measure gains as a percentage of that 5000.

Once the steps are small, halving the step roughly halves the error. That makes explicit Euler a first-order method. But the error never changes sign: the moon always gains, so over enough orbits it always leaves.

The same bug in a jump

Take the jump from the timestep lesson: launch at 600 px/s, gravity 1800 px/s², designed peak 100 px. With explicit Euler at 60 steps per second, each step rises at its starting speed, which is always a little faster than its average speed. The peak comes out at 105 px. In general the overshoot is half of launch speed × dt, here 600 × 1/60 ÷ 2 = 5 px.

Feel beats realism, until it adds up

An extra 5 px on a jump is a tuning detail: you might never notice, and you could design around it. What makes explicit Euler dangerous is anything that repeats: orbits, springs, pendulums, swinging ropes. Each cycle adds a little more energy, and the motion keeps growing until something breaks.

The code

The demo as a LÖVE program, with the same constants. Positions are stored relative to the planet. The highlighted function is the whole integrator.

local GM = 1000000          -- planet strength, px^3/s^2
local STEP = 1 / 60         -- fixed step, seconds
local CX, CY = 400, 300     -- planet position on screen
local moon = { x = 100, y = 0, vx = 0, vy = 100 }   -- relative to the planet
local accumulator = 0

local function gravity(x, y)
  local r = math.sqrt(x * x + y * y)
  local pull = GM / (r * r)              -- px/s^2, toward the planet
  return -x / r * pull, -y / r * pull
end

local function energy(b)
  local r = math.sqrt(b.x * b.x + b.y * b.y)
  return 0.5 * (b.vx * b.vx + b.vy * b.vy) - GM / r
end

local function explicitEuler(b, dt)
  local ax, ay = gravity(b.x, b.y)       -- acceleration at the START of the step
  b.x = b.x + b.vx * dt                  -- move with the OLD velocity
  b.y = b.y + b.vy * dt
  b.vx = b.vx + ax * dt                  -- then change the velocity
  b.vy = b.vy + ay * dt
end

local startEnergy = energy(moon)         -- -5000

function love.update(dt)
  accumulator = accumulator + math.min(dt, 0.25)
  while accumulator >= STEP do
    explicitEuler(moon, STEP)
    accumulator = accumulator - STEP
  end
end

function love.draw()
  love.graphics.circle("fill", CX, CY, 16)
  love.graphics.circle("fill", CX + moon.x, CY + moon.y, 5)
  local gained = (energy(moon) - startEnergy) / -startEnergy * 100
  love.graphics.print(string.format("energy gained: %.1f%%", gained), 10, 10)
end
# Godot 4. A RigidBody2D is integrated by the physics engine, not like this.
# Explicit Euler appears when you move a Node2D yourself and write
# the position line before the velocity line.
extends Node2D

const GM := 1000000.0
var planet := Vector2(400, 300)
var velocity := Vector2(0, 100)

func _physics_process(delta: float) -> void:
    var offset := position - planet
    var accel := -offset.normalized() * GM / offset.length_squared()
    position += velocity * delta   # old velocity
    velocity += accel * delta      # updated second: explicit Euler
// Unity. A Rigidbody2D is integrated by the physics engine, not like this.
// Explicit Euler appears when you move a Transform yourself and write
// the position line before the velocity line.
public float gm = 1000000f;
public Vector2 planet = Vector2.zero;
Vector2 velocity = new Vector2(0f, 100f);

void FixedUpdate()
{
    float dt = Time.fixedDeltaTime;
    Vector2 offset = (Vector2)transform.position - planet;
    Vector2 accel = -offset.normalized * gm / offset.sqrMagnitude;
    transform.position += (Vector3)(velocity * dt);   // old velocity
    velocity += accel * dt;                           // updated second
}

Unity works in metres, so a real scene would use a much smaller gm. In both engines, hand-written movement is integrated by whatever two lines you write, in the order you write them.

Pitfalls

  • Blaming rounding errors. The drift is not floating-point noise. It is a built-in, one-directional error of the method, and switching to double precision does not change it.
  • Trusting a smaller step. Halving the step doubles the cost and only halves the drift. A long-running orbit or spring still gains energy, just more slowly.
  • Hiding the gain with drag. A little damping can cancel the extra energy. The balance depends on the step size, so it breaks when the step changes.
  • Swapping lines by accident. Refactoring a step function can quietly reorder the position and velocity updates. Put a comment on the order, because it changes the behaviour.

In real games

Explicit Euler mostly shows up in hand-written movement code. It is often harmless there: a player who lands every second or two never builds up extra energy, and short-lived particles die before the error matters.

General physics engines avoid it. Box2D, for example, updates velocities first and then moves bodies with the new velocities, which is the method of the next lesson. Unity's 2D physics is built on Box2D.

Your turn (15 minutes)

Take any object in your project that jumps under gravity and print the highest point of each jump. Write the two update lines in the explicit Euler order (position first), run at a fixed step, and record the peak. Then swap them and record it again.

You are done when you can predict both numbers before running: the designed peak is launch² / (2 × gravity), explicit Euler adds launch × dt / 2, and the other order subtracts the same amount.

Takeaways

  • Explicit Euler moves with the velocity and acceleration from the start of the step, as if nothing changed during it.
  • On curved paths, each straight step lands outside the curve and each velocity update makes the body faster, so energy grows every step.
  • The error is first-order: halving the step halves the drift, but never removes it.
  • It is tolerable for short, one-off motion, and dangerous for anything that repeats, such as orbits, springs and pendulums.
